1  A real dataset, at last

For nine weeks every number in this project has come out of a formula you derived or a simulator you wrote. Both are yours, which is their strength and their weakness: they agree with each other because you built them from the same beliefs. This week you meet something that was not built from your beliefs at all.

PBMC 3k, in one paragraph

PBMC stands for peripheral blood mononuclear cells: the round-nucleus white blood cells you get from a tube of blood after the red cells and the granulocytes have been separated out. Mostly T cells and monocytes, with B cells, natural killer cells and dendritic cells in smaller numbers. The dataset is about 2,700 such cells from a single healthy donor, sequenced by 10x Genomics on their v1 chemistry and released in 2016 as a demonstration dataset.

It is, without much competition, the standard teaching dataset of the field. The Seurat tutorial uses it. The scanpy tutorial uses it. Thousands of methods papers benchmark on it. That ubiquity is exactly why it is the right choice here: when you present a result on PBMC 3k, nobody in the room has to take your word for what the data is.

The number that matters for this project is the library size: the total number of molecules counted per cell. The median in PBMC 3k is a couple of thousand UMIs. Two thousand molecules, spread across roughly 20,000 genes. Everything Weeks 1 through 5 said about small samples applies here literally.

Two thousand molecules per cell is not a lot, and it is worth converting that into the language of Week 1 before going further. If a well-expressed marker gene is $p = 0.002$ of the transcriptome, its expected count in one cell is $\lambda = 2000 \times 0.002 = 4$, with standard deviation $\sqrt4 = 2$: a relative noise of 50%. That is the raw material of every classification decision you will make this week and next.

"UMI count" and "molecule captured" — close enough, and the caveat

A UMI (unique molecular identifier) is a short random barcode attached to each mRNA molecule before amplification. Because amplification copies the barcode along with the molecule, you can collapse all the reads carrying the same UMI back to one original molecule, which removes most of the distortion PCR would otherwise introduce. Counting UMIs is therefore a much better approximation to "counting molecules" than counting reads is, and for this project's purposes the two phrases are interchangeable: a UMI count is a count of captured molecules.

The caveat, which belongs in your limitations section rather than in the main argument: the correspondence is not exact. Two distinct molecules of the same gene in the same cell can be assigned the same random barcode (a UMI collision), which undercounts; sequencing errors in the barcode can split one molecule into two apparent ones, which overcounts; and PCR is not perfectly corrected by any of this. The standard fixes are saturation corrections and barcode error-correction, and the residual error is generally small at PBMC 3k's counts per gene. cited Say the sentence "I am treating UMI counts as molecule counts, which is standard and approximately right" out loud in Week 15 before anyone asks you.

2  Getting the data, and the one line that matters

The dataset is not in this repository. Thirty-five megabytes of sparse matrix does not belong in git, and the download depends on scanpy, which is a heavy dependency you should not need in order to run pytest. So the project splits the job in two: one script that downloads once, and a cache that everything afterwards reads.

The download, once and only once

pip install -r requirements-optional.txt  (this is what brings in scanpy and anndata; the ordinary requirements.txt deliberately does not)

python experiments/fetch_pbmc3k.py

It downloads about 35 MB, runs the standard clustering pipeline to obtain labels, and writes python/data/pbmc3k_cache.npz containing four arrays: X (cells × genes, int32 raw counts), y (integer labels), genes (names) and types (label names in order). After that, celldetect.realdata.load_pbmc() reads the cache and scanpy is never needed again. The reproducibility of everything downstream does not depend on a scanpy version.

The script prints the cell count, the per-type counts, the gene count after filtering, and the median library size. Copy those four numbers into progress/week10.md the moment you see them. They are the "materials" paragraph of your report and you will not want to re-derive them in Week 14.

The two cell types are CD14+ monocytes and CD4 T cells, chosen deliberately: they are the two largest and cleanest populations in PBMC 3k, and they are separated by well-known markers (LYZ, S100A8, S100A9, CST3 on the monocyte side; IL7R, CD3D, CD3E, LTB on the T-cell side). Choosing an easy pair is not cheating, and you should be ready to say why: this project measures how accuracy scales with depth, not how hard one particular pair of cell types is. A hard pair would pin the accuracy curve near chance at every depth, and a trivially easy pair would pin it at 1. An easy-but-not-trivial pair keeps the curve in the range where it can move, which is where a scaling law can be measured at all.

The single most important line in fetch_pbmc3k.py

The script runs the standard preprocessing — normalize_total(target_sum=1e4), log1p, highly-variable-gene selection, scaling, PCA, neighbours, Leiden — on a copy of the data called proc, and caches the counts from the original:

raw_counts = adata.X.copy()  # keep the RAW counts; we need integers

Why. "Counts per 10,000, then $\log(1+x)$" is what almost every scRNA-seq pipeline does before anything else, and it is the right thing to do if your goal is clustering or visualization. It is fatal here. Normalization divides each cell's counts by that cell's library size, turning integers into real numbers whose variance no longer equals their mean; $\log(1+x)$ then compresses the scale further, and the compression is strongest exactly where the counts are smallest, which is where all the Poisson structure lives. After normalizing and logging, the Fano factor means nothing, $d' = |\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|/\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$ has no derivation behind it, and the negative binomial fit of Week 11 is fitting noise you manufactured.

Normalization destroys the Poisson structure this entire project is about. The preprocessing is run only to obtain cluster labels; the numbers that are analysed are raw integers. If you take one habit from this chapter into any future single-cell work, take this one: know which of your matrices are counts and which are not, and never let them get mixed up.

There is a second reason to keep integers, less philosophical and equally practical: downsample_matrix thins each entry with a binomial draw, and a binomial draw needs an integer $n$. Downsampling normalized data is not a well-defined operation. The Week 11 headline figure would be impossible.

No download yet? Then you are on surrogate data — and you must not forget it

If python/data/pbmc3k_cache.npz does not exist, week10_markers.py does not crash. It prints two loud lines beginning [week10] !!, falls back to surrogate_pbmc() — a synthetic, deliberately messy stand-in — sets a module flag USED_STAND_IN = True, and stamps a large red SURROGATE DATA — not a result across the figure. In Week 14, week14_report_figures.py reads that flag and refuses to copy the figure into the report directory, listing it under "Figures refused".

This exists so you can build and test the whole pipeline before the download finishes. It is not a shortcut. The failure mode it is designed against is a real one and it happens to real students: you run the week on the surrogate, the shapes look right, you screenshot a figure into a slide, and three weeks later nobody remembers where that panel came from. If the stamp is on the figure, the result does not exist. Look at every figure before you commit it, and if you see the stamp, write "surrogate" next to the figure name in your progress log rather than hoping you will remember.

3  The labels are not ground truth

Here is the sentence that has to be in your report, your poster and your talk, and that you should be able to say before anyone asks.

Where the cell-type labels actually come from

Nobody looked at these cells and identified them. There is no independent measurement of what cell type each barcode belongs to — no flow-sorting, no imaging, no second assay. The labels were produced by a computation on the same counts you are analysing: normalize, log, select 2,000 highly variable genes, scale, PCA, build a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph, run Leiden community detection to get clusters, then score each cluster on two panels of known marker genes and name it after whichever panel scores higher.

That is a well-established annotation. It is the same procedure every tutorial uses, the marker panels are textbook, and the two populations it produces are as uncontroversial as anything in this field. It is still not a measurement, and the distinction is not pedantry:

  • The clustering used the counts. So a cell whose counts are unusual can be assigned to the wrong cluster because its counts are unusual — which is precisely the kind of cell your classifier will also get wrong. The errors are correlated, and that inflates measured accuracy.
  • Cells that are genuinely intermediate get forced into one cluster or the other. Real biology has continua; Leiden returns partitions.
  • The resolution parameter (resolution=0.5 here) is a choice. A different value gives a different number of clusters and therefore different labels.
  • fetch_pbmc3k.py discards clusters whose two marker scores are within 0.25 of each other rather than guessing — which is the honest thing to do, and which also means the cells you analyse are the confidently labelled ones. Your dataset is easier than the full dataset, by construction.

Everything this project concludes about real data is conditional on those labels being right. Write that clause into the report as a clause, not a footnote: "conditional on the standard Leiden-plus-marker annotation, the measured accuracy at full depth is ...". The [PROVIDED] code says the same thing in its docstring; the mentor check-in will ask you to say it out loud.

Notice how different this is from the other two lines of evidence. In simulation you know the type of every cell because you chose it. In the bead experiment you know the jar because the tally sheet was pre-randomized and recorded. Only in the real-data arm is the label itself an inference. That asymmetry is exactly the point of having three arms, and Week 12 turns it into a table.

4  Choosing markers

PBMC 3k has around 20,000 genes, of which perhaps 13,000 survive the "detected in at least 10 cells" filter. You are not going to build a classifier on 13,000 features from 2,700 cells, and you should not want to: the project's theory is about a panel of $k$ informative genes, and the whole $\sqrt k$ story of Week 5 presumes you have chosen which $k$.

The ranking rule select_markers uses

Given the count matrix $X$ (cells × genes) and labels $y$ with exactly two classes:

  1. Detection filter. Keep only genes detected (count $> 0$) in at least min_cells cells; the default is 10, matching the filter fetch_pbmc3k.py already applied.
  2. Class means. $m^{(1)}_g$ = mean count of gene $g$ over class-1 cells, $m^{(2)}_g$ likewise.
  3. Score. $$\mathrm{score}_g = \left|\log_2 \frac{m^{(1)}_g + 1}{m^{(2)}_g + 1}\right|.$$
  4. Return the indices of the n_markers highest-scoring genes.

Three design decisions, each of which you should be able to defend: the absolute value (you do not care which type is higher), the pseudocount of 1, and the detection filter.

Why the pseudocount. Without it the score is $|\log_2(m^{(1)}_g/m^{(2)}_g)|$, and a gene that is silent in one class gives $\log_2$ of zero or of infinity. Formally that is $\pm\infty$; numerically it is a warning and a nan; practically it means a gene that appeared once, in one cell, by accident, wins the ranking outright. With 13,000 genes and most of them near-silent, there are hundreds of such genes, and every one of them beats every real marker. The pseudocount of 1 count keeps the ratio finite and, more usefully, damps it where the counts are small: a gene at means $0.05$ and $0.005$ has a raw fold change of 10 ($\log_2 = 3.32$) but a scored value of $|\log_2(1.05/1.005)| = 0.063$, which puts it nowhere.

A pseudocount is a modelling choice, not a numerical patch

It is tempting to describe the $+1$ as "avoiding division by zero". It does that, but that is a side effect. What it really does is change the ranking — it converts fold change, at low counts, into something much closer to an absolute difference, and at high counts leaves it essentially alone. A different pseudocount gives a different marker list. If you had used $+0.1$ you would get a different set of genes and a different accuracy curve.

So say it in the report as a choice with a consequence: "markers were ranked by $|\log_2$ fold change$|$ with a pseudocount of 1 count; the pseudocount suppresses genes whose apparent fold change comes from single stray counts, and changing it changes the panel." One sentence. That is the difference between a method and a magic number.

Why the detection filter. Same disease, second dose of medicine. test_select_markers_respects_min_cells in tests/test_realdata.py builds exactly the pathological case: a gene that is zero in every cell except one, where it is 500. Its class means differ enormously and its pseudocounted score is large; it is also worthless, because it describes one cell. The filter disqualifies it before scoring. The test asserts gene 7 is not selected — read that test, it is four lines and it is the argument in executable form.

5  Selection leakage: this week's actual lesson

You now have a rule that picks the best-looking genes. Here is how to fool yourself with it, and it is so easy that entire published literatures have done it.

Selection leakage (also: selection bias, "peeking") If you use the labels of a set of cells to choose which genes to look at, and then measure accuracy on those same cells, the accuracy you measure is too high. The test cells helped choose the features, so they are no longer a test.

The mechanism is worth spelling out concretely, with the actual numbers of this dataset, because "leakage inflates accuracy" as a slogan is forgettable and the arithmetic is not.

Why chance alone manufactures markers

Take the extreme case: suppose no gene whatsoever differs between the two cell types. Every gene has the same expected count in both. Now split 2,700 cells into two labelled groups and compute, for each of 13,000 genes, the difference between the two group means.

Those 13,000 differences are not zero. They are random, centred on zero, with some spread $\sigma$ set by the counting noise and the group sizes. The largest of 13,000 draws from a distribution centred at zero is a long way from zero — for Gaussian draws, roughly $3.8\sigma$ out. Rank the genes by $|{\rm difference}|$ and the top of the list is populated by genes whose difference is real in this sample and zero in the population.

Now hand those top genes to a classifier and test it on the same cells. The classifier's rule points in the direction the noise happened to point, and the test cells are the cells whose noise pointed that way. It works. It reports accuracy well above chance. Nothing is informative and the measurement says otherwise.

And the effect grows with the size of the panel, because taking more genes means averaging more independent lucky flukes that all happen to lean the same way — the same $\sqrt k$ accumulation as Week 5, running in the wrong direction. ∎

Do not take this on trust. Lab 1 builds precisely that dataset — 1,000 genes, none of them informative, labels assigned by coin flip — and shows you the accuracy climbing.

Lab 1 · Manufacturing a discovery out of pure noise
Every gene here is drawn from the same Poisson distribution in both classes. There is nothing to find. The red curve chooses its markers using all 200 cells (including the 60 test cells) and then reports test accuracy; the teal curve chooses them using the 140 training cells only. Both curves then fit the same classifier on the same training cells and score the same test cells. The only difference between the two curves is which cells were allowed to help pick the genes.

The red curve starts near 0.6 with a single gene and climbs past 0.85 by the time the panel reaches 128 genes. The teal curve sits on 0.5 and stays there, wobbling by a few percent in either direction because 60 test cells is a small test set and a proportion from 60 trials has a standard error of about 0.065. Both curves are describing the same data, in which nothing is going on. Press "new dataset" a few times: the red curve is not a fluke of one seed, it is what the procedure does.

The honest protocol, in three lines
  1. Split the cells into a training set and a test set first, before looking at anything.
  2. Do everything that uses the labels — marker selection, rate estimation, classifier fitting, threshold choice — on the training set alone.
  3. Touch the test set exactly once, at the end, to produce one number.

The rule that catches every case: any step that reads y is part of the model. Marker selection reads y. Therefore marker selection is part of the model, and fitting a model on the test data is obviously wrong. Written that way it is not a rule to memorize, it is a consequence of what "test set" means.

This is why week10_markers.py is built the way it is. It splits once, then runs both protocols side by side:

Note carefully that the classifier is fitted on the training split in both arms. Only the selection changes. That is what makes the gap between the two curves attributable to selection leakage and to nothing else, and it is what turns a warning into a measurement.

The number you report this week

The script prints a table: marker count, leaky accuracy, honest accuracy, and the difference. The inflation column is your result. Not "leakage is bad" — a number, on your data, at each panel size, with the seed recorded.

On real PBMC data with genuinely informative markers the gap will be smaller than in Lab 1, because there real signal to find and the leaky arm does not have to invent everything. That is worth saying precisely rather than apologetically: the size of the inflation measures how much of the apparent performance was manufactured, and on an easy two-class problem with strong markers most of the performance is real. A small gap is a result. A gap you did not measure is a hole in your methods section.

6  Fold change is not $d'$

The second lesson of the week is that the ranking rule you have just been given is not the ranking the theory would choose, and the reason is Week 3.

Fold change asks: by what factor do the two means differ? $d'$ asks: how many noise widths apart are the two means? Those are different questions, and at low counts they give wildly different answers, because the noise width $\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$ shrinks relative to nothing when $\bar\lambda$ shrinks.

Worked arithmetic · a 5-fold change worth almost nothing

Gene A. Mean counts $\lambda_1 = 0.02$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.10$. The fold change is $5\times$; $\log_2 5 = 2.32$, which is enormous by any differential-expression standard.

$$d'_A = \frac{|0.10 - 0.02|}{\sqrt{(0.10+0.02)/2}} = \frac{0.08}{\sqrt{0.06}} = \frac{0.08}{0.2449} = 0.327.$$

Accuracy from that one gene: $\Phi(0.327/2) = \Phi(0.163) = 56.5\%$. Six points above a coin.

Gene B. Mean counts $\lambda_1 = 20$ and $\lambda_2 = 24$. The fold change is $1.2\times$; $\log_2 1.2 = 0.263$, which no differential-expression tool would call interesting.

$$d'_B = \frac{|24-20|}{\sqrt{22}} = \frac{4}{4.690} = 0.853, \qquad \Phi(0.426) = 66.5\%.$$

Gene B is worth two and a half times as much $d'$ as gene A, while having one ninth of the fold change. The reason is entirely in the denominators: gene A's difference of $0.08$ counts is measured against a noise width of $0.24$ counts, while gene B's difference of $4$ counts is measured against a noise width of $4.7$. Ratios are scale-free; detection is not. ∎

Connect this back to Week 3, Section 1, where $d'$ was introduced precisely because "the means differ" is not a statement about whether you can tell two things apart, and to Week 1 exercise 1.3, where two means $1.8$ apart with widths of $3$ were the first time you saw the point.

Now the twist, and it is the kind of detail that makes the difference between reciting a lesson and understanding it. The rule in select_markers is not raw fold change — it is fold change with a pseudocount of 1 count. Score gene A: $|\log_2(1.10/1.02)| = 0.109$. Score gene B: $|\log_2(25/21)| = 0.252$. The pseudocounted rule prefers gene B, which is the right answer.

So the pseudocount does much of the work of fixing the pathology, and it is worth understanding why: adding 1 count to both means makes the ratio behave like an absolute difference whenever the counts are below about 1, which is exactly the regime where fold change was lying. What survives is a subtler disagreement, at the high end:

Gene$m^{(1)}$$m^{(2)}$scored $|\log_2\frac{m_1+1}{m_2+1}|$$d'$
C511.5852.309
D60400.5732.828

Gene C wins on the scored fold change; gene D wins on $d'$. Both are legitimate genes at realistic expression levels, and a top-$k$ list built one way is not the top-$k$ list built the other way. This is why week10_markers.py's second figure exists and why it prints a specific number: the overlap between the top 40 by fold change and the top 40 by $d'$, out of 40. Report that number. It is a one-line, concrete, honest statement of how much the choice of ranking rule matters on your data.

Lab 2 · Two rankings of the same 700 genes
Each dot is a simulated gene, with a random expression level and a random log fold change between the two types. The horizontal axis is the score select_markers computes; the vertical axis is the per-gene $d'$ at the current depth. Orange dots are the top 40 by fold change, blue dots the top 40 by $d'$. Move the depth slider and watch the two lists agree and disagree — and turn the pseudocount off to see what the ranking looks like without it.

Three things to notice, all of which belong in your notebook.

First, with the pseudocount on, the overlap is high at low depth and falls as depth rises — on the default panel it runs from around 38/40 at a depth of 500 to around 23/40 at 10,000. That is not an accident. At low depth the counts are well below the pseudocount, so $\log_2\frac{m_1+1}{m_2+1} \approx (m_1-m_2)/\ln 2$: the score has become an absolute difference, which is the numerator of $d'$, so the two rankings nearly coincide. As depth grows the pseudocount stops mattering, the score reverts to a genuine ratio, and the two rankings separate.

Second, with the pseudocount off the overlap collapses to about 12/40 and stays there at every depth, because a ratio of two rates does not depend on depth at all. Watch the median $d'$ of the fold-change panel in the readout: turning the pseudocount off roughly halves it. The orange dots migrate left and down, into the low-expression corner where large ratios are cheap and $d'$ is small.

Third, the vertical axis moves when you move the depth slider and the horizontal axis (with the pseudocount off) does not. That asymmetry is the difference between the two quantities: $d'$ is a statement about a particular experiment at a particular depth, and fold change is a statement about the cells.

"Why not just rank by $d'$, then?"

Good question, and you should ask it. Two honest answers.

Practical: ranking by $d'$ is what the field does not do. Every differential-expression tool in single-cell biology reports fold changes, and every marker list you will ever read was built from them. Using the field's rule, and then measuring how much it costs you relative to $d'$, is a more interesting result than quietly using a better rule nobody else uses.

Statistical: $d'$ estimated from data has the same leakage problem, only worse. It is a ratio whose denominator is itself estimated, so it is noisier than fold change at low counts and will happily rank a gene top because its estimated pooled variance came out small by luck. Neither rule is safe used on the test set; both are fine used on the training set.

If you want the strongest version of the week's work, do both: run the honest protocol with fold-change selection and with $d'$ selection, and report the two accuracy curves. That is exercise 10.4.

7  Downsampling: the knob for next week

One more function this week, and it is two lines, but it is the experimental knob of the entire real-data arm.

Why downsampling a real dataset is legitimate

downsample_matrix(X, keep_prob, rng) replaces every entry $x$ by a $\mathrm{Binomial}(x, \text{keep\_prob})$ draw: each counted molecule survives independently with probability keep_prob.

By Week 2's thinning theorem, if $x \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ then the thinned count is $\mathrm{Poisson}(\text{keep\_prob}\cdot\lambda)$ exactly. So downsampling a real library by a factor $q$ produces exactly the data a run at $q$ times the depth would have produced — you can sequence more shallowly after the fact, for free, with no new experiment.

Assumptions used. (i) The counts are Poisson to begin with. (ii) Loss is independent per molecule and does not depend on the gene.

Assumption (i) is the one Week 11 is about, and it is false. Note carefully what that does and does not break: thinning a negative binomial is still a legitimate physical operation and still gives you the data a shallower run would have given; what changes is that the resulting distribution is not the Poisson your theory curve assumed. The knob still works. The prediction drawn through it does not.

Week 9 exercise 9.2(c) already licensed this: thinnings compose, and $s_1 s_2 = s_2 s_1$. The PBMC counts have already been thinned once, by the capture process, by an unknown factor. Downsampling further just moves you along the same curve, which is why you can measure the shape of the depth dependence without knowing where on the depth axis you started.

Exercise 10.1★
A cell has a library size of 2,000 UMIs. Gene CD3D makes up a fraction $p = 0.0015$ of a T cell's transcriptome and $p = 0.0001$ of a monocyte's. (a) Give both expected counts. (b) Give both standard deviations. (c) Compute $d'$ for this one gene at this depth, and the accuracy $\Phi(d'/2)$. (d) Compute the $\log_2$ fold change with and without the $+1$ pseudocount, and comment.
Show solution

(a) $\lambda_{\rm T} = 2000 \times 0.0015 = 3.0$; $\lambda_{\rm M} = 2000 \times 0.0001 = 0.2$.

(b) $\sqrt{3.0} = 1.73$ and $\sqrt{0.2} = 0.447$.

(c) $\bar\lambda = 1.6$, so $d' = |3.0 - 0.2|/\sqrt{1.6} = 2.8/1.265 = 2.214$, and the accuracy is $\Phi(1.107) = 86.6\%$.

(d) Raw: $\log_2(3.0/0.2) = \log_2 15 = 3.91$. Pseudocounted: $\log_2(4.0/1.2) = \log_2 3.333 = 1.74$. The pseudocount roughly halves the score, and it would halve it much more severely if both counts were smaller — which is the point.

86.6% from one gene, in one cell, at PBMC depth. This is why the monocyte/T-cell pair was chosen: the signal is real and large, so the accuracy curve you measure next week has room to move without being pinned at either end. Keep this number; you will compare your measured single-marker accuracy against it.

Exercise 10.2★
A collaborator sends you a matrix and says "it's the PBMC data, already cleaned up for you". You open it and the first row reads 0.00, 1.386, 0.00, 2.079, 0.693, .... (a) What was done to it? (b) Name three specific things in this project you can no longer do with it. (c) What do you ask for?
Show solution

(a) It has been normalized and log-transformed. The giveaway is that the entries are not integers, and that the non-zero values are suspicious: $0.693 = \log 2$, $1.386 = \log 3$, $2.079 = \log 8$. So these are $\log(1+x)$ of small integers, possibly after a per-cell rescaling that happened to be near 1 for this cell.

(b) Three, and each for a stated reason:

  • Compute a Fano factor. Variance-over-mean is only 1-for-Poisson on counts. On logged data the number has no reference value to be compared against, so "1.8" would mean nothing.
  • Downsample. rng.binomial(x, q) needs an integer $x$. There is no meaningful way to thin $1.386$ molecules.
  • Use $d' = |\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|/\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$. That denominator is the Poisson standard deviation, and it is only the standard deviation because variance equals mean, which is a fact about counts.

(c) Ask for the raw count matrix, integers, before any normalization, plus the barcode and gene names so you can line it up with the labels. If they only have the processed object, ask whether the raw counts are still in adata.raw or adata.layers['counts'], which is where scanpy usually keeps them.

This exercise is not hypothetical. Receiving normalized data when you needed counts is one of the most common practical failures in single-cell analysis, and it is silent — everything still runs, and the answers are just wrong. Learning to check "are these integers?" on any matrix you are handed takes two seconds and will save you a week at some point in your life.

Exercise 10.3★★
In Lab 1 no gene is informative, yet the leaky curve rises steadily with the size of the panel. Explain, in terms of Week 5's quadrature argument, why it rises rather than staying flat. Then predict what happens to the leaky curve as you increase the number of cells while keeping the number of genes fixed, and say why.
Show solution

Why it rises. Each selected gene carries a small amount of spurious "signal": a mean difference that exists in this sample only. Because the gene was selected for having that difference in the full dataset (test cells included), the difference is present in the test cells too, with the same sign as the weight the classifier learned. So each selected gene contributes a small positive $d'$ on the test set. These contributions are approximately independent across genes, so they add in quadrature exactly as Week 5's theorem says: $d'_{\rm total} \approx \sqrt{k}\, d'_{\rm spurious}$. Accuracy $\Phi(d'/2)$ therefore climbs with $\sqrt k$. The $\sqrt k$ law is not broken here — it is being obediently obeyed by a signal that does not exist.

More cells. The leaky curve falls back towards 0.5. The spurious per-gene difference has magnitude of order $\sigma \propto 1/\sqrt{N}$, so $d'_{\rm spurious}$ shrinks as $1/\sqrt N$ — and, more importantly, the fraction of the selected difference that is shared with the test cells falls, because the test cells become a smaller and smaller influence on which genes get chosen. With 2,700 cells the inflation is smaller than with 200; with 100,000 cells it would be negligible. Leakage is a small-sample disease, and single-cell datasets of a few thousand cells are firmly in the regime where it bites.

The second half of this answer is what stops the lesson from becoming superstition. Leakage is not a moral failing that is always catastrophic; it is a bias with a size, and the size depends on the number of cells, the number of candidate genes, and how many you select. Measuring it — which is what the week's experiment does — is better than fearing it.

Exercise 10.5★★
A reviewer says: "Your labels came from clustering the same counts you then classify. Isn't your accuracy therefore meaningless?" Write the answer you would give, in about 150 words. It must concede what is true, state precisely what the bias does to your number, and explain why the project's main claim survives.
Show solution

"You are right that the labels are annotations, not measurements, and that they were derived from these counts. That does bias the accuracy upwards: a cell whose counts are atypical is likely to have been clustered wrongly and also to be classified wrongly, so the two kinds of error are correlated and cancel. The absolute accuracy I report is therefore an optimistic estimate of accuracy against true cell identity.

But the project's claim is not about the level of the accuracy. It is about how accuracy scales with sequencing depth, and I measure that by downsampling the same cells with the same fixed labels. The labelling bias is a constant offset across the whole depth sweep, so it shifts the curve without changing its exponent. That is why the headline result is a slope in $d'$ space, and why the simulation and bead arms — where the truth is known by construction — exist to check the same exponent."

Practise this out loud. It is the single most likely hostile question at a poster session, and the structure of the answer (concede, quantify the direction of the bias, then explain why the claim is robust to it) is the structure of every good answer to a methodological objection. Note that the last sentence is what buys you the right to make the concession cheerfully.

Exercise 10.6★★★
Estimate the size of the leakage inflation from first principles. Suppose $G$ candidate genes, none informative, $N$ cells split into $N_{\rm tr}$ training and $N_{\rm te}$ test, and you select the top $k$ by absolute mean difference computed on all $N$ cells. Argue that the spurious per-gene $d'$ on the test set is of order $\sqrt{N_{\rm te}/N}$ times the typical selected difference in units of noise, and hence that the inflation grows like $\sqrt{k}$ and shrinks as the test fraction shrinks. Check the prediction in Lab 1.
Show solution

Setup. For one gene with no true difference, let $\Delta_{\rm all}$, $\Delta_{\rm tr}$, $\Delta_{\rm te}$ be the between-class mean differences computed on all, training and test cells. Each is a difference of averages, so each is approximately normal with mean 0, and — crucially — $\Delta_{\rm all}$ is a weighted average of the other two, $\Delta_{\rm all} \approx f_{\rm tr}\Delta_{\rm tr} + f_{\rm te}\Delta_{\rm te}$ with $f = N/N_{\rm total}$ the split fractions.

The correlation is the whole effect. Selecting on $\Delta_{\rm all}$ selects genes with large $|\Delta_{\rm all}|$, and because $\Delta_{\rm te}$ is one of the two ingredients of $\Delta_{\rm all}$, the selected genes have $\Delta_{\rm te}$ shifted in the same direction. Standard properties of the bivariate normal give $\mathrm{corr}(\Delta_{\rm all}, \Delta_{\rm te}) = \sqrt{f_{\rm te}}$ (the variance of $\Delta_{\rm te}$ scales as $1/N_{\rm te}$, and its weight in $\Delta_{\rm all}$ is $f_{\rm te}$; the two combine to $\sqrt{f_{\rm te}}$). So if selection pushes $\Delta_{\rm all}$ out to $c$ noise widths, the conditional mean of $\Delta_{\rm te}$ is about $c\sqrt{f_{\rm te}}$ noise widths.

Accumulation. The classifier's weight for each selected gene has the sign of $\Delta_{\rm tr}$, which is also positively correlated with $\Delta_{\rm all}$. Each selected gene therefore contributes a small positive spurious $d'$, call it $\delta$, and by Week 5's quadrature the panel gives $d'_{\rm total} = \sqrt{k}\,\delta$. So the apparent accuracy is $\Phi(\sqrt k\,\delta/2)$: rising in $k$, and rising fastest at small $k$ where $\Phi$ is steepest.

Two testable consequences. (i) The inflation grows like $\sqrt k$ until $\Phi$ saturates. (ii) A smaller test fraction $f_{\rm te}$ gives a smaller $\delta$ and hence less inflation, because the test cells had less influence on the selection. Both are visible in Lab 1: the red curve is roughly $\Phi(\sqrt k\,\delta/2)$ with $\delta \approx 0.4$, and shrinking the test set (try it in the code) flattens it.

The pleasing part of this argument is which theorem it uses. Selection leakage is not a new phenomenon requiring new mathematics; it is the $\sqrt k$ quadrature law of Week 5 applied to a signal that is an artefact of selection. Being able to see your own theorems working against you is a good sign that you own them.
