The Separation Index d′

1  One observation, two hypotheses

Everything so far has been description: counts are Poisson, the noise has a law, thinning does not break it. This week we start deciding.

Here is the situation, stripped to nothing. A cell arrives. You measure a single marker gene and get a single number, say $x = 14$. The cell is either type 1, in which case that count came from $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_1)$, or type 2, in which case it came from $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_2)$. You must say which. You will sometimes be wrong. The question of this project is: how often, and what does it cost to do better?

Definition · the two-hypothesis detection problem

You observe one realization $x$ of a random variable whose distribution is one of two known candidates:

$$H_1: X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_1), \qquad H_2: X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_2).$$

A decision rule is a function that maps each possible $x$ to a verdict. Its quality is measured by how often the verdict is wrong, averaged over both hypotheses (we take them equally likely throughout; unequal priors are exercise 4.5).

Notice what is not in this setup: no training data, no model fitting, no hyperparameters. The two distributions are given. Everything that follows is a statement about the information in the observation itself, and no classifier — none, not one you invent, not one Google invents — can beat it.

The framing is not new, and it is worth knowing whose it is.

Where this comes from · radar, 1954  cited

A radar receiver in 1950 faced exactly our problem. A blip appears on the scope. Either there is an aircraft out there and the blip is signal plus noise, or there is no aircraft and the blip is noise alone. The operator must call it. Call too eagerly and you scramble fighters at thunderstorms; call too cautiously and you miss the bomber.

Peterson, Birdsall and Fox, in The theory of signal detectability (IRE Transactions on Information Theory, 1954), made the decisive move: they separated the information in the signal from the criterion the operator adopts. A nervous operator and a calm operator, given the same receiver, trade false alarms against misses differently — but the receiver's quality is a single number that does not depend on which operator is sitting at it. That number is what we are about to define. Their paper also introduced the ROC curve, which is Week 4.

The same mathematics was picked up by psychophysics (Green & Swets, 1966) to measure whether a listener can hear a tone in noise, and it is from that literature that the notation $d'$, read "d-prime", comes. So when you write $d'$ on a poster you are using a radar engineer's idea with a psychologist's notation to describe a sequencer. That is not a coincidence; it is what happens when a problem is really about sampling.

Why this reframing is the point of the project

The standard way to talk about cell-type classification is in the language of machine learning: pick a classifier, tune it, report accuracy on held-out data. That framing invites the question "which classifier is best?", and the honest answer for our problem turns out to be "it does not matter much" (Week 8 measures exactly how much).

The detection framing invites a different and better question: how much information about cell type is in a library of $D$ molecules at all? That question has an answer that no amount of engineering can improve, it depends on quantities a biologist controls (depth, choice of markers), and it tells you when to stop optimizing software and start buying reads.

2  The separation index

How far apart are two distributions? "The means differ by 4.5" is not an answer, because 4.5 is enormous if the distributions are each a tenth of a unit wide and invisible if they are each thirty units wide. The only meaningful statement is a comparison of the gap to the noise.

Definition · the separation index $d'$ For two Poisson counts with rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, write the pooled mean $\bar\lambda = \dfrac{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}{2}$. The separation index is $$\boxed{\;d' \;=\; \frac{|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda}}\;}$$

It is dimensionless: counts on top, counts on the bottom (a standard deviation is in counts). It is zero when the two types are identical and grows without bound as they separate. Read it as "how many noise-widths apart the two cell types are".

In code: d_prime(lam1, lam2) in celldetect/detection.py, and CD.dPrime(l1, l2) in the labs on this page.

The numerator needs no defence: it is the signal, the distance between where the two types put their counts. The denominator is where the whole project lives, so let us be slow about it.

Why the denominator is $\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$, and why that is not arbitrary

We need to divide the gap by "the width of the noise" in order to get a dimensionless comparison. The natural width is a standard deviation. For a general pair of distributions you would have to go and measure that standard deviation, and it would be a second, independent parameter.

For Poisson counts you do not, because:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = \lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{sd}(X) = \sqrt{\lambda}.$$

The noise scale is already determined by the mean. There is no second parameter to measure, estimate, or fit. That is the line — this line, right here — that gives $d'$ its square root, and every square root downstream of it. It was proved twice in Week 2: once as the limit $Dp(1-p) \to \lambda$ and once by direct summation over the Poisson pmf.

If Poisson variance were not the mean — if it were $\lambda + \varphi\lambda^2$, as real data will insist in Week 11 — then this denominator would be wrong, and every consequence in this chapter would be wrong with it. Mark the line. You will come back to it.

A number to hold on to

Take the marker gene the Week 3 experiment uses: $p_1 = 0.0040$ and $p_2 = 0.0055$ (a 1.375-fold difference — modest, and entirely typical of a real marker). At depth $D = 3000$:

$$\lambda_1 = 12, \quad \lambda_2 = 16.5, \quad \bar\lambda = 14.25,$$ $$d' = \frac{|12 - 16.5|}{\sqrt{14.25}} = \frac{4.5}{3.775} = 1.19.$$

The two cell types are 1.19 noise-widths apart on this gene. Week 4 will convert that into an accuracy of $\Phi(d'/2) = \Phi(0.596) = 72.4\%$ — which is well above chance, and nowhere near good enough to publish a cell-type call on.

3  Lab: what $d'$ actually looks like

Before deriving anything, build the intuition. Move the sliders and watch the shaded region: that is the mass the two distributions share, and it is the part of the problem no decision rule can fix.

Lab 1 · Two cell types, one gene, and the overlap you cannot argue with
Blue: the count distribution for type 1 at rate $\lambda_1 = D p_1$. Teal: type 2. The dark shaded region is $\min\big(P_1(k), P_2(k)\big)$ at every count — the probability mass the two types genuinely share. Raise the depth and watch it drain away. Note that $p_1$ and $p_2$ never change: the biology is fixed, only the amount of data moves.

Three observations worth writing down.

First, at low depth the two histograms sit almost on top of each other. This is not a failure of any algorithm — it is a statement that the data does not contain the answer. No classifier can recover information that was never sampled.

Second, as you raise the depth both distributions get wider in absolute terms (the standard deviation $\sqrt{\lambda}$ grows) and yet they separate. The gap grows like $D$ and the width like $\sqrt{D}$, so the gap wins. That race is the entire content of the next section.

Third, the shaded overlap area is not decoration. The error probability of the best possible decision rule is exactly half of it, a fact you will prove in Week 4 and implement as bayes_error_exact. Right now, just notice that the overlap is what $d'$ is trying to summarize in a single number.

4  The depth law

Now the derivation this week is named for. It is four lines long and it is the first genuinely new result in the project.

Theorem · $d'$ grows as the square root of depth  proved Let two cell types express a gene at per-molecule fractions $p_1$ and $p_2$, and let both be sequenced to the same depth $D$, so that $\lambda_i = D p_i$. Then $$d'(D) \;=\; \sqrt{D}\;\cdot\;\frac{|p_1 - p_2|}{\sqrt{\bar p}}, \qquad \bar p = \frac{p_1 + p_2}{2}.$$ The second factor depends only on the biology. So $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$: the separation between two cell types grows as the square root of the sequencing depth.
Proof

Substitute $\lambda_1 = Dp_1$ and $\lambda_2 = Dp_2$ into the definition and watch the two powers of $D$ fail to cancel.

Numerator. The gap between the expected counts is linear in depth:

$$|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2| = |Dp_1 - Dp_2| = D\,|p_1 - p_2|.$$

Denominator. The pooled mean is also linear in depth,

$$\bar\lambda = \frac{Dp_1 + Dp_2}{2} = D\,\bar p,$$

but the noise scale is its square root — and it is a square root because Poisson variance equals mean (section 2). This is the load-bearing step:

$$\sqrt{\bar\lambda} = \sqrt{D\,\bar p} = \sqrt{D}\,\sqrt{\bar p}.$$

Divide.

$$d' = \frac{D\,|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{D}\,\sqrt{\bar p}} = \frac{D}{\sqrt{D}}\cdot\frac{|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{\bar p}} = \sqrt{D}\;\frac{|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{\bar p}}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

The whole theorem is $D/\sqrt{D} = \sqrt{D}$. Signal grows linearly, noise grows as a square root, and the ratio therefore grows as a square root.

Assumptions used. Three, and you must be able to recite them:

  1. The counts are Poisson — specifically, that $\mathrm{Var} = \lambda$, which is what made the denominator $\sqrt{D\bar p}$ rather than something with its own depth dependence. This is the one that fails in Week 11.
  2. Both cell types are sequenced at the same depth $D$. The single symbol $D$ appears in both $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$. If the depths differ, the algebra does not go through (exercise 3.5), and worse, $d'$ starts reporting a difference in library size as though it were biology.
  3. The pooled variance $\bar\lambda$ is the right noise scale. This is a modelling choice, not a theorem, and section 6 defends it honestly.

Note also what is not assumed: nothing about the classifier, nothing about training data, nothing about how many cells you have. This is a statement about one cell.

The 4× rule, with real money attached

Because $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$, doubling $d'$ requires quadrupling the depth. Turn that into the sequencing budget, using the marker gene from section 2 ($p_1 = 0.0040$, $p_2 = 0.0055$) and Week 4's conversion $\text{accuracy} = \Phi(d'/2)$:

Depth $D$ (molecules per cell)$\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$$d'$Accuracy from this one geneCost
3,00012.0, 16.51.1972.4%$1\times$

Say it like this: going from 72% to 88% costs four times the sequencing. Going from 88% to 99% costs four times again — sixteen times the original run. On a 10x experiment where the original run costs a few thousand dollars, that last step is a five-figure decision made by a square root.

This is the claim the entire rest of the project exists to test, three independent ways. It is also the claim that Week 11 will show has a ceiling.

5  Lab: measure the exponent

A proportionality claim is a claim about a slope. Taking logs of $d' = c\sqrt{D}$ gives

$$\log_{10} d' \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\log_{10} D \;+\; \log_{10} c,$$

a straight line of slope exactly $1/2$. So the way to test "$d' \propto \sqrt{D}$" is not to look at a curve and nod; it is to plot $\log_{10} d'$ against $\log_{10} D$, fit a line, and report the number. If the slope comes out $0.50$, the law holds. If it comes out $0.31$, you have found something.

Lab 2 · The slope is the claim
At each depth, this lab draws cells of both types, measures $\hat d' = |\bar x_1 - \bar x_2| / \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(s_1^2 + s_2^2)}$ from the samples — no formula used, only data — and plots $\log_{10}\hat d'$ against $\log_{10} D$. Blue points: measurement. Orange: a reference line of slope exactly $1/2$. Gray dashed: the least-squares fit through the measurements. Raise the cells-per-point slider and watch the fitted slope settle onto $0.5$.
Why the axes are labelled "log₁₀ d′", not "d′"

The plotting library on this site has no logarithmic axes, so these labs plot the logarithm and say so on the axis. Your matplotlib figure in experiments/week03_dprime_depth.py uses ax.loglog instead, which does the same thing with prettier tick labels. Either is fine; silently plotting $d'$ against $D$ on linear axes and asserting a square root from the shape of the curve is not. On linear axes, $\sqrt{D}$, $D^{0.4}$ and $\log D$ all look like "bends over to the right".

And at small numbers of cells the fitted slope will not be $0.500$. The lowest depths are the worst offenders: at $D = 100$ the expected counts are $0.4$ and $0.55$, so most cells return a zero and the measured $\hat d'$ is dominated by sampling error. That is a property of your estimate, not of the law — and knowing the difference is what Week 13 is for.

6  Why pool the variances?

Time for the uncomfortable question. The two distributions have different variances: $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$. So which one belongs in the denominator of $d'$?

There is no theorem that answers this. It is a choice, and a chapter that pretended otherwise would be lying to you. Here are the candidates, evaluated on a deliberately extreme case, $\lambda_1 = 4$ against $\lambda_2 = 16$:

DenominatorValue$d'$Implied accuracy $\Phi(d'/2)$
$\sqrt{\lambda_1}$ (the quieter type)2.006.0099.87%
$\sqrt{\lambda_2}$ (the noisier type)4.003.0093.32%
$\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$ (pooled) — our choice3.163.7997.11%
the exact answer, by summing the pmfs (Week 4)97.83%

Three arguments for pooling, in increasing order of honesty.

It is symmetric. $\bar\lambda = (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/2$ is unchanged if you swap the labels of the two cell types, so $d'(\lambda_1,\lambda_2) = d'(\lambda_2,\lambda_1)$ by construction. Choosing $\sqrt{\lambda_1}$ would mean that "how well can I tell T cells from monocytes" and "how well can I tell monocytes from T cells" had different answers, which is absurd. The test test_d_prime_is_symmetric_and_non_negative pins this down in code.

It is the pooled variance, not an arbitrary average. The standard two-sample pooled variance is the average of the two variances, $\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$. For Poisson counts $\sigma_i^2 = \lambda_i$, so the pooled variance is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2) = \bar\lambda$ and the pooled standard deviation is $\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$. Our denominator is therefore the textbook choice, not an invention — and it is exactly what experiments/week03_dprime_depth.py computes from data as np.sqrt(0.5 * (x1.var(ddof=1) + x2.var(ddof=1))). Theory and measurement use the same formula, which is what makes the comparison meaningful.

It is the least wrong. In the table above, pooling lands closest to the exact answer, and it errs on the conservative side (97.11% predicted against 97.83% true) rather than the flattering side. But note the size of the disagreement: with rates as lopsided as 4 against 16, the three choices differ by six percentage points of accuracy. $d'$ is a summary, and summaries lose information.

What this means for how you report results

$d'$ is a one-parameter description of a two-parameter situation, and it is therefore exact only when the two distributions have the same width. Ours never do exactly, and the approximation degrades as $\lambda_1/\lambda_2$ moves away from 1.

The honest protocol, which this project follows throughout: use $d'$ as the theoretical scaling quantity — it is what makes $\sqrt{D}$ and $\sqrt{k}$ visible — and check it against bayes_error_exact, which sums the actual pmfs and makes no Gaussian or equal-variance assumption, whenever the numbers matter. Week 4 builds that check. In the Week 14 limitations section, "the pooled-variance $d'$ is a same-width approximation" is one of the entries.

7  What $d'$ is not

Two misreadings will cost you a month if you do not stamp them out now.

$d'$ is not an accuracy

$d'$ is unbounded: it runs from 0 to infinity. An accuracy runs from 0.5 to 1. They are not the same quantity and they do not even have the same shape — as $d'$ marches from 4 to 8 to 16, the accuracy crawls from 97.7% to 100.0% to 100.0%.

That saturation is precisely why this project does all of its comparisons in $d'$ space. Two methods that both report "99.4% accurate" may differ by a factor of two in $d'$, and the accuracy number hides it. Week 4 supplies the conversion $\text{accuracy} = \Phi(d'/2)$ in one direction and d_prime_from_auc in the other, so that a measured performance can be turned back into a $d'$ and put on the same axis as a prediction. Until then, resist the urge to translate.

$d'$ is not the fold change

This is the one that catches biologists, and it is worth an example with numbers.

A 5-fold marker that is worse than a 1.4-fold marker

Two candidate marker genes, both at depth $D = 3000$:

Gene$p_1$$p_2$Fold change$\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$$d'$
A (rare, dramatic)$1\times10^{-5}$$5\times10^{-5}$5.0×0.03, 0.150.40
B (common, modest)0.00400.00551.375×12.0, 16.51.19

Gene A has nearly four times the fold change and one third of the separation. The reason is visible in the counts: gene A produces a count of zero in 97% of type-1 cells and 86% of type-2 cells. A five-fold difference between two numbers that are both almost always zero is not information you can act on for a single cell.

The algebra behind it: with $\lambda_2 = f\lambda_1$ for fold change $f$, $$d' = \frac{\lambda_1|f - 1|}{\sqrt{\lambda_1(1+f)/2}} = \sqrt{\lambda_1}\cdot\frac{|f-1|}{\sqrt{(1+f)/2}}.$$

The fold change $f$ sets one factor; the absolute expression level $\lambda_1$ sets the other, and it enters as a square root that no amount of fold change can substitute for.

Differential-expression pipelines rank genes by fold change (usually with a significance filter attached). That ranking answers the question "for which genes can I establish, across many cells, that the population means differ?" It does not answer "which gene helps me classify this cell?" — and those two questions have different winners. Week 10 makes the comparison on real PBMC 3k data, ranks the same gene list both ways, and finds that they disagree; that disagreement is one of the project's results.

The mistake, in the form you will actually make it You will find a gene with a beautiful fold change in a paper's marker table, put it in your classifier, and get nothing. Then you will suspect your code. The code will be fine. Before debugging anything, compute $\lambda_1 = Dp_1$ and $\lambda_2 = Dp_2$ at your actual depth and look at whether both are below 1. If they are, the gene carries almost no per-cell information no matter how large the ratio between them is, and $d'$ told you so in one line.

Stars mark difficulty: ★ routine · ★★ requires an idea · ★★★ challenge. Write full sentences; every claim needs a reason. Solutions are provided — attempt first, then compare.

Exercise 3.1★
Compute $d'$ by hand for $\lambda_1 = 100$ and $\lambda_2 = 121$. Then compute it for $\lambda_1 = 10$ and $\lambda_2 = 12.1$ (the same ratio, one tenth the depth) and state the relationship between the two answers. Does it match the theorem?
Show solution

First pair: $\bar\lambda = 110.5$, so $d' = 21/\sqrt{110.5} = 21/10.512 = 1.998$.

Second pair: $\bar\lambda = 11.05$, so $d' = 2.1/\sqrt{11.05} = 2.1/3.324 = 0.6317$.

The ratio is $1.998/0.6317 = 3.162 = \sqrt{10}$. Ten times the depth gives $\sqrt{10}$ times the $d'$, exactly as $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$ requires.

The first pair is test_d_prime_known_value in tests/test_detection.py. Having done it on paper, you now know that a failing test means your code is wrong, not the test — which is the only frame of mind in which tests are useful.

Exercise 3.2★
A marker gene has $p_1 = 0.0040$, $p_2 = 0.0055$. (a) Write $d'$ as an explicit function of $D$. (b) What depth is needed for $d' = 2$? (c) Your funding lets you triple the depth from 3,000 to 9,000. By what factor does $d'$ improve, and how many more percentage points is that on the 4× rule's ladder?
Show solution

(a) $\bar p = 0.00475$, $|p_1-p_2| = 0.0015$, so $$\frac{|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{\bar p}} = \frac{0.0015}{0.068920} = 0.021764, \qquad d'(D) = 0.021764\sqrt{D}.$$

Sanity check at $D = 3000$: $0.021764 \times 54.77 = 1.192$. ✓

(b) Set $0.021764\sqrt{D} = 2$, so $\sqrt{D} = 91.90$ and $D = 8{,}445$ molecules per cell.

(c) Tripling the depth multiplies $d'$ by $\sqrt{3} = 1.732$, giving $d' = 2.065$ and (using Week 4's $\Phi(d'/2)$) an accuracy of $84.9\%$ against $72.4\%$ — a gain of 12.5 points. Contrast that with the full 4× step, which buys $15.9$ points. The last quarter of the quadrupling buys only a fifth of the improvement.

Part (b) is the shape of every experimental-design question in the project: you are given a target separation and asked what it costs. Week 6 does exactly this for the bead experiment, and Week 14's methods section will contain a sentence of this form.

Exercise 3.3★★
Both cell types are subjected to the same thinning: each molecule survives with probability $q$. Using Week 2's thinning theorem, show that $d'$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{q}$. Then answer a practical question: a protocol with 32% capture efficiency, run at depth 4,000, gives what $d'$ on the gene of exercise 3.2 — and what depth would a perfect protocol need to match it?
Show solution

The claim. After thinning, type $i$'s count is $\mathrm{Poisson}(q\lambda_i)$ — exactly Poisson, by the thinning theorem, so $d'$ is still the right formula to apply. Then

$$d'_{\text{thinned}} = \frac{|q\lambda_1 - q\lambda_2|}{\sqrt{(q\lambda_1+q\lambda_2)/2}} = \frac{q\,|\lambda_1-\lambda_2|}{\sqrt{q}\,\sqrt{\bar\lambda}} = \sqrt{q}\;d'. \qquad \blacksquare$$

It is the same $D/\sqrt{D} = \sqrt{D}$ manoeuvre with $q$ in place of $D$, which is exactly what you should expect: thinning by $q$ is reducing the depth to $qD$.

The numbers. Effective depth $= 0.32 \times 4000 = 1280$, so $d' = 0.021764\sqrt{1280} = 0.7787$. A perfect protocol ($q = 1$) would need depth $1280$ to match it — which is the same statement, said twice.

This exercise is why Week 9 can claim "dropout is just less depth" as a theorem rather than an analogy: thinning multiplies $\lambda$ by $q$, $d'$ by $\sqrt{q}$, and changes nothing else at all. Week 9's actual work is finding the low-$\lambda$ regime where the Gaussian step in Week 4 breaks down, which is a different failure from this one.

Exercise 3.4★★
Fix the depth at $D = 3000$ and the fold change at $f = 2$ exactly, so $p_2 = 2p_1$. Write $d'$ as a function of $\lambda_1$ alone. How much expression must the gene have (what $\lambda_1$) for a 2-fold marker to reach $d' = 1$? Compare that to the 1.375-fold gene B of section 7 and say which you would rather have.
Show solution

With $\lambda_2 = 2\lambda_1$: $|\lambda_1-\lambda_2| = \lambda_1$ and $\bar\lambda = 1.5\lambda_1$, so

$$d' = \frac{\lambda_1}{\sqrt{1.5\lambda_1}} = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_1}}{\sqrt{1.5}} = 0.8165\,\sqrt{\lambda_1}.$$

Setting $d' = 1$ gives $\sqrt{\lambda_1} = 1.2247$, so $\lambda_1 = 1.5$ counts, meaning $p_1 = 1.5/3000 = 5\times10^{-4}$.

Comparison. Gene B (1.375-fold, $\lambda_1 = 12$) achieves $d' = 1.19$. A 2-fold gene needs only $\lambda_1 = 1.5$ to hit $d' = 1$, so at equal expression the 2-fold gene is far better — at $\lambda_1 = 12$ it would give $d' = 0.8165\sqrt{12} = 2.83$. The point is not that fold change is worthless; it is that fold change alone is not enough information to rank genes. You need both factors, and $d'$ is the thing that combines them correctly.

Keep the formula $d' = \sqrt{\lambda_1}\,|f-1|/\sqrt{(1+f)/2}$ in your notebook. It is the cleanest way to see the two-factor structure, and in Week 10 you will use it to build a marker-ranking that beats the fold-change ranking on real data — which is a result, and a small one you can genuinely claim as your own.

Exercise 3.5★★
Assumption 2 of the theorem says both types are sequenced at the same depth. Suppose instead that type 1 cells happen to yield $D_1 = 2000$ molecules and type 2 cells $D_2 = 4000$. Take a gene with identical biology in both types, $p_1 = p_2 = 0.003$. Compute $d'$. What has your detector detected? What is the fix, and which week implements it?
Show solution

$\lambda_1 = 2000 \times 0.003 = 6$ and $\lambda_2 = 4000 \times 0.003 = 12$, so $\bar\lambda = 9$ and

$$d' = \frac{|6-12|}{\sqrt{9}} = \frac{6}{3} = 2.00.$$

A separation of 2.00 — equivalent to 84% classification accuracy — from a gene whose expression is exactly the same in the two cell types. The detector has detected library size. If type 2 cells happen to be bigger, or to have been captured on a better day, you will "classify" them beautifully and learn nothing about biology.

The fix is to put every cell on a common depth before comparing: downsample each cell's counts to a fixed $D$ using downsample_counts. This is legitimate precisely because of Week 2's thinning theorem — the downsampled cell is distributed exactly as a genuinely shallower cell, so nothing is being faked. Week 10 does this to the real PBMC 3k matrix, and Week 11's depth-ceiling result depends on it having been done correctly.

This is not a hypothetical failure mode. Library size is the single largest source of variation in most scRNA-seq datasets, and "we found a marker that turned out to be library size" is a genuine, published category of error. Write this exercise out in full; it is the strongest sentence you can put in the Week 14 limitations section.

Exercise 3.6★★★
Redo the depth derivation with overdispersed counts. Assume $\mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \lambda_i + \varphi\lambda_i^2$ for a fixed $\varphi > 0$ (this is the negative binomial of Week 11), and keep the pooled variance as the noise scale. Show that $d'$ no longer grows as $\sqrt{D}$, find its limit as $D \to \infty$, and evaluate that limit for $p_1 = 0.0040$, $p_2 = 0.0055$, $\varphi = 0.5$. What accuracy does it cap out at?
Show solution

Setup. With $\lambda_i = Dp_i$, the pooled variance is

$$\bar\sigma^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[(\lambda_1 + \varphi\lambda_1^{2}) + (\lambda_2 + \varphi\lambda_2^{2})\Big] = D\bar p + \varphi D^{2}\,\frac{p_1^{2}+p_2^{2}}{2},$$

so that

$$d'(D) = \frac{D|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{D\bar p + \varphi D^{2}(p_1^{2}+p_2^{2})/2}}.$$

The two regimes. Factor $D$ out of the square root:

$$d'(D) = \frac{D|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{D}\sqrt{\bar p + \varphi D (p_1^2+p_2^2)/2}}.$$

When $D$ is small the $\varphi$ term is negligible, the bracket is $\approx\bar p$, and we recover $d' \approx \sqrt{D}|p_1-p_2|/\sqrt{\bar p}$ — the Poisson law. When $D$ is large the $\varphi$ term dominates, the denominator becomes proportional to $D$ rather than $\sqrt{D}$, and the $D$'s cancel completely:

$$\lim_{D\to\infty} d'(D) = \frac{|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{\varphi\,(p_1^{2}+p_2^{2})/2}}.$$

The number. $|p_1-p_2| = 0.0015$; $(p_1^2+p_2^2)/2 = (1.6\times10^{-5} + 3.025\times10^{-5})/2 = 2.3125\times10^{-5}$; times $\varphi = 0.5$ gives $1.1563\times10^{-5}$, whose square root is $3.400\times10^{-3}$. So

$$d'_{\max} = \frac{0.0015}{0.003400} = 0.441,$$

and the accuracy ceiling is $\Phi(0.441/2) = \Phi(0.2206) = 58.7\%$.

Read that again. With this much overdispersion, infinite sequencing depth on this gene buys you 58.7% accuracy. At $D = 3000$ you already have $d' = 0.414$, which is 94% of everything the gene will ever give you. Sequencing ten times deeper would move the accuracy from 58.2% to 58.7%.

This is Week 11, done early and in one exercise. It is also the most important single calculation in the project, because it converts "real data is overdispersed" from an annoyance into a quantitative prediction with a ceiling in it — and a prediction with a ceiling is something you can go and test. When you measure $\varphi$ on PBMC 3k in Week 11, this formula is what turns that measurement into a claim about how much deeper sequencing is worth buying. Bring this derivation to the M1 check-in; it is the answer to "what breaks?".
