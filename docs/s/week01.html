1  Where we are going

A human cell contains a few hundred thousand messenger RNA molecules. A single-cell RNA sequencing experiment — the standard 10x Genomics protocol, say — captures and reads a few thousand of them. Not a few thousand kinds: a few thousand molecules, total, spread over twenty thousand genes. Most genes come back as a zero. Many come back as a one.

So when a paper says "this cell expresses CD3D at 4 counts and that one at 6", the honest translation is: we drew a small random sample from each cell, and got 4 and 6. Draw again and you would get 3 and 8. The question this project exists to answer is what that irreducible randomness costs you.

The central question How does the accuracy of cell-type classification scale with (a) sequencing depth per cell and (b) the number of informative marker genes? And where — precisely — does that scaling law stop being true?

By the end of Week 5 you will have derived an answer with no free parameters:

$$\text{accuracy} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{2}\right), \qquad d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth}\times k}.$$

Nothing in that formula is obvious yet, and two of its three symbols are undefined. What matters today is the shape of the claim: discriminability grows as a square root, so doubling how well you can tell two cell types apart costs four times the data. In Weeks 7–12 you will test that claim three independent ways, and in Week 11 you will find the place where it breaks.

What a "cell type" is, for our purposes

Biologists group cells into types — T cell, monocyte, neuron — by what they do and what they express. For this project a cell type is simply a probability distribution over which gene the next transcript belongs to. Type 1 is a vector $p^{(1)}$, type 2 a vector $p^{(2)}$, both summing to 1 over all genes. A marker gene is one where the two vectors differ noticeably.

This is a simplification, and it is worth naming as one right now: real cells of the "same type" differ from each other, which is exactly what breaks the theory in Week 11. Writing the assumption down today is what lets you measure its failure in ten weeks. See the biology background page for what is being glossed over.

2  Counting is noisy, and the noise has a law

Here is the model, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. A cell of type 1 has a fraction $p_g$ of its transcripts belonging to gene $g$. The sequencer captures $D$ molecules at random (this $D$ is the depth, or library size — a few thousand for 10x data). The number of captured molecules that turn out to be gene $g$ is then a count out of $D$ independent draws, each landing on gene $g$ with probability $p_g$: a binomial.

When $D$ is large and $p_g$ is small — thousands of draws, one gene in ten thousand — the binomial is indistinguishable from something simpler:

Definition · the Poisson distribution A random variable $X$ is Poisson with rate $\lambda > 0$, written $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, if $$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$ Its mean is $\lambda$. Its variance is also $\lambda$. You will prove both in Week 2.

That second sentence is the hinge of the entire project, so it deserves its own box:

The coincidence everything rests on For a Poisson variable, variance = mean. So the standard deviation is $\sqrt{\lambda}$, and the relative noise is $$\frac{\text{standard deviation}}{\text{mean}} = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}.$$ Count ten molecules and you are off by about three. Count a thousand and you are off by about thirty — which is a much smaller fraction. Every square root in this project descends from this one line.

It is worth pausing on how unusual this is. Most distributions have a mean and a spread you can set independently: a normal distribution can have mean 100 and standard deviation 1, or mean 100 and standard deviation 50. A Poisson cannot. Fix the mean and the noise is decided. That rigidity is what makes an exact, parameter-free prediction possible at all.

3  Lab: watch the noise track the signal

Before any algebra, look at the thing. Drag the rate slider and watch two numbers: the sample mean and the sample variance. They should stay locked together.

Lab 1 · Poisson counts at a rate you control
Each sample is one "cell": a Poisson count for a single gene at rate $\lambda$. The histogram shows many such cells. Watch the mean and the variance in the readout — and watch the relative width, which is what actually matters for telling cells apart.

Two things to notice. First, the mean and the variance stay equal (up to sampling error) no matter where you put the slider — that is the Fano factor, defined below, sitting at 1. Second, as $\lambda$ grows the histogram gets wider in absolute terms and narrower relative to its mean. Deeper sequencing does not make the noise go away; it makes the noise small compared to the signal. That distinction is the whole project in one sentence.

Definition · the Fano factor For a sample of counts, the Fano factor is $$F = \frac{\text{variance}}{\text{mean}}.$$ Poisson data has $F = 1$ exactly. $F > 1$ means overdispersed: something beyond counting noise is varying. $F < 1$ means underdispersed, which essentially never happens in this setting.

Remember this number. In Week 11 you will compute it on real PBMC data and find it is not 1, and that single measurement is the most important negative result in the project.

Lab 2 · How well can you measure a Fano factor?
The Fano factor of true Poisson data is 1 — but you never have infinite cells. This lab draws many independent samples of the size you choose and histograms the Fano factors you would have reported. Ask yourself: at 50 cells, would you be able to tell $F = 1$ from $F = 1.3$?
The lesson of Lab 2, stated early because it will save you a month A statistic computed from a small sample is itself a random variable. "The Fano factor is 1.3, so the data is overdispersed" is only a claim if you know how much 1.3 could have wobbled. In Week 13 you will put an interval on every number in the project; Lab 2 is the first hint of why that is not optional.

4  What a derivation is, and what it is not

You have just observed that variance tracks mean. That is evidence. It is not a derivation, and the difference matters more in this project than it might in a class.

A derivation is an argument that covers every case at once — including the cases nobody will ever run. It starts from stated assumptions and moves by steps a skeptical reader can check. The reason it matters here is specific and practical: a derivation tells you when it stops applying. Lab 1 will never tell you that the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law fails under overdispersion; the derivation in Week 3 will, because you will be able to point at the exact line that used "variance = mean" and ask what happens when that line is false.

Worked derivation 1 · the mean of a sum

Claim. If $X$ and $Y$ are random variables, $E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$ — whether or not they are independent.

Argument. Expectation is a weighted sum over outcomes. For each joint outcome $(x, y)$ with probability $P(x,y)$, $$E[X+Y] = \sum_{x,y} (x + y) P(x,y) = \sum_{x,y} x\,P(x,y) + \sum_{x,y} y\,P(x,y) = E[X] + E[Y],$$ where the last step collapses each double sum over the variable it does not involve. Nothing about independence was used. ∎

Keep this in view: the corresponding claim for variance does need independence, and that asymmetry is exactly what Week 5's $\sqrt{k}$ law turns on.

Worked derivation 2 · why a square root, in general

Claim. If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent with common mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then the sum $S = \sum_i X_i$ has mean $n\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{n}$ — so the ratio (mean to standard deviation) grows like $\sqrt{n}$.

Argument. $E[S] = n\mu$ by the previous result. For independent variables variances add, so $\mathrm{Var}(S) = n\sigma^2$ and $\mathrm{sd}(S) = \sigma\sqrt{n}$. Therefore $$\frac{E[S]}{\mathrm{sd}(S)} = \frac{n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\mu}{\sigma}\sqrt{n}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

This is the skeleton of every square-root law you will meet — in depth (Week 3), in the number of genes (Week 5), in the number of beads (Week 12), and even in the width of your own error bars (Week 13). Four of the five appearances are this one argument wearing different clothes. Recognizing that is worth more than memorizing any of them.

Three habits to build from day one
  • Say what you assumed. Every derivation in this project should end with a one-line list of the assumptions it used. That list is what Week 11 will attack.
  • Do not verify and call it proving. Ten examples are evidence. Label it observed and move on honestly.
  • Write down the failures. "I tried to get the variance by squaring the mean; that is wrong because…" is worth more in eight weeks than a clean page.

6  Exercises

Stars mark difficulty: ★ routine · ★★ requires an idea · ★★★ challenge. Write full sentences; every claim needs a reason. Solutions are provided — attempt first, then compare.

Exercise 1.1★
A gene makes up a fraction $p = 0.002$ of a cell's transcripts. The sequencer captures $D = 3000$ molecules. What is the expected count for that gene, and what is its standard deviation? What is the relative noise?
Show solution
The expected count is $\lambda = Dp = 3000 \times 0.002 = 6$. For a Poisson variable the variance equals the mean, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{6} \approx 2.45$. The relative noise is $2.45 / 6 \approx 41\%$.

Forty-one percent. And $p = 0.002$ makes this a well-expressed gene — most genes are an order of magnitude rarer. This single number is why the project is not trivial.

Exercise 1.2★
You want the relative noise on that gene's count down to 10%. What depth $D$ do you need? What multiple of the original depth is that?
Show solution
Relative noise is $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$, so $1/\sqrt{\lambda} = 0.10$ gives $\lambda = 100$, and $D = \lambda/p = 100/0.002 = 50{,}000$ molecules per cell — about $17\times$ the original depth.

Note the shape of the answer: to improve the noise by a factor of 4 ($41\% \to 10\%$) you needed roughly $4^2 = 16$ times the data. The square-root law, met before it is derived.

Exercise 1.3★★
Two cell types express a marker gene at $p_1 = 0.0030$ and $p_2 = 0.0036$. At depth $D = 3000$, write down both expected counts and both standard deviations. Sketch the two distributions on one axis. Would you be willing to classify a single cell from this one gene? Say why in one sentence, using numbers.
Show solution

$\lambda_1 = 9$ and $\lambda_2 = 10.8$, with standard deviations $3.0$ and $3.29$.

The two means are $1.8$ apart while each distribution is about $3$ wide: the separation is well under one standard deviation, so the two histograms overlap almost completely. From one gene in one cell you would be guessing — barely better than a coin.

The ratio $1.8/3.1 \approx 0.58$ is the quantity you will name $d'$ in Week 3, and $\Phi(0.58/2) \approx 0.61$ will turn out to be the exact accuracy of the best possible rule. You are two weeks from being able to say that instead of "you would be guessing".

Exercise 1.4★★
Prove that if $X$ and $Y$ are independent then $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)$, and give a concrete example (any two dependent variables you like) showing the claim fails without independence.
Show solution

Write $\mathrm{Var}(Z) = E[Z^2] - (E[Z])^2$. With $Z = X + Y$, $$E[(X+Y)^2] = E[X^2] + 2E[XY] + E[Y^2], \qquad (E[X+Y])^2 = (E[X])^2 + 2E[X]E[Y] + (E[Y])^2.$$ Subtracting, $$\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y) + 2\big(E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]\big).$$ The bracketed term is the covariance, which is zero for independent variables. ∎

Counterexample: take $Y = X$ with $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 > 0$. Then $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \mathrm{Var}(2X) = 4\sigma^2$, not $2\sigma^2$. Take $Y = -X$ and the variance of the sum is $0$.

Keep the general formula, not just the independent case. In Week 5 you will assume genes are independent in order to add their evidence; the covariance term is precisely what you are throwing away, and a good Week 14 limitations section names it.

Exercise 1.5★★
A friend proposes: "Instead of sequencing deeper, just measure more cells. Ten times as many cells is the same as ten times the depth." Is that right? Answer for two different questions: (a) estimating the average expression of a gene in a cell type, and (b) classifying one particular cell.
Show solution

(a) Yes, essentially. The mean expression over $N$ cells at depth $D$ is built from $ND$ molecules in total, and its standard error falls as $1/\sqrt{ND}$. For estimating a population quantity, cells and depth are interchangeable.

(b) No, not at all. Classifying this cell uses only the molecules captured from this cell. Sequencing a million other cells does not tell you anything more about which type this one is — except indirectly, by pinning down the rates $p^{(1)}, p^{(2)}$ the classifier uses. Once those are known accurately, more cells buy nothing for a per-cell decision.

This distinction — population estimation versus per-cell detection — is why the project is about depth and genes rather than about cell numbers, and it is the single most common misunderstanding you will have to correct in the Q&A in Week 15. Write the two-sentence answer in your notebook now.

Exercise 1.6★★★
In Lab 2, the spread of the measured Fano factor shrinks as the number of cells grows. Guess the rate at which it shrinks (as a power of $n$), test your guess in the lab by comparing $n = 50$ to $n = 200$, and say why the answer is not a coincidence. Hint: the Fano factor is built from a sample variance, and a sample variance of $n$ values is itself an average of $n$ things.
Show solution

The spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$: quadrupling the number of cells halves the width of the Fano-factor distribution. In the lab, $n = 200$ should give a histogram about half as wide as $n = 50$.

Why: the sample variance is an average of $n$ terms (roughly, the squared deviations), and an average of $n$ independent things has a standard deviation falling as $1/\sqrt{n}$ — the same argument as Worked derivation 2. For Poisson data one can go further and show the standard deviation of $F$ is close to $\sqrt{2/n}$; at $n = 50$ that is $0.20$, which matches the lab.

So the tool you use to detect a square-root law is itself governed by one. That is not a coincidence: both are the central limit theorem, which is also where the $\Phi$ in Week 4's formula comes from. Three of this project's main results are one theorem in three costumes.
