Week 14 MILESTONE M7 git tag report-draft

Writing the Report

Six to eight self-contained pages · the derivation chain and what to cut · the figure freeze · and the limitations section that earns everything else

Objectives

  • State who the report is for, and what "self-contained" costs you in each section.
  • Lay out the report section by section against a word budget, and keep the total inside 6–8 pages.
  • Write the theory section as a single chain — Poisson → variance $=$ mean → $d'$ → $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ → quadrature → $\sqrt{k}$ → $\Phi(d'/2)$ — with one sentence of motivation and one line of algebra per step, and cut everything that is not on the chain.
  • Write a limitations section that covers the full checklist, unprompted, before any reader finds an item you missed.
  • Freeze the figures: regenerate F1–F6 from one command, with captions and seeds, and verify that a fresh run reproduces exactly what the report cites.
  • Write a caption that stands alone: what is shown, what was varied, what the error bars are, what $n$ was, and what seed produced it.
  • State a negative result in a way that reads as a finding rather than an apology.
  • Check every claim in the draft against its status in the Week 13 ledger and repair any that drifted.

1  The target, and who it is for

Six to eight pages. Self-contained. Readable by someone who has not taken this course.

That last constraint is the one that does the work, so make it concrete: picture a first-year undergraduate in physics or biology, or a science-fair judge with a chemistry PhD and eleven minutes. They know what a mean and a standard deviation are. They have not heard of $d'$, they do not know what a UMI is, and they will not look anything up. Everything your report needs, your report must supply.

Self-contained does not mean long. It means that every symbol is defined where it first appears, every dataset is named with its size, and every claim is either supported inside the document or attributed to something outside it. A reader should never have to hold an unexplained term in their head and hope it gets explained later — and if they do, they stop reading, and they were not being unfair.

What this report is claiming

Written out once, so that every section can be tested against it:

Cell-type classification from single-cell RNA counts is a detection problem. Under Poisson counting the separation index obeys $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth}\times k}$ and the optimal accuracy is $\Phi(d'/2)$, with no free parameters. Simulation, downsampled 10x PBMC data, and a physical bead-drawing experiment each test that prediction. It holds in simulation, it holds in the beads, and on real data it fails in a specific and measurable way: counts are overdispersed, so $d'$ saturates and deeper sequencing eventually stops buying accuracy.

If a paragraph you are writing does not serve that claim, its assumptions, its tests or its failure, it is not in this report. Put it in an appendix or in the notebook.

The thing to hold on to this week A good report is not a transcript of what you did. It is the shortest honest path from a question to an answer and the limits of that answer, arranged so a stranger can check it. The work you did in the order you did it is a diary; almost nobody wants to read a diary. What they want is the argument, and the argument only became clear in Week 11.

2  The structure, with a word budget

Budget before you write. A report that is written first and cut afterwards loses the wrong material, because by then you are attached to the paragraphs that cost you the most effort — and those are usually the ones documenting a detour.

The conversion used throughout this chapter: a full page of text is about 600 words at 11pt single-spaced, a figure with its caption takes about a third of a page, and the references take about 0.4 of a page. Those numbers are not laws; they are what makes the arithmetic checkable.

The Space column allocates each section its share of the page including whatever figure sits in it. Lab 1 below reaches the total the other way — words divided by 600, plus a fixed allowance of 0.33 pages per figure and 0.4 for references — and gets 7.16 pages. Two routes, the same budget, which is the check that the plan is arithmetically possible before you write a word of it.

Note what the budget implies. The Results section gets 750 words to cover four figures, which is under 200 words per figure — one paragraph each. If that feels impossible, it is because you are planning to narrate the figures instead of stating what they show. And Limitations gets 350 words, which is more than the Conclusion and half of Results: that ratio is deliberate and Section 7 argues for it.

Why the Results-versus-Theory warning exists A report whose results outweigh its theory and methods combined is a report a reader cannot check. They can see what you found; they cannot see what you predicted before you found it, or how you found it, so they have no way to tell a confirmed prediction from a described observation. In this project that would be fatal, because the entire contribution is that the prediction was made first, from Poisson statistics, with no parameters fitted to the data. Space spent on the derivation is what turns your results from measurements into a test.

3  How to write the theory section

This is where most student reports go wrong, in both directions at once: too much algebra and too little argument. The theory section has exactly one job, which is to get the reader from "counting is random" to "accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$ with $d' \propto \sqrt{Dk}$" without losing them and without asking them to take anything on faith.

There is a single chain, and it has seven links.

The chain — one sentence of motivation, one line of algebra, each
  1. Counts are Poisson. Motivation: a sequencer draws $D$ molecules at random from a cell whose gene $g$ makes up a fraction $p_g$ of transcripts, so the count is binomial with $D$ large and $p_g$ small. Algebra: $x_g \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(D, p_g) \approx \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_g)$, $\lambda_g = Dp_g$.
  2. Variance $=$ mean. Motivation: for a Poisson variable the spread is not a free parameter — fix the mean and the noise is decided. Algebra: $E[x] = \mathrm{Var}(x) = \lambda$, so $\mathrm{sd}(x) = \sqrt{\lambda}$.
  3. Define $d'$. Motivation: what matters for telling two types apart is the gap between their means measured in units of the noise. Algebra: $d' = |\lambda_1 - \lambda_2| / \sqrt{\bar\lambda}$ with $\bar\lambda = (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/2$.
  4. $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$. Motivation: depth scales both means, so the gap grows linearly while the noise grows as a square root. Algebra: $d' = D|p_1 - p_2| / \sqrt{D\bar p} = \sqrt{D}\,|p_1-p_2|/\sqrt{\bar p}$.
  5. Evidence adds in quadrature. Motivation: the optimal rule is the log-likelihood ratio, which for independent genes is a sum, and for independent terms variances add. Algebra: $(d'_{\text{total}})^2 = \sum_g (d'_g)^2$.
  6. $d' \propto \sqrt{k}$. Motivation: $k$ equally informative genes each contribute the same squared term. Algebra: $d'_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{k}\,d'_{\text{gene}}$, hence $d' \propto \sqrt{Dk}$.
  7. Accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$. Motivation: under the Gaussian approximation with equal priors the optimal threshold sits midway between two distributions separated by $d'$ standard deviations. Algebra: $\text{accuracy} = \Phi(d'/2)$, with no free parameters.

Seven links, seven sentences, seven displayed lines: roughly 450 words, which leaves 150 for the assumption list and the transition. That is your 1.5 pages including F2.

What to cut, specifically
  • The derivation of $E[X] = \lambda$ from the pmf. State it. It is standard, it is in every probability text, and your reader either knows it or will accept it. Week 2's two pages of algebra belong in the notebook.
  • The binomial-to-Poisson limit in detail. One clause — "with $D$ in the thousands and $p_g$ below $10^{-3}$, the binomial and Poisson agree to well within the noise" — replaces a page.
  • The full log-likelihood-ratio derivation. Give the result $\mathrm{LLR}(x) = \sum_g[x_g\log(\lambda_{1g}/\lambda_{2g}) - (\lambda_{1g}-\lambda_{2g})]$, note that it is linear in the counts and that the $\log(x_g!)$ terms cancel, and move on. The reader needs to know the rule is linear; they do not need to watch it become linear.
  • ROC curves and AUC. Interesting, real, and not on the chain. One sentence in Methods if a classifier was scored that way, otherwise nothing.
  • Every classifier that is not naive Bayes. Logistic regression and kNN were Week 8's way of checking that the classifier was not the bottleneck. That is a Methods sentence and possibly a number in Results, not a theory subsection.
  • Anything you learned that the argument does not need. This is the hard one. Delete it anyway, and put it in the talk, where a good tangent is an asset.

End the section with the assumption list, in one short paragraph and in plain words: counts are Poisson; genes are independent given the cell type; all cells of a type are identical; the two types are equally likely; the Gaussian approximation to the log-likelihood ratio is adequate. Five items. Each one is attacked later in the report, and listing them here is what makes the Limitations section read as a continuation rather than as a confession.

4  Methods, and Results built around F1–F6

Methods: enough to re-run you

The test for the Methods section is not "is it complete" — nothing is complete. It is: could a competent stranger with your repository reproduce your numbers? Four paragraphs, roughly 110 words each.

  • Simulator. The generative model in one sentence (a type is a probability vector over genes; a cell is Poisson counts at rate $D p_g$; dropout deletes molecules independently). The marker-mass convention, stated explicitly — see Section 7, this one bites. Cells per type per point. The function names, so the reader can find them.
  • PBMC protocol. Dataset and version (10x PBMC 3k), how many cells and genes survive filtering, which two types, where the labels came from and that they are annotations rather than ground truth, the marker count (32) and how markers were selected using training data only, and the downsampling procedure with its keep-fractions.
  • Bead protocol. Jar compositions (30/70 and 35/65), draw sizes, trials per draw size (40), the dropout rule, who drew and who tallied, and how a trial was scored. The detail that matters most: the decision rule was fixed in Week 6, before any beads were drawn.
  • Seeds and software. Every seed (101, 105, 107, 109, 111, 112, 113, 115), the one command that regenerates every figure, the Python and NumPy versions, and the repository commit or tag. Point at figures/report/SEEDS.json rather than listing seeds twice.

Results: four figures, four paragraphs, no narration

The report's figures are already chosen: F1–F6, produced by the freeze script in Section 6. F1 sits in the Introduction (the phenomenon) and F2 in Theory (the prediction). Results carries F3 to F6, and the shape of the section is fixed by them.

Do not narrate. "We first ran the simulation at depth 250, then at 500, and observed that accuracy increased" is a description of a loop. "Across depths 250–16,000 the measured accuracy tracks $\Phi(d'/2)$ with a maximum absolute deviation of 0.02 (F3)" is a result. The second sentence is shorter and says more, and it can be checked.

5  Writing a caption

The standard a caption has to meet A reader who reads nothing else — who lands on your figure while flipping through — must be able to say what is being shown, what was varied, what the error bars mean, how many trials produced each point, and what seed generated it. Captions are read far more often than body text. In a poster session they may be all that is read.

A caption is not a title. "Accuracy versus depth" is a title, and it is already on the axes. The caption is where the figure becomes evidence rather than decoration.

The same figure, captioned twice

Weak. "Figure 5. Results of the bead experiment. The data agrees well with the theoretical prediction."

What is on the axes? How many trials? What are the bars? Which curve is which arm? What does "well" mean? And "agrees well" is a claim, made in a caption, with nothing supporting it.

Strong. "Figure 5. Bead-drawing accuracy against draw size $n$, for the plain arm (magenta) and the 30% dropout arm (teal). Points are the fraction of correct jar identifications over 40 trials per draw size; error bars are 95% Wilson intervals. Curves are the prediction computed in Week 6 before any beads were drawn, with no fitted parameters. Analysis seed 112."

Sixty words. It names the axes, the arms, the colours, the trial count, the interval type, the provenance of the curve, and the seed — and the clause "before any beads were drawn" does more for the reader's trust than a paragraph of discussion could.

6  The figure freeze

At some point the figures have to stop moving. Otherwise the number in your Results paragraph came from a run you no longer have, the PNG in the document came from a different one, and neither matches what the script produces today. This is not hypothetical; it is the normal fate of a project that regenerates figures by hand.

The freeze script

python experiments/week14_report_figures.py regenerates the report's figures into figures/report/. It works from a table at the top of the file:

  • PLAN — a list of (destination name, module, index) triples. It imports each week's experiment module, calls its main(), and copies the figure at the given index out of the returned path list. So ("F5_beads", week12_beads, 0) means "F5 is the first figure week12_beads.main() returns".
  • CAPTIONS — a dictionary from destination name to caption text. Edit the captions here, not in your word processor, so that the caption and the figure are regenerated by the same command.

It writes CAPTIONS.md (each caption with its source script, seed and status) and SEEDS.json (machine-readable provenance for every panel). Cite SEEDS.json in Methods instead of listing seeds by hand.

To regenerate everything in the project, not just the report set: python experiments/make_all_figures.py (minutes), or --smoke for a tiny run that checks the pipeline works and produces figures you must never cite. --only week12_beads week13_error_bars restricts the list.

The refusal rule

Two of the experiment scripts can fall back to stand-in data: week11_overdispersion.py runs on a surrogate matrix if the PBMC cache is missing, and week12_beads.py simulates draws if no filled-in tally sheets exist. Each sets a module-level flag USED_STAND_IN = True when it does, and stamps the figure.

The freeze script reads that flag and refuses to copy the figure. It does not warn; it does not copy with a stamp; it leaves the slot empty, lists the figure under Figures refused in CAPTIONS.md, and tells you what to do (fetch_pbmc3k.py, or fill in data/beads/*.csv).

The rule is worth stating as a principle: the pipeline must make the dishonest thing harder than the honest one. A figure that says SURROGATE in faint red across the middle is easy to overlook at 1 a.m. the night before a deadline. A figure that is not there is not.

The freeze test, and why byte-identical is the standard

Delete the directory, regenerate, and compare:

rm -rf python/figures/report
cd python && python experiments/week14_report_figures.py
md5sum figures/report/*.png > /tmp/freeze1.txt
rm -rf figures/report
python experiments/week14_report_figures.py
md5sum figures/report/*.png > /tmp/freeze2.txt
diff /tmp/freeze1.txt /tmp/freeze2.txt   # must be empty

Why byte-identical and not "looks the same". Because "looks the same" is not a test anyone can run, and because a difference in the bytes is a difference in the pixels, and a difference in the pixels means something in the pipeline was not seeded. The check is cheap, automatic and total: one diff covers every number in every panel at once. Anything weaker requires a human to squint at two images and decide, and humans reliably decide that two figures look the same.

It also buys something specific: it lets you answer "where did this number come from?" with a command rather than with a memory. That question will be asked in Week 15, and it is the question a mentor most wants to hear answered well.

The honest caveat. Byte-identity holds within one environment. matplotlib writes its own version string into the PNG header, so upgrading matplotlib changes the bytes without changing the science. Record the Python, NumPy and matplotlib versions in Methods, and run the freeze test twice on your machine on the same day. If the hashes differ there, something is genuinely unseeded — an unseeded generator, a set or dictionary being iterated where order matters, or an unsorted glob over the bead CSVs — and you have found a real bug in your own reproducibility.

7  The limitations section is the one that earns the rest

Most students treat Limitations as a tax: a paragraph of hedging at the end, written in the passive voice, listing things that "could be improved in future work". That paragraph is worth nothing, and every experienced reader skips it.

Turn it around. A reader trusts a paper in proportion to what its authors admit. This is not a moral claim, it is a practical one. Your reader cannot check your code. They cannot re-run your beads. The only evidence they have about your judgement is what you say about your own work — and specifically, whether you found the weaknesses before they did. An author who names five real problems has demonstrated that they looked; the rest of the report inherits that credit. An author who names none has demonstrated only that they did not look, or looked and did not say.

So write it early — this week, first, before Results — and write it in the first person, in full sentences, with numbers.

The limitations checklist — every item must appear
  • Labels are annotations, not ground truth. PBMC cell types come from clustering and marker-based annotation of the same data you are classifying. "Accuracy" is agreement with a labelling that was itself produced by an algorithm, and it is not obvious in which direction that biases the number. Say what your labels came from and that they can be wrong.
  • One donor. Use the Week 13 sentence: intervals on real-data quantities are bootstraps over cells within one donor; they quantify cell sampling, not biological variation between people; with $n = 1$ donor the latter cannot be estimated at all.
  • Genes are assumed independent. They are not — co-regulated genes rise and fall together. Independence is what licensed adding $d'$ in quadrature (chain step 5), so positive correlation means the effective number of independent genes is below $k$ and the $\sqrt{k}$ law overstates what markers buy. State the direction of the error even if you cannot quantify it.
  • The marker-mass convention. In marker_profiles the two types put an average of marker_mass on the marker set, split equally, with type 1 exceeding type 2 by the fold change. Whether you hold the pooled mass fixed as $k$ grows (so per-gene signal falls as $1/k$) or hold per-gene signal fixed (so total mass grows with $k$) changes what a $\sqrt{k}$ curve even means. Say which convention each figure used — F2/F3 and F6 do not use the same one — and why.
  • The Gaussian approximation fails at low depth. $\Phi(d'/2)$ comes from treating the log-likelihood ratio as normal. At very low depth the counts are mostly zeros, the ratio is lumpy and the approximation breaks. Week 9 measured this. Note that the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law itself is not what fails there — a distinction worth one sentence, because it is the difference between a broken theory and a known-range theory.
  • $\varphi$ treated as fixed under thinning. The negative-binomial prediction scales the rates by the keep-fraction and holds the per-gene dispersion $\varphi$ constant. That is exactly right only in a limit; under real thinning the measured $\varphi$ moves. Say that the NB curve is therefore approximate, and in which direction.
  • The bead exponent's interval. Quote it as a number with its interval and say plainly what it does and does not exclude. An exponent of $0.42 \pm 0.20$ is consistent with $1/2$; it is also consistent with $0.3$ and with $0.6$. It supports the prediction weakly. Weak support honestly described is worth more than strong support asserted.
  • Any point dropped from any fit. Every one. Which point, why, what the fit gives with it included, and who made the decision. If a bead point was dropped because the jar was refilled mid-run, say so — that is a good reason and it reads as one. A dropped point that surfaces in Q&A instead of in the text is the single fastest way to lose a room.
  • Surrogate or simulated stand-ins, if any figure still uses one. If the freeze script refused a figure and the report ships without it, say which claim consequently has no figure behind it. If, against the rule, a stamped figure is included anywhere, it must be labelled as such in the caption and in this list, in the same words.
One limitation, written badly and then written well

Bad. "Some limitations exist in this study. The data was obtained from a single source and gene independence was assumed, which may have affected results. Future work could address these issues."

Good. "Two assumptions limit what these results support. First, the real-data analysis uses one donor (10x PBMC 3k), so my intervals describe resampling of cells within one blood sample and say nothing about variation between people; testing that would need several donors analysed with the donor, not the cell, as the unit. Second, adding $d'$ in quadrature across genes assumes the genes are conditionally independent. Marker genes are co-regulated, so the effective number of independent genes is below the nominal $k$, and my $\sqrt{k}$ prediction should be read as an upper bound on what additional markers buy."

The good version is longer, and it is the better use of the words. It names the assumption, says what it affects, gives the direction of the error, and states what would settle it. "May have affected results" does none of those and asks the reader to imagine the problem for themselves — which they will, generously, in the worst available direction.

8  Stating a negative result, and who decided what

The most interesting thing in this project is a negative result: on real data the Poisson prediction fails, because counts are overdispersed, and past a certain depth more sequencing buys almost nothing. Reports routinely bury this kind of finding under apology. Do not.

The same finding, twice

Bad. "Unfortunately, our theoretical model did not work on the real dataset. We were unable to confirm our hypothesis, likely due to noise in the data and the limitations of our approach. With more time we would have investigated further."

Four failures in three sentences. "Did not work" says nothing measurable. "Unable to confirm" frames a result as a personal shortfall. "Likely due to noise" is a guess offered as an explanation. And "with more time" invites the reader to discount everything above it.

Good. "On downsampled PBMC data the measured accuracy falls below the Poisson prediction at every keep-fraction, by ⟨0.0X to 0.0Y⟩, with Wilson intervals excluding zero at ⟨m⟩ of 8 points. Replacing the Poisson variance with the measured negative-binomial variance $\mathrm{Var} = m + \varphi m^2$ ($\varphi$ median ⟨0.0Z⟩) reduces the discrepancy to ⟨0.0W⟩. The failure is therefore attributable to overdispersion rather than to the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ scaling, which the simulation and bead arms both reproduce. The practical consequence is a ceiling: past ⟨depth⟩, additional depth changes predicted accuracy by less than ⟨amount⟩."

The bracketed values are placeholders for your own run's numbers — the shape of the sentences is the lesson, not the digits. Notice what the good version does: it quantifies the gap, it names the cause, it shows the cause is sufficient by fixing the model and re-measuring, it isolates which part of the theory survived, and it ends with something a person designing a sequencing run could act on. It reads as a finding because it is one.

The passive voice, and "we chose 32 markers"

The passive hides the agent, and the agent is usually you. "The threshold was set at 0.5" — by whom, when, and knowing what? If you set it before seeing the test data, that is a fact in your favour and the passive voice throws it away. If you set it after, that is something the reader is entitled to know. Compare: "I fixed the decision threshold at 0.5 before evaluating on held-out cells" against "a threshold of 0.5 was used". Same threshold; entirely different evidential value. Write Methods in the first person and in the past tense. It is shorter, and it is answerable.

"We chose 32 markers" is half a sentence. Why 32? The reader's suspicion — reasonable, and the one a judge will voice — is that you tried several values and reported the best. Kill it with the truth: "I used the top 32 markers by training-set separation. The number was fixed before any test-set evaluation; I did not tune it. Results at 16 and 64 markers are in the appendix and change the headline accuracy by less than ⟨amount⟩." If you did not run the sensitivity check, say that instead — "the choice was not varied" is a real limitation and a cheap one to admit; a fabricated robustness claim is not recoverable.

The general rule: any number in your report that a reader could imagine having been chosen to make things look good needs one clause saying when it was chosen and against what.

9  Common report failures

Four failures, and how to detect each in your own draft

1. Results that are really Methods. "We then trained a logistic regression with learning rate 0.5 for 1200 iterations and evaluated it on the held-out 30%." That is a procedure, and procedures belong in Methods. Detection: go through Results and highlight every sentence containing a parameter value or the word "then". If more than a fifth of the section lights up, you are describing rather than reporting. Fix: move the sentence to Methods and replace it with what it produced, plus an interval.

2. A Discussion that repeats the Results. "As shown in Figure 4, accuracy decreased at low keep-fractions." The reader saw Figure 4 ninety seconds ago. Detection: if a Discussion sentence could be deleted without removing any claim not already made, it is a repeat. Fix: the Discussion answers three questions Results does not — why the numbers came out that way, what would have to be true for the alternative explanation to hold, and what someone should do differently as a result. In a 6–8 page report, most of this belongs inside the Results paragraphs and the rest in Limitations; a separate Discussion is optional here and is usually where padding hides.

3. Claims that drifted a level. Between the Week 9 log ("simulation matched to within 0.02 at this seed" — observed) and the report ("we proved that simulation matches theory" — proved) a claim moved up a level, and nobody decided to move it. Detection: put the draft next to progress/week13_claims.md and check every claim's status word by word. Hunt the verbs: "proves", "shows", "demonstrates", "confirms", "establishes". Fix: demote to the ledger status, or promote the ledger status and be prepared to defend the promotion at the whiteboard. Never let the report be the first place a claim appears at its new level.

4. The abstract that promises a different paper. Written first, from ambition, and never revised. Detection: read the abstract last, out loud, and check every noun in it against the finished Results. Fix: write the abstract after everything else, in 150 words: question, approach, headline number with its interval, the limit. Four sentences is enough.

"Figure 3. Classification accuracy of the optimal (naive-Bayes) rule on simulated cells against sequencing depth, for marker-set sizes $k = 1$, 8 and 64 (colours). Curves are the prediction $\Phi(d'/2)$ with $d' = \sqrt{\sum_g (\lambda_{1g}-\lambda_{2g})^2/\bar\lambda_g}$, computed from the simulator's own rate vectors with no parameters fitted to these points. Seventy words. It names what varied (depth, and $k$ by colour), the sample size (4,000 per type per point), the error bars (Wilson, and why they are not drawn — never silently omit them), the provenance of the curve, the no-free-parameters claim, and the seed.

The clause about omitted intervals is the one students leave out and the one a careful reader looks for. "There are no error bars" and "the error bars are smaller than the dots" look identical on paper and mean completely different things.

Exercise 14.4★★
For each pair, say whether the claim drifted a level between the log and the report, name the two statuses, and give a correct report sentence. (a) Log: "measured median Fano 2.4 on PBMC markers, seed 111". Report: "we showed that single-cell counts are overdispersed." (b) Log: "derived that dropout at rate $q$ equals depth $D(1-q)$; verified numerically at three values of $q$". Report: "dropout is equivalent to reduced depth (Section 2.3)." (c) Log: "bead exponent 0.42, 95% interval $[0.22, 0.62]$". Report: "the bead experiment confirms the square-root law."
Show solution

(a) Drifted. observed → an implied proved, and it also generalizes from one donor's marker genes to "single-cell counts" everywhere. Correct: "In this dataset the median Fano factor across the selected marker genes is 2.4 (seed 111, one donor), well above the Poisson value of 1; counts here are overdispersed."

(b) Did not drift. proved in the log, proved in the report, and the report's version is fine — but it should carry its assumptions. Better: "Dropout at rate $q$ is exactly equivalent to sequencing at depth $D(1-q)$, by the thinning property of the Poisson distribution, provided dropout acts independently on each molecule and does not depend on the gene or the cell (Section 2.3); the equivalence was also verified numerically at $q = 0$, 0.3 and 0.6."

(c) Drifted, badly. conjectured → proved. "Confirms" is far too strong for an interval that spans 0.22 to 0.62 and therefore excludes almost nothing. Correct: "The bead experiment gives a fitted exponent of 0.42 with a 95% interval of $[0.22, 0.62]$. This is consistent with the predicted 1/2, but the interval is wide enough to be consistent with a range of other exponents; the experiment supports the prediction weakly, and the width is set by the 40 trials per draw size that were feasible by hand."

Part (c) is the one to internalize. "Consistent with" and "confirms" are not synonyms: a measurement consistent with everything confirms nothing. Saying so yourself is what makes the rest of your report believable — and it is the honest answer to the Week 15 question "isn't $0.42 \pm 0.2$ consistent with anything?"

Exercise 14.5★★
Write the limitations entry for the marker-mass convention, 60–90 words, for a reader who has never seen marker_profiles. It must say what the convention is, which figures use which version, and why a reader should care.
Show solution

"My simulator gives two cell types an average total probability marker_mass spread over $k$ marker genes, with type 1 exceeding type 2 by a fixed fold change. Two conventions appear in this report. Where the pooled mass is held fixed as $k$ grows (F2, F3), per-gene signal falls as $1/k$ and the $\sqrt{k}$ law is tested against a genuine trade-off. Where per-gene mass is held fixed (F6), total marker signal grows with $k$. The two answer different questions, and only the first bears on whether adding markers to a real panel helps."

Eighty-eight words. It states the convention, distinguishes the two versions, attributes each to specific figures, and gives the reader the reason to care in the final clause: only one of the two conventions corresponds to the decision an experimenter actually faces.

This is the limitation most likely to be missed, because it lives inside a function signature rather than in the biology. It is also the one a sharp judge will find: "if you split the same signal over more genes, why would anything improve?" is a natural question, and Week 5 answered it — a fixed total split over more genes gains you nothing. If a figure appears to show a gain, the reader deserves to know which convention produced it.

Exercise 14.6★★★
You run the freeze test and the two sets of hashes differ for exactly one figure. List five distinct causes that could produce that, ordered by how likely they are in this repository, and give the diagnostic and the fix for each. Then say which of the five would not be caught by simply looking at the two images.
Show solution
  1. An unseeded generator. A stray np.random.default_rng() with no argument, or np.random.rand used directly instead of the passed rng. Diagnostic: grep the module for random and check every construction site takes a seed. Fix: thread the single seeded rng through.
  2. A shared generator consumed in a different order. Every script threads one rng; if a loop's iteration order changes, or a step is added, every subsequent draw shifts. Diagnostic: the figure changes only after an unrelated edit earlier in the script. Fix: derive an independent generator per stage, or accept it and re-freeze deliberately.
  3. Unsorted glob over the bead CSVs. glob.glob returns filesystem order, which is not guaranteed. If records are concatenated in a different order, ties and floating-point summation order can change. Diagnostic: it reproduces on your machine but not on another. Fix: sorted(glob.glob(...)).
  4. Iteration over a set, or over a dict built from one. Set ordering is not stable across runs for strings when hash randomization is on. Diagnostic: legend entries or colours swap between runs. Fix: sort before iterating.
  5. A library version change. matplotlib writes its version into the PNG header, so an upgrade changes the bytes with no visible change at all. NumPy changes can also move the last digit of a reduction. Diagnostic: every figure's hash changes at once, and the images look identical. Fix: pin the versions, record them in Methods, and re-freeze once deliberately.

Not caught by looking: number 5 for certain — a header-only change is invisible. Number 4 is often invisible too, if the swap only changes which of two overlapping series is drawn on top. And numbers 1–3 are invisible whenever the change is smaller than a pixel, which for a well-converged Monte Carlo is most of the time. That is the whole argument for hashing: the failures that eyes miss are exactly the quiet ones that make a number irreproducible.

Cause 2 is worth dwelling on. It means "the figure changed" does not imply "the figure is wrong" — a deliberate, documented re-freeze is a normal event. What must never happen is a change nobody noticed. Record the hashes in the progress log so that a change always has a date attached to it.
