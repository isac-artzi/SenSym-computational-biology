1  Why simulate when you already have the formula?

Aim 1 is finished. You can compute the accuracy of the optimal classifier for any depth, any set of rates, any number of genes, in one line, exactly. So why spend a week writing a program that draws random numbers and counts how often it gets the answer right, when you can just evaluate $\Phi(d'/2)$?

The bad answer is "to check that the theory works". It sounds reasonable and it is a trap: if the simulator is written by the person who derived the theory, from the same mental model, in the same afternoon, then it will usually agree with the theory even when both are wrong. Agreement between two things you built from the same belief is not evidence about the world.

The good answer is sharper.

What a simulator actually is

A simulator is a second, independent implementation of your beliefs — one written in the language of procedures (draw a Poisson count, then another, then classify) rather than the language of formulas (integrate, approximate, take a limit).

The two implementations use different machinery. The formula uses the Gaussian approximation, quadrature, and the assumption that the pooled variance is the right noise scale. The simulator uses none of these — it just draws numbers and counts. So when they agree, they agree despite travelling by different routes, and that agreement is informative in a way that re-deriving the same formula twice is not.

And when they disagree, you have learned something you could not have learned any other way: one of them is wrong, and you now have to find out which. That question — not the agreement — is where most of the value is.

There is a practical corollary that shapes the whole week. A simulator earns its keep exactly to the extent that it is not a translation of the formula into code. If you were to write a "simulator" that computed $d'$ and returned $\Phi(d'/2)$, it would agree perfectly and tell you nothing. So the rule for this week is: the simulator must go through the counts. Draw Poisson numbers, form a count matrix, run a classifier on it, tally the mistakes. Nowhere in simulate.py does the word $\Phi$ appear, and that is deliberate.

Three things the simulator has already caught, in projects like this one
  • An off-by-one in the decision rule. Ties at $\mathrm{LLR} = 0$ assigned to the wrong class shift accuracy by a percent or two at low depth, where the count distribution has real mass at the tie. The formula cannot see this because it treats the score as continuous; the simulator sees it immediately.
  • A convention mismatch. A theory curve computed at fixed per-gene marker mass compared against a simulation at fixed total mass — the Week 5 pitfall — which produces a clean, believable, entirely wrong disagreement by a factor of $\sqrt{k}$.
  • A genuine limitation of the theory. At depth 250 with a single gene, the Gaussian approximation is optimistic by about half a percentage point. That is not a bug in either implementation; it is the approximation doing what Week 4 said it would, and the simulator is how you find out that it matters.

Two of those three are bugs and one is physics. Telling them apart is the skill this week develops.

2  The generative model, in three assumptions

Everything the simulator does is contained in three sentences. Write them in your notebook; they are the model that Weeks 9 through 11 exist to break.

The generative model
  1. A cell type is a probability vector over genes. Type $c$ is a vector $p^{(c)}$, where $p^{(c)}_g$ is the probability that any given transcript in a cell of that type belongs to gene $g$. Every cell of type $c$ has the same vector.
  2. Counts are independent Poisson at depth times probability. Sequencing a cell of type $c$ to depth $D$ gives $x_g \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(D\,p^{(c)}_g)$, independently for each gene $g$.
  3. Dropout thins independently. Each captured molecule is lost, independently of every other, with probability $q$.

Now the honest part. Each of those is false about real cells, in a specific way, and this project measures the failure of two of them.

AssumptionHow it is false about real cellsWhich week attacks it
1. One vector per type; all cells of a type identical Cells of the "same type" differ from one another — cell cycle stage, activation state, size, ambient contamination. The vector varies from cell to cell, which adds variance on top of the counting noise. Week 11. The extra variance shows up as a Fano factor above 1 and is modelled as a negative binomial, $\mathrm{Var} = m + \varphi m^2$. This is the project's headline negative result.
2. Independent Poisson counts Genes are co-regulated, so counts are correlated across genes within a cell; and the total library size varies per cell, which correlates everything. Independence is what quadrature needs. Week 5's exercise 5.6 quantifies the cost (a correlation of $\rho$ caps $d'$ at $d'_1/\sqrt\rho$); Week 10 sees it on real marker panels; Week 14 names it in the limitations.
3. Independent, gene-blind dropout Real dropout is not gene-blind: lowly expressed genes are lost preferentially, and capture efficiency varies between droplets. A single global $q$ is a caricature. Week 9 proves that gene-blind dropout is exactly reduced depth (so it cannot break the law), and then finds where the low-depth regime breaks $\Phi(d'/2)$ anyway.
Why write down a model you already know is wrong

Because "wrong" is not a useful category for a model; "wrong in a way I can measure" is. A model that is stated precisely enough to be simulated is stated precisely enough to be falsified, and each of the three rows above turns into a specific experiment later in this project.

The alternative — using a black-box simulator, or a real dataset, without ever writing the assumptions down — does not make the assumptions go away. It only makes them impossible to test, and it guarantees that when the answer looks strange you will not know which belief to revise. This is why simulate.py's module docstring lists all three assumptions before a single function is defined, and why the first thing you should do this week is read that docstring.

3  The marker_mass convention

Here is a small piece of bookkeeping that causes more confusion than anything else in the simulator, and that has a genuinely interesting reason behind it.

You want two cell types that differ by a fold change $f$ on each of $k$ marker genes, and you want to control how much of the transcriptome those markers account for — call that total the marker mass $m$. The obvious thing to write is "each type puts mass $m$ on the marker set". But look at what that demands: if both types spend exactly $m$ across $k$ genes, and the genes are symmetric, then both types put $m/k$ on each gene — and then they are identical, and the fold change is 1.

The constraint, stated plainly

Two types cannot each carry exactly marker_mass on the marker set and differ by a fold change on every marker in it. The two requirements contradict each other. Something has to give, and the convention you choose is a decision you must make explicitly and then never quietly change.

marker_profiles resolves it by pinning the average: the two types put an average of marker_mass on the marker set. Per gene, writing $\text{base} = m/k$ for the average rate and $a, b$ for the two types' rates on one marker:

Derivation · the two rates proved (this is exercise 7.1)

Two conditions, two unknowns:

$$\frac{a}{b} = f \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{a+b}{2} = \text{base}.$$

Substitute $a = fb$ into the second: $\,(fb + b)/2 = \text{base}$, so $b(f+1) = 2\,\text{base}$, giving

$$\boxed{\;b = \frac{2\,\text{base}}{1+f}, \qquad a = \frac{2\,\text{base}\,f}{1+f}\;}$$

Check the two conditions: $a/b = f$ immediately, and $a + b = 2\,\text{base}(f+1)/(1+f) = 2\,\text{base}$. Also check a limit: at $f = 1$ both become $\text{base}$, the types coincide, and $d' = 0$ — which is what test_marker_profiles_fold_change_one_is_indistinguishable asserts.

The signal per gene is

$$\delta = a - b = 2\,\text{base}\,\frac{f-1}{f+1},$$

a formula you have already met: it is the expression from Week 5, Section 7, and the factor $2(f-1)/(f+1)$ is what turns a fold change into a usable signal. Note how slowly it grows: $f = 1.5$ gives $0.40$, $f = 2$ gives $0.67$, $f = 5$ gives $1.33$, and $f = \infty$ gives only $2$. A gene that is on in one type and completely off in the other is worth just five times a 1.5-fold gene, not infinitely more.

Two consequences to notice, both of which are load-bearing later.

The convention keeps $d'$ symmetric in the two types. Because $\bar\lambda_g = D\,\text{base}$ exactly, neither type is privileged: swap the labels and every number is unchanged. Pin type 1's mass instead and the pooled rate becomes type-dependent, so $d'$ acquires a spurious asymmetry. Exercise 7.2 works out what the alternatives actually do.

The leftover mass is absorbed by the genes the simulation does not model. The two profiles sum to $m\,f\cdot 2/(1+f)$ and $m\cdot 2/(1+f)$, which are not equal — type 1 spends more of its transcriptome on markers. In a real cell that difference would be made up elsewhere, across the eighteen thousand genes we are not simulating. This is a real simplification and it belongs in the limitations section: the simulator models a marker panel, not a transcriptome.

The mass argument that trips everyone

marker_profiles(n_genes, fold_change, marker_mass) takes the mass of the whole panel, not of one gene. So marker_profiles(16, 1.5, 0.02) spreads 2% of the transcriptome over sixteen genes ($\text{base} = 0.00125$ each), while marker_profiles(16, 1.5, 0.02*16) gives each gene 2%.

These are the two conventions of Week 5's Lab 2, and they answer different questions: the first holds the biological difference fixed and re-describes it with more genes (no gain, $d'$ independent of $k$); the second claims the types differ in more places (genuine gain, $d' \propto \sqrt{k}$). week07_simulator.py uses the second — it passes MASS_PER_GENE * k — precisely so that the $\sqrt{k}$ law is visible in the figure. If your $k$ curves lie on top of each other, this is why.

4  Lab: the simulator, live

Before writing any Python, get the picture. This lab is the whole simulator in miniature: two profiles, Poisson counts, the log-likelihood ratio, and the tally.

Lab 1 · Cells in, decisions out
Two thousand cells of each type are drawn afresh whenever you move a slider. One gene shows the two count distributions for a single marker — the view you would get from looking at one column of the matrix. All genes shows the distribution of the log-likelihood ratio, which is the number the classifier actually thresholds at zero. Watch how much better separated the second view is, and check the measured accuracy against $\Phi(d'/2)$ in the readout.

What to look for, in this order:

  • At $k = 1$, the two count histograms overlap heavily and the measured accuracy is barely above chance. Nothing about that view suggests the problem is solvable.
  • Switch to the LLR view and raise $k$. The two LLR distributions pull apart as $\sqrt{k}$ — the same evidence, viewed along the one direction in count space that contains all of it.
  • The measured and predicted accuracies should agree to about $\pm 0.01$ everywhere (2000 cells per type gives a standard error near $0.008$). If you find a corner where they disagree by much more than that, you have found something: note the settings before you move the sliders.

5  What the simulated data actually looks like

Running week07_simulator.py produces figures/week07_cells.png: a heatmap of 30 cells of each type over 20 marker genes at depth 3000, and beside it a scatter of two of those genes. Look at that figure for longer than feels necessary, because the intuitions it corrects are the ones that cause trouble in Week 10.

Three properties of a counts matrix observed

The entries are small integers. At depth 3000 with 20 markers carrying $0.004$ each, the expected counts are $\lambda_1 = 14.4$ and $\lambda_2 = 9.6$, with standard deviations $3.8$ and $3.1$. So the matrix is full of numbers like 8, 12, 15, 11 — and the relative noise on a count of 10 is $1/\sqrt{10} = 32\%$. There is no smooth "expression level" anywhere in the data; there are tallies.

At low depth it is mostly zeros. Drop the depth to 250 and the expected counts become $1.2$ and $0.8$. Then $P(x = 0) = e^{-1.2} = 0.30$ for type 1 and $e^{-0.8} = 0.45$ for type 2: about 38% of the entries in the matrix are zero, for no reason other than Poisson sampling. Nothing was "dropped out". This is worth internalising before Week 9, where you will meet people who assume every zero is a technical failure.

Real matrices are more extreme still. A real 10x matrix has ~20,000 genes and ~3,000 counts per cell, so the average gene has $\lambda = 0.15$ and over 90% of the entries are zero. The simulator does not reproduce that, because it models only the marker panel — every gene in it is a well-expressed marker. Say so when you present the figure; it is exactly the sort of thing a good question in Week 15 will go after.

Now the scatter, which is the part that surprises people.

Two genes look hopeless. Twenty genes are nearly perfect.

Same parameters: 20 markers, $f = 1.5$, depth 3000. Per gene, $\delta = 4.8$ and $\bar\lambda = 12$, so

$$d'_g = \frac{4.8}{\sqrt{12}} = 1.386.$$

Plot gene 1 against gene 2 and you see two clouds whose centres are at $(14.4, 14.4)$ and $(9.6, 9.6)$, each about 3.5 units wide in every direction. They visibly overlap. And yet, in quadrature, those two genes alone give $d' = \sqrt2 \times 1.386 = 1.96$ and an accuracy of $\Phi(0.98) = 84\%$ — already far better than the picture suggests.

All twenty give $d' = \sqrt{20}\times 1.386 = 6.20$, hence $\Phi(3.10) = 99.9\%$. The scatter has not changed. Your eye has simply never been able to see a 20-dimensional diagonal.

This is the single most useful piece of intuition in the whole simulation week: overlap in a low-dimensional projection tells you almost nothing about separability in the full space. Evidence that is invisible one gene at a time accumulates along a direction no two-dimensional plot can display. Keep it for Week 10, when you will look at a real PBMC scatter and be tempted to conclude that the cell types are inseparable.

6  The first real comparison

Now the week's actual result. Sweep the depth over a factor of 64, at $k = 1, 4$ and $16$ genes; at each setting simulate 5,000 cells of each type, classify every one of them with the exact log-likelihood ratio, and count the mistakes. Then, on the same axes, draw $\Phi(d'/2)$ computed from the true rates.

The theory has no fitted parameters. There is no scaling constant, no offset, nothing tuned. The lines are drawn before the points are measured, and either they pass through them or they do not.

Read that table along its diagonals. The $k=1$ column at depth 4000, the $k=4$ column at depth 1000, and the $k=16$ column at depth 250 are all $0.788$ — because $d' \propto \sqrt{Dk}$ and $4000\times1 = 1000\times4 = 250\times16$. Depth and gene count are interchangeable at the same product. That is the Week 5 symmetry, visible as a fact about a figure, and it is a good thing to point at when you show it to someone.

This is a WIN week: the figure is the thing you can hand to a person who has not read any of this and say "the line is a prediction made from first principles, the dots are what happened, and nothing was adjusted to make them meet."

What agreement licenses. If the points sit on the lines to within the sampling error of the measurement — with 5,000 cells per type per point the standard error of a proportion is at most $\sqrt{0.25/10000} = 0.005$ — then you have earned the right to do something you will do constantly for the next five weeks: draw the theory curve as a reference line on every subsequent plot. When Week 9 adds dropout, or Week 10 uses real data, the theory line is no longer a hypothesis under test at every point; it is a calibrated ruler, and any deviation from it is attributable to the new ingredient rather than to a mistake in the machinery. You cannot borrow that authority. You have to earn it once, here, with the simplest possible data.

What disagreement would have meant. This is worth writing down before you run the script, so that you are not deciding what counts as a good outcome after you have seen one. There are exactly three possibilities, and they can be told apart:

  1. The simulator does not implement the model. A shape error, a dropout applied twice, a label mismatch. Diagnosed with unit tests on the pieces — does simulate_cells produce a Fano factor of 1? does the mean equal $D p$? — which is exactly what test_simulate.py checks before it checks any accuracy.
  2. The theory has an algebra error. Diagnosed by comparing against bayes_error_exact, which is a completely separate route to the same number for the one-gene case and uses no approximation at all. If the simulation matches the exact Bayes error but not $\Phi(d'/2)$, the algebra between them is where to look.
  3. The theory is right and its approximation is failing. Diagnosed the same way, but with the opposite outcome: the simulation matches bayes_error_exact, and that differs from $\Phi(d'/2)$. Then nothing is broken — you have measured the Gaussian approximation's error, which is a result, not a bug. Expect this at the low-depth, small-$k$ corner: at $D = 250$ with $k = 1$ the exact answer is $0.5741$ while $\Phi(d'/2)$ says $0.5793$, a systematic gap of $0.005$ that is right at the edge of what 5,000 cells can resolve.

Notice that all three diagnoses are available in advance, and that two of the three are settled by a function you wrote in Week 4 for exactly this purpose. That is what it looks like when a project has been built in the right order.

8  Two ways to ruin this week

The label-order bug, which raises no error and destroys everything

In simulate_dataset you build the matrix $X$ by stacking blocks of cells, and the label vector $y$ by concatenating blocks of labels. If those two get built in different orders — or if you later shuffle the rows and the labels independently, which is the classic version, usually written while trying to randomise a train/test split — then every row is paired with somebody else's label.

Nothing raises an exception. The shapes still match. The matrix is still full of perfectly good Poisson counts. Every test that checks a mean or a variance still passes. The only symptom is that your accuracy is 0.5 at every depth and every $k$, forever, and since a broken classifier also gives 0.5, you can spend two days debugging the classifier.

How to check, in ninety seconds. Build a tiny dataset in which the label is obviously recoverable from the row, and demand near-perfect accuracy:

  • p1, p2 = marker_profiles(4, fold_change=50.0, marker_mass=0.02) — that is $\text{base} = 0.005$, so $a = 0.00980$ and $b = 0.000196$.
  • X, y = simulate_dataset([p1, p2], depth=5000.0, n_cells_per_type=50, rng=rng) — expected counts of $49.0$ against $0.98$, a $d'$ per gene of $9.6$ and a total of $19.2$.
  • Now X[y == 0].mean() must be about fifty times X[y == 1].mean(), and any classifier must score essentially 1.000.

If that returns 0.5, the labels are scrambled and no amount of staring at the classifier will help. Keep this four-line check somewhere you can paste it; you will want it again in Week 8 (train/test splits), Week 9 (five types), and Week 10 (real data, where the labels come from a file and the rows from another).

Do not tune until it agrees

Suppose the points miss the line. The temptation — and it is a strong one, at eleven at night, with a check-in tomorrow — is to change something: nudge the marker mass, adjust the fold change, drop the awkward low-depth point, switch the pooled variance to the type-1 variance because "that looks closer".

Every one of those moves converts a measurement into a decoration. And they work: with two or three free knobs you can make almost any curve pass through almost any points, and you will never find out that your simulator applied dropout twice.

The rule: when simulation and theory disagree, do not adjust parameters until they agree. Find out which one is wrong. Use the three-way diagnosis of Section 6. The tools are already written — bayes_error_exact settles it for one gene, and the unit tests settle it for the simulator's pieces.

And if you genuinely cannot resolve it: report the disagreement. A figure with a visible, honestly labelled gap and a paragraph explaining what has been ruled out is a far stronger piece of work than a clean figure obtained by fiddling. Week 9's milestone is worded to allow exactly this: "simulation matches theory, or the disagreement is characterized."

Stars mark difficulty: ★ routine · ★★ requires an idea · ★★★ challenge. Write full sentences; every claim needs a reason. Solutions are provided — attempt first, then compare.

Exercise 7.1★
Derive $a = 2\,\text{base}\,f/(1+f)$ and $b = 2\,\text{base}/(1+f)$ from the two conditions $a/b = f$ and $(a+b)/2 = \text{base}$. Then evaluate them for the simulator's defaults: $\text{base} = 0.004$ and $f = 1.5$. Give $\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$, $\delta$, $\bar\lambda$ and $d'_g$ at depth 1000, and confirm $d'_g$ against the shortcut $\sqrt{D\,\text{base}}\cdot 2(f-1)/(f+1)$.
Show solution

From $a = fb$: $(fb+b)/2 = \text{base} \Rightarrow b(f+1) = 2\,\text{base} \Rightarrow b = 2\,\text{base}/(1+f)$, and $a = fb = 2\,\text{base}\,f/(1+f)$.

With $\text{base} = 0.004$, $f = 1.5$: $b = 0.008/2.5 = 0.0032$ and $a = 0.012/2.5 = 0.0048$. Check: $a/b = 1.5$ ✓, $(a+b)/2 = 0.004$ ✓.

At $D = 1000$: $\lambda_1 = 4.8$, $\lambda_2 = 3.2$, $\delta = 1.6$, $\bar\lambda = 4.0$, so $$d'_g = \frac{1.6}{\sqrt{4}} = 0.80.$$

Shortcut: $\sqrt{1000 \times 0.004}\times 2(0.5)/2.5 = \sqrt{4}\times 0.4 = 0.80$ ✓.

Keep the number $d'_g = 0.0253\sqrt{D}$ for these parameters; it lets you predict every point of this week's figure in your head, which is exactly the state you want to be in before running the script rather than after.

Exercise 7.2★
Two alternatives to the average-mass convention. (a) Pin type 1: let type 1 spend exactly $m$ on the markers and type 2 spend $m/f$. (b) Pin the geometric mean: $\sqrt{ab} = \text{base}$. For each, give $a$, $b$, $\delta$ and $\bar\lambda$, and say what changes about $d'$. Which is symmetric under swapping the two type labels?
Show solution

(a) Pin type 1. $a = \text{base}$, $b = \text{base}/f$. Then $\delta = \text{base}(1 - 1/f)$ and $\bar\lambda = D\,\text{base}(1 + 1/f)/2$, so $$d'_g = \sqrt{D\,\text{base}}\;\frac{1 - 1/f}{\sqrt{(1+1/f)/2}}.$$ At $f = 1.5$, $\text{base} = 0.004$, $D = 1000$: $a = 0.004$, $b = 0.002667$, $\delta = 0.001333$, $\bar\lambda = 3.333$, $d'_g = 1.3333/1.826 = 0.730$ — about 9% smaller than the 0.80 of the average convention, because the pooled rate has been pulled up towards type 1.

(b) Geometric mean. $a = \text{base}\sqrt f$, $b = \text{base}/\sqrt f$, so $\delta = \text{base}(\sqrt f - 1/\sqrt f)$ and $\bar\lambda = D\,\text{base}(\sqrt f + 1/\sqrt f)/2$. At the same numbers: $a = 0.004899$, $b = 0.003266$, $\delta = 0.001633$, $\bar\lambda = 4.082$, $d'_g = 1.633/2.020 = 0.808$ — within about 1% of the arithmetic convention at this fold change.

Symmetry. Conventions (a) fails: swapping the labels turns "type 1 has mass $m$" into "type 2 has mass $m$" and gives different rates. Both the arithmetic-average convention and the geometric one are symmetric — swapping the labels swaps $a$ and $b$ and leaves $\delta$, $\bar\lambda$ and $d'$ alone.

The lesson is not that one convention is correct. It is that at $f = 1.5$ the three differ by under 10%, and at $f = 10$ they differ by a lot — so a $d'$ quoted without its convention is not a number anyone else can reproduce. Put the convention in the figure caption, every time.

Exercise 7.4★★
(a) Explain why the label-order bug produces exactly 0.5 accuracy rather than something random. (b) Design a four-line check that catches it, and say what makes a good check here as opposed to a bad one. (c) A friend suggests instead asserting that accuracy is above 0.5 on the real dataset. Why is that a much weaker test?
Show solution

(a) If the labels are permuted independently of the rows, then the label attached to a row carries no information about that row: for a balanced two-class dataset, the probability that a randomly attached label matches the row's true class is exactly $1/2$, whatever the classifier does. The classifier may be working perfectly — it is predicting the true class of each row correctly — but it is being scored against labels drawn independently of the truth, so the score converges to 0.5. That is why the symptom is so precisely uninformative.

(b) Use a dataset where the answer is not in doubt: marker_profiles(4, 50.0, 0.02) at depth 5000 gives expected counts of $49.0$ against $0.98$ per gene, a total $d'$ of $19.2$, and a true accuracy indistinguishable from 1. Assert accuracy $> 0.99$. What makes it a good check is that the expected and failure values are far apart (1.0 versus 0.5), so the test cannot pass by luck and cannot fail by noise. A bad check compares against a value the bug could also produce.

(c) Because at any reasonable setting the true accuracy is well above 0.5 anyway, so "accuracy $> 0.5$" passes with a partially broken pipeline, a mislabelled subset, or a classifier that is only using one of sixteen genes. A test whose passing condition is much weaker than the correct behaviour will not fail when the code degrades — and silent degradation is precisely the failure mode you are guarding against.

The general principle is worth a line in the notebook: a test is only as good as the distance between "correct" and "broken" in the quantity it measures. This is the reasoning behind almost every tolerance in tests/, and it is worth re-reading a few of them with this in mind.

Exercise 7.5★★
A real 10x matrix has about 20,000 genes and about 3,000 counts per cell, and is typically over 90% zeros. (a) Under the pure Poisson model with uniform expression, what fraction of entries would be zero? (b) Real expression is highly skewed — a few hundred genes carry most of the mass. Does that push the zero fraction up or down? (c) Someone tells you the zeros are "dropout", a technical failure that must be corrected before analysis. What is your reply?
Show solution

(a) If all 20,000 genes were equally expressed, each would have $\lambda = 3000/20000 = 0.15$, and $P(x = 0) = e^{-0.15} = 0.861$. So 86% zeros from counting statistics alone, with no technical failure of any kind.

(b) Up. Skew moves mass onto a few genes, so the great majority of genes have $\lambda$ far below 0.15 and are essentially always zero, while the few high ones are almost never zero. Since $e^{-\lambda}$ is convex, redistributing a fixed total $\sum_g \lambda_g$ unequally increases $\sum_g e^{-\lambda_g}$ — the average zero probability rises. A realistic skew takes you from 86% to well over 90%, which is the observed figure.

(c) That most of them are not a failure at all: they are what a small random sample looks like. You did not fail to detect the transcript; you sampled 3,000 molecules out of hundreds of thousands and this gene's molecules were not among them. There are technical losses on top of that — capture efficiency is well under 100% — but by the thinning theorem of Week 2 those are mathematically equivalent to having sequenced at a lower depth, and they produce Poisson counts too. The claim that requires evidence is that there are more zeros than Poisson sampling predicts, and for UMI-based data that claim has largely not survived scrutiny. cited (Svensson 2020, Droplet scRNA-seq is not zero-inflated.)

This is one of the most useful things you will be able to say in a conversation with a working biologist, and it descends directly from Week 1's first sentence: the output of a sequencer is a sample. An entire subfield of "imputation" methods exists to fill in zeros that, for the most part, were never missing.
