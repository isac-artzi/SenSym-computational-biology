Dropout, and Where the Law Bends

Dropout is a relabelling of the depth axis, proved · the collapse test · and the low-depth regime where $\Phi(d'/2)$ fails while the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law does not

Objectives

  • Say what dropout is biologically, and quote the rough range of molecular capture efficiency for droplet protocols with the caveat that the number is protocol-dependent and contested.
  • Prove, from Week 2's thinning theorem, that dropout at rate $q$ applied to data of depth $D$ gives exactly the distribution of depth $D(1-q)$ — and name the assumption that makes it work.
  • Explain why that assumption is the suspicious one, in terms of what real dropout actually depends on.
  • Run the collapse test: accuracy against nominal depth for several $q$, then against $D(1-q)$, and show the curves superimposing.
  • Distinguish sharply between "the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law fails" (it does not, in this model) and "the accuracy formula $\Phi(d'/2)$ fails" (it does, at low depth), and say which one the low-depth residual is evidence for.
  • Extend to more than two cell types with multi_type_profiles and posterior_log_odds, and state what changes and what does not.
  • Implement apply_dropout, get tests/test_simulate.py and all earlier tests green, produce the M3 figures and table with SEED 109, and either pass the M3 gate or state precisely why you did not.

1  What dropout is, before it is mathematics

Between a messenger RNA molecule sitting in a cell and a count appearing in your matrix, a lot has to go right. The cell has to be captured in a droplet with a barcoded bead. The molecule has to bind an oligo-dT primer. Reverse transcriptase has to convert it to cDNA without falling off. The cDNA has to be amplified without being lost in the reaction, and the resulting fragment has to be sequenced deeply enough to be seen at all.

Each of those steps has an efficiency below 1, and they multiply.

Capture efficiency, and how uncertain it is cited

The fraction of a cell's mRNA molecules that end up as counted UMIs — the molecular capture efficiency — is usually quoted for droplet protocols as somewhere in the range of roughly 10% to 30%. So a cell containing 300,000 transcripts contributes something like 30,000–90,000 counted molecules, and the "depth" you nominally paid for is not the depth you effectively have.

Treat that range with suspicion, and say so out loud when you present it. The number is protocol-dependent (Smart-seq-style plate protocols are usually credited with higher efficiency than droplet ones), it is estimate-dependent (most measurements come from spiked-in synthetic RNA of known concentration, which may not behave like cellular mRNA), it is version-dependent within a single vendor's chemistry, and published values for nominally the same platform differ by more than a factor of two. See Svensson et al. (2017), Nature Methods, and Zheng et al. (2017), Nature Communications, in Section 12.

This is the first place in the project where you have to cite a number you cannot derive. Label it cited, give the range rather than a point value, and — the important part — design the analysis so that nothing depends on which value inside the range is right. Section 2 is what makes that possible.

The modelling question is what dropout does to the counts. And the answer, which is the reason this chapter exists, is: nothing you have not already studied.

2  Dropout is a relabelling of the horizontal axis

Theorem · dropout equals reduced depth  proved

Let a cell of type $c$ be sequenced to depth $D$, so that its count for gene $g$ is $X_g \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(D p_g^{(c)})$, independently across genes. Suppose each captured molecule is then independently lost with probability $q$, and let $Y_g$ be the surviving count. Then

$$Y_g \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\big(D(1-q)\,p_g^{(c)}\big) \quad\text{independently across genes.}$$

That is exactly the distribution of a dropout-free experiment run at depth $D_{\text{eff}} = D(1-q)$. No experiment, no statistic, no classifier can tell the two apart, because they are the same probability distribution.

Proof · it is the thinning theorem, applied once per gene

Week 2 proved: if $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ and each of the $X$ items survives independently with probability $s$, then the number of survivors is exactly $\mathrm{Poisson}(s\lambda)$. (Recall the shape of the argument: condition on $X = n$, so the survivors are $\mathrm{Binomial}(n, s)$; sum over $n$; the $n!$ in the Poisson pmf cancels the $n!$ in the binomial coefficient; a change of variable $j = n-m$ turns what is left into the Taylor series for $e^{\lambda(1-s)}$.)

Apply that with $\lambda = D p_g^{(c)}$ and survival probability $s = 1-q$: $$Y_g \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\big((1-q)\,D\,p_g^{(c)}\big) = \mathrm{Poisson}\big(D_{\text{eff}}\,p_g^{(c)}\big), \qquad D_{\text{eff}} = D(1-q).$$

Independence across genes survives too: the counts $X_1,\dots,X_k$ were independent to begin with, and each is thinned using its own coin flips, so the survivor counts are functions of disjoint collections of independent random variables and are therefore independent. ∎

Assumptions used: (i) the counts are Poisson; (ii) each molecule is lost independently of every other molecule; (iii) the loss probability $q$ is the same for every molecule — in particular it does not depend on which gene the molecule belongs to, on how highly that gene is expressed, or on the cell.

Three consequences follow immediately, and they are the reason this theorem is worth a whole chapter rather than a footnote.

What the theorem buys you
  • Dropout is not a new phenomenon. It is the depth axis with different tick labels. Every result you have already derived — $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$, $d'\propto\sqrt k$, accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$ — carries over unchanged with $D$ replaced by $D(1-q)$. In particular $d'$ falls by a factor $\sqrt{1-q}$: 60% dropout costs you a factor $\sqrt{0.4} = 0.63$ in separation, not a factor 0.4.
  • You no longer need to know $q$ to test the theory. Section 1's honest admission that capture efficiency is somewhere between 10% and 30% would be fatal if dropout had its own separate effect. It does not. Whatever $q$ is, it moves you along a curve you have already predicted.
  • The bead experiment tests the same law. Week 12's physical protocol draws $n$ beads and then discards some by a die roll. That is thinning with the same theorem behind it, so the beads are not a separate analogy — they are the same mathematics with nothing simulated.
Now attack assumption (iii), because it is the weak one

Real dropout is not a single per-molecule coin with a gene-independent probability. At least three things break assumption (iii):

  • Gene length and sequence. Reverse transcription efficiency and priming depend on transcript length, GC content and secondary structure, so different genes are genuinely lost at different rates.
  • Expression level. Much of what the literature calls "dropout" — the excess of zeros in scRNA-seq — is simply what a Poisson at $\lambda = 0.3$ looks like, and needs no extra mechanism at all. Deciding how much is sampling and how much is a real capture failure is a live argument in the field, and the honest position is that the sampling part explains most of it. cited
  • Cell-to-cell variation. Droplets differ in bead quality, lysis and reaction volume, so $q$ varies from cell to cell. A per-cell $q$ that varies is not the same as a fixed average $q$, because the mixture of Poissons it produces is overdispersed.

That last point is the important one, and it is the bridge to Week 11: constant-$q$ dropout preserves the Fano factor at 1 (Week 2's corollary), while variable dropout inflates it above 1. So the failure of assumption (iii) does not show up as "the collapse is slightly off" — it shows up two weeks later as overdispersion, which is a different and much more damaging failure. Write the assumption in your notebook now so that in Week 11 you can point at the line it breaks.

3  The collapse test, and what it looks like as a figure

A theorem that says "these two things are the same distribution" makes a very specific graphical prediction, and it is one of the most satisfying kinds of figure in science: a collapse.

The protocol
  1. For each dropout level $q \in \{0, 0.3, 0.6\}$ and each nominal depth $D$, simulate cells, apply dropout, classify with the optimal rule at the effective rates, and measure the test accuracy.
  2. Plot accuracy against $D$. You get three separate curves, the higher-$q$ ones shifted down and to the right.
  3. Now plot the identical accuracies against $D(1-q)$ instead. If the theorem is right, the three curves land on top of one another, and on the no-dropout theory curve.
Nothing is fitted. There is no scaling parameter chosen to make it work: $1-q$ is known in advance because you chose it.

Do it in the lab first, then reproduce it with the experiment script.

Lab 1 · The collapse, with a switch for the horizontal axis
Blue: measured accuracy with dropout $q$, at each nominal depth. Grey: the same measurement with no dropout at all, as a reference. Orange: the theory curve $\Phi(d'/2)$, which knows nothing about dropout. Move the dropout slider and the blue curve falls away from the grey one. Then tick the box: the horizontal axis becomes the effective depth $D(1-q)$ and the blue points snap onto the grey curve and the orange line. Both axes are $\log_{10}$ of depth, since these plots are only readable on a log axis.

Push the dropout up to 0.8 and look again. The blue curve on the nominal axis has moved a long way — at $D = 1000$ you have lost about nine points of accuracy — and yet on the effective axis it is still sitting on the theory line, just further to the left along it. That is what "the same law" means. The classifier is not doing worse because dropout is a different kind of damage; it is doing worse because it is being handed a shallower experiment.

The mistake that makes the collapse look broken

When you classify dropped-out data, the rule must use the effective rates $D(1-q)p_g$, not the nominal ones $Dp_g$. Feed it $Dp_g$ and you have a classifier with the wrong intercept: $b = -\sum_g(\lambda_{1g}-\lambda_{2g})$ is off by a factor $1/(1-q)$, so the threshold sits in the wrong place and the accuracy drops for a reason that has nothing to do with dropout.

Look at how _measure in week09_dropout.py does it: it computes eff = depth * (1.0 - dropout) and passes eff * p1, eff * p2 into log_likelihood_ratio. In simulation you know $q$, so this is legitimate. On real data you would have to estimate the effective rates from the data itself, which is exactly Week 10's problem.

A related trap: apply_dropout(X, dropout, rng) takes the probability of loss, while downsample_counts(counts, keep_prob, rng) underneath it takes the probability of survival. The one-line implementation must invert. Getting it backwards produces a figure that looks completely reasonable and is wrong, which is the worst kind of bug — test_apply_dropout_never_increases_counts checks that the mean ratio is $1-q$ and not $q$.

4  The other failure, which is not the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law at all

This section is the single most valuable thing in the chapter, and it is a question you will be asked in the Week 15 Q&A, in roughly these words: "you said the square-root law breaks down at low depth — where exactly does it break?" The correct answer begins by rejecting the premise.

Two different claims, which students merge and must not
Claim A · the scaling lawClaim B · the accuracy formula
What it says $d' \propto \sqrt{D\,k}$ — separation grows as the square root of depth and of the number of genes. accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$ — the optimal rule's error probability is a Gaussian tail.
What it rests on Poisson counts (variance = mean) and independence across genes. Nothing else. All of that, plus the Gaussian approximation to the Poisson, plus pooling the two variances.
At low depth Exactly true. $d'$ is defined by means and variances, and Poisson means and variances are exact at every $\lambda$. Wrong, measurably. A Poisson with $\lambda \approx 1$ looks nothing like a bell curve.
Where it does fail Week 11: real counts are overdispersed, so $d'$ saturates and depth stops buying separation. Here, this week, at low depth — and the failure is in a known direction.

Why Claim A survives: $d'_g = |\lambda_{1g}-\lambda_{2g}|/\sqrt{\bar\lambda_g}$ is built from a difference of means and a variance. For a Poisson, $E[X] = \lambda$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda$ are exact identities — they are true at $\lambda = 0.03$ just as they are at $\lambda = 3000$. Substituting $\lambda_{cg} = D p_g^{(c)}$ gives $d'_g = \sqrt{D}\,|p^{(1)}_g - p^{(2)}_g|/\sqrt{\bar p_g}$, and the $\sqrt D$ is exact. Nothing in that chain of reasoning approximates anything.

Why Claim B does not survive: $\Phi(d'/2)$ came from replacing each Poisson by a normal distribution with the same mean and variance, and then integrating a Gaussian tail. At $\lambda = 15$ that substitution is excellent. At $\lambda = 0.3$ the Poisson puts 74% of its mass on the single value $0$ and the "distribution" is a spike, not a bell. A continuous symmetric curve cannot represent a spike at zero, and the error it makes is not random — it is systematic and it has a sign.

The exact answer, for the case this chapter needs  proved

Suppose all $k$ marker genes are identical: rate $\lambda_1$ per gene under type 1 and $\lambda_2$ per gene under type 2. Then the optimal rule depends on the counts only through the total $S = \sum_{g=1}^k x_g$, and

$$S \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(k\lambda_1) \ \text{ under type 1}, \qquad S \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(k\lambda_2)\ \text{ under type 2},$$

so the exact optimal accuracy for $k$ genes is the exact single-gene answer at the rates $k\lambda_1$ and $k\lambda_2$:

$$\text{accuracy}_{\text{exact}} = 1 - \tfrac12\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\min\!\big(P_{k\lambda_1}(s),\,P_{k\lambda_2}(s)\big).$$
Proof · equal weights, then superposition

Week 5's rule is $\mathrm{LLR}(x) = \sum_g x_g \log(\lambda_{1g}/\lambda_{2g}) - \sum_g(\lambda_{1g}-\lambda_{2g})$. With every gene identical, $\log(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)$ is the same constant $w$ for all $g$, so $$\mathrm{LLR}(x) = w\sum_g x_g - k(\lambda_1-\lambda_2) = wS - k(\lambda_1-\lambda_2),$$ which is a strictly increasing function of $S$ when $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2$ (so $w > 0$). A rule that thresholds a strictly increasing function of $S$ is a rule that thresholds $S$, so $S$ is all the classifier ever uses. (This is exercise 5.3(d) — the total count is sufficient exactly when every gene has the same fold change.)

And a sum of independent Poissons is Poisson with the sum of the rates: $S$ is $\mathrm{Poisson}(k\lambda_c)$ under type $c$. Substituting into Week 4's exact expression for the optimal error, $\varepsilon^\star = \tfrac12\sum_s\min(P_1(s),P_2(s))$, gives the statement. ∎

Assumptions used: all $k$ genes identical; equal priors; the rates known. The identical-genes condition is exactly what marker_profiles constructs, so this is not a special case invented for convenience — it is the case your simulator produces. For unequal genes there is no closed form, which is why week09_dropout.py reports the single-gene exact value as a diagnostic rather than trying to compute the general one.

So now you have three numbers for the same quantity — measured, Gaussian, exact — and disagreements between them mean different things:

  • measured vs exact: a check on your code. These must agree to within sampling error. If they do not, you have a bug, and the number of cells tells you how big a discrepancy is allowed.
  • exact vs Gaussian: a measurement of the approximation's error. It is not a bug in anything, it is a property of the formula, and it is the result this section exists to produce.
  • measured vs Gaussian: the sum of both, which is what week09_breakdown.png's residual panel plots. Only worth interpreting once the first comparison has ruled out code.

The direction has a clean explanation, and it is Week 4's: at low rates the decision is effectively "did I see any molecule at all?", and both Poissons place a large discrete lump of probability on exactly zero. A continuous bell curve cannot represent a lump, so it cannot exploit it, and it therefore undersells the achievable accuracy. The magnitude grows with fold change because that is when the two lumps at zero differ most.

Notice what this means for how you report the project. The one place your headline formula is provably wrong, it is wrong in the conservative direction: real classifiers do slightly better than $\Phi(d'/2)$ at low depth. A prediction that errs towards under-promising is a much more comfortable thing to defend than one that errs the other way, and it is worth one sentence in the report saying so.

The sentence you must never write

"At low depth the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law breaks down." It does not, in this model, and a reviewer who knows the material will stop listening at that point.

What breaks down is the Gaussian approximation used to convert $d'$ into an accuracy. The correct sentence is: "At low depth, $d'$ still grows as $\sqrt{D}$ exactly — it is built from Poisson means and variances, which are exact at every rate — but the mapping accuracy $=\Phi(d'/2)$ becomes inaccurate, by up to three points at large fold change, because the Poisson is no longer close to a Gaussian. We measured that error against the exact Bayes accuracy, and it is positive: the closed form is pessimistic."

Say it in exactly that order — what survives, what fails, how much, in which direction, measured how — and you have turned a weakness in the theory into a demonstration that you understand it.

5  More than two cell types

Everything so far has been a two-way decision, because that is where the theory is clean. Real annotation is a choice among many types, so it is worth knowing what changes.

Two provided functions do the work. multi_type_profiles(n_types, n_genes, fold_change, marker_mass, rng) builds $T$ profiles in which type $t$ over-expresses its own disjoint block of $n_{\text{genes}}/T$ markers and sits at baseline on everyone else's blocks, renormalised so that each type spends exactly marker_mass on markers. posterior_log_odds(X, rates, priors) then returns, for each cell, the normalised log posterior over all $T$ types:

$$\log P(t \mid x) \;\propto\; \sum_g x_g\log\lambda_{tg} \;-\; \sum_g \lambda_{tg} \;+\; \log\pi_t,$$

and you classify by taking the arg max over $t$. That expression is the same Poisson log-likelihood you derived in Week 5, simply not yet turned into a ratio — with two types, subtracting row 2 from row 1 gives back log_likelihood_ratio exactly.

What changes, and what does not

Changes:

  • Chance is $1/T$, not $1/2$. With five types, a coin-flipping classifier gets 20%, so "80% accuracy" means something quite different from what it means with two types. Always state the chance level on the axis or in the caption.
  • There are $\binom{T}{2}$ separation indices, not one. Every pair of types has its own $d'_{tu}$, and they are generally very different: in a PBMC panel, T cells and monocytes are far apart while CD4 and CD8 T cells are close.
  • The hardest pair dominates. Total error is roughly the union of the pairwise errors, so it is bounded below by the worst pair's error and above by the sum: $\max_{t\ne u}\varepsilon_{tu} \le \varepsilon \le \sum_{t \lt u}\varepsilon_{tu}$ (the upper bound is a union bound). When one pair is much harder than the rest — which is the usual case — both bounds are dominated by that pair, and the multi-class problem is really a two-class problem wearing a hat.

Does not change:

  • The $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law. Every pairwise $d'_{tu}$ scales as $\sqrt{D}$ for exactly the reason it did with two types, so the whole error curve moves with depth in the same way.
  • Dropout equals reduced depth. The thinning theorem is a statement about a single Poisson variable; it never knew how many hypotheses you were entertaining.
  • The rule is still linear in the counts. Each type's log posterior is linear in $x$, so the decision regions are intersections of half-spaces.

test_simulate_dataset_handles_more_than_two_types checks that the simulator produces five labelled types; extending the accuracy measurement to them is exercise 9.6, and it is optional for the milestone. Do it if the two-type work is finished and solid, not instead of it.

6  The M3 gate

Milestone 3 is not a presentation. It is a decision, and the whole point of writing the decision rule down in advance is that you do not get to negotiate with it afterwards.

M3 · the decision rule, stated before you look at the numbers

Run python experiments/week09_dropout.py at SEED 109 and open progress/week09_table.md. It has one row per (dropout, depth) pair, with the measured accuracy, the theory value at the effective depth, and their difference.

PASS if every row satisfies $|\text{measured} - \text{theory at } D_{\text{eff}}| \le 0.02$, and the typical row is well inside that. At 6,000 cells per type the sampling standard error of each accuracy is about $\sqrt{0.25/12000} \approx 0.005$, so a tolerance of 0.02 is roughly four standard errors: tight enough to catch a real disagreement, loose enough not to fire on noise. You should in fact find the worst row lands somewhere near 0.005–0.010.

If it passes: commit the table and the two figures, tag the repository simulator-validated, and go to Week 10 and real data.

If it fails — do NOT proceed to Week 10. Spend Week 10 finding out why. A simulator that disagrees with the theory it implements is either a broken simulator or a broken theory, and taking either one to real data means you will never be able to attribute a disagreement to anything. In order, check: the label convention (1 - y); the effective rates in the classifier (Section 3's pitfall); the keep/loss inversion in apply_dropout; whether the failing rows are all at the lowest depths, in which case you have rediscovered Section 4 and the fix is to compare against the exact accuracy rather than the Gaussian one. Consult your mentor and invoke the fallback ladder in plans/ (F1–F4), which specifies in advance how the project's scope contracts if a milestone slips, so that the decision is not made under time pressure in Week 13.

Notice the structure of that rule: a stated tolerance, a stated sample size that justifies the tolerance, a pass action, a fail action, and a named place where the fail action is written down. That is what a milestone is for. A milestone you can talk your way past is not a milestone.

8  Exercises

Stars mark difficulty: ★ routine · ★★ requires an idea · ★★★ challenge. Write full sentences; every claim needs a reason. Solutions are provided — attempt first, then compare.

Exercise 9.1★
An experiment is run at depth $D = 5000$ with a capture efficiency of 20% (i.e. dropout $q = 0.8$). (a) What is the effective depth? (b) By what factor does $d'$ fall? (c) If the no-dropout accuracy would have been $\Phi(1.6) = 94.5\%$, what is it with dropout? (d) What nominal depth would you have to buy to get back to 94.5%?
Show solution

(a) $D_{\text{eff}} = 5000 \times 0.2 = 1000$.

(b) $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$, so it falls by $\sqrt{0.2} = 0.447$ — a factor of about 2.2, not a factor of 5.

(c) The original $d'$ was $2\times1.6 = 3.2$; after dropout it is $3.2\times0.447 = 1.43$, so the accuracy is $\Phi(0.716) = 76.3\%$. Eighteen points lost.

(d) To restore $d' = 3.2$ at 20% efficiency you need $D_{\text{eff}} = 5000$, i.e. a nominal depth of $5000/0.2 = 25{,}000$ — five times the original, which is exactly the reciprocal of the efficiency and not a square-root relationship at all.

Part (d) is worth pausing on, because the two square roots cancel. Recovering a given $d'$ costs a factor $1/(1-q)$ in depth, flat. The square root appears in what dropout costs you, and disappears in what it costs to undo. Being able to switch between those two framings without confusing yourself is most of what "understanding a scaling law" means in practice.

Exercise 9.2★
A library is sequenced at half depth, and then 30% of the surviving molecules are lost to dropout. (a) What single thinning is that equivalent to? (b) Show in general that thinning by $s_1$ then by $s_2$ is thinning by $s_1 s_2$. (c) Explain why this means the order of the two operations does not matter.
Show solution

(a) Survival probabilities multiply: $0.5\times0.7 = 0.35$, so it is a single thinning with $s = 0.35$, i.e. an effective depth of $0.35D$ and a dropout-equivalent of $q = 0.65$.

(b) Apply the thinning theorem twice. Start with $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$; after the first thinning the survivors are $\mathrm{Poisson}(s_1\lambda)$ exactly, and this is a genuine Poisson variable, not an approximation, so the theorem's hypotheses are met a second time. Thinning it by $s_2$ gives $\mathrm{Poisson}(s_2 s_1 \lambda)$. The key point is that thinning maps the Poisson family into itself, which is what makes it composable at all.

(c) $s_1 s_2 = s_2 s_1$: multiplication of the survival probabilities is commutative, and the resulting distribution depends only on the product. So "downsample then drop out" and "drop out then downsample" produce identically distributed data — which is why Week 10's protocol of downsampling real data that has already been through dropout is legitimate.

Part (c) is not a technicality; it is the licence for the entire real-data arm of the project. The PBMC counts you download in Week 10 have already been thinned once, by the capture process, by an unknown amount. Composability says that downsampling them further just moves you along the same curve, so you can measure the shape of the depth dependence without ever knowing where on the axis you started.

Exercise 9.3★★
Prove the dropout-equals-reduced-depth theorem from scratch: state it precisely, prove it (you may quote the thinning theorem if you also state its assumptions), and then write the one-paragraph version you would say out loud to a biologist who has just told you that dropout is "a completely different problem from shallow sequencing".
Show solution

The statement and proof are Section 2. The essential content: counts are $\mathrm{Poisson}(Dp_g)$; each captured molecule independently survives dropout with probability $1-q$; thinning a Poisson gives a Poisson with the rate multiplied by the survival probability; therefore the observed counts are $\mathrm{Poisson}(D(1-q)p_g)$, which is the distribution of a clean experiment at depth $D(1-q)$. Assumptions: Poisson counts, independent per-molecule loss, and a loss probability that does not depend on the gene, the expression level or the cell.

Out loud: "Suppose you sequence a cell and capture 3,000 molecules, and then 40% of them are lost. What you are holding is 1,800 molecules drawn at random from that cell — and that is exactly what you would have been holding if the sequencer had captured 1,800 in the first place. The molecules do not know why they were left out. So dropout is not an extra source of noise on top of sampling noise; it is sampling noise, relabelled. The only way dropout becomes a genuinely different problem is if it is selective — if some genes or some cells lose molecules at higher rates than others — and that is a different claim, one we can test, and one that shows up as overdispersion rather than as a shift along the depth axis."

Practise that paragraph. It is the single most surprising claim in the project to a biologist audience, it takes forty seconds, and delivering it well is worth more in a poster session than any figure.

Exercise 9.4★★
Take $k = 8$ identical genes with per-gene rates $\lambda_1 = 0.096$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.064$ (that is, $D = 20$ at fold change 1.5 with per-gene mass 0.004). (a) Compute $d'_{\text{total}}$ and $\Phi(d'/2)$. (b) Using the sufficiency theorem of Section 4, write down the exact problem this reduces to. (c) Compute the exact accuracy by summing over $s = 0,1,2,3,4$ and argue why truncating there is safe. (d) Which is bigger, and why?
Show solution

So the whole 8-gene problem is a single-count problem at rates $0.768$ and $0.512$. (Sanity check: $d'$ of that single problem is $0.256/\sqrt{0.64} = 0.320$ — the same number, as it must be.)

Exercise 9.5★★
Suppose dropout is not constant: each cell has its own loss probability $q_i$, drawn from some distribution with mean $\bar q$ and variance $\tau^2 > 0$. (a) Is the count for a gene still Poisson? (b) Compute the mean and variance of the count, averaging over $q$. (c) What happens to the Fano factor? (d) Which of the two failures of Section 4 does this correspond to — and which week does it belong to?
Show solution

(a) No. Conditional on $q_i$ the count is $\mathrm{Poisson}(D(1-q_i)p)$, but a mixture of Poissons with different rates is not a Poisson. It is a compound distribution, and mixing always adds variance.

(b) Write $\mu_i = D(1-q_i)p$ and let $m = Dp$. By the law of total expectation, $E[X] = E[\mu_i] = m(1-\bar q)$. By the law of total variance, $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[\mathrm{Var}(X\mid q)] + \mathrm{Var}(E[X\mid q]) = E[\mu_i] + \mathrm{Var}(\mu_i) = m(1-\bar q) + m^2\tau^2,$$ since $\mathrm{Var}(\mu_i) = m^2\mathrm{Var}(1-q_i) = m^2\tau^2$.

(c) $F = \dfrac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{E[X]} = 1 + \dfrac{m\,\tau^2}{1-\bar q} > 1$. The data is overdispersed, and — note this carefully — the excess grows linearly with the mean count. Deeper sequencing makes the overdispersion worse, not better.

(d) Neither of Section 4's two failures. Section 4 was about the accuracy formula $\Phi(d'/2)$ being the wrong function of $d'$; this is about $d'$ itself being computed from the wrong variance, which breaks Claim A — the scaling law. The variance is now $m + \phi m^2$ in the notation of Week 11, so $d' = |\Delta m|/\sqrt{\bar m + \phi\bar m^2}$ saturates at large depth instead of growing as $\sqrt D$. This is the negative binomial, it is the depth ceiling, and it is Week 11's entire subject.

You have just derived the central result of Week 11 as a side effect of taking one of this week's assumptions seriously. That is the argument for writing assumptions down: the list is not bookkeeping, it is a list of experiments waiting to be done. Put this derivation in your notebook now and re-read it in two weeks.

Exercise 9.6★★★
Extend the measurement to five cell types. Use multi_type_profiles(5, 50, fold_change=3.0) and posterior_log_odds to classify by arg max, and measure accuracy against depth. (a) What is chance? (b) Compute all ten pairwise $d'$ values at some depth — what do you notice about them, given how multi_type_profiles is built? (c) Predict the accuracy curve using only the pairwise $d'$s, via the union bound, and compare with the measurement. (d) State one reason the union bound is loose here.
Show solution

(a) Chance is $1/5 = 20\%$.

(b) By construction every type over-expresses its own disjoint block by the same fold change and sits at baseline elsewhere, so all ten pairs are equivalent by symmetry and share a single value of $d'$. Each pair differs on exactly two blocks (mine, where I am high and you are low, and yours, where the reverse holds), so $d'_{tu}{}^2 = 2\times(\text{block size})\times d'_{\text{gene}}{}^2$. This makes the five-type problem an unusually clean test case and an unusually unrealistic one — the whole difficulty of real annotation is that the pairs are not equivalent.

(c) The union bound gives $\varepsilon \le \sum_{u\ne t}\varepsilon_{tu}$ for a fixed true type $t$: with symmetry, $\varepsilon \le 4\,\Phi(-d'/2)$, so the predicted accuracy is $\ge 1 - 4\Phi(-d'/2)$. At $d' = 3$, $\Phi(-1.5) = 0.0668$, so the bound predicts accuracy at least $1 - 0.267 = 73.3\%$, while the two-class accuracy at the same $d'$ would be $93.3\%$. The measurement should land above the bound — comfortably so — and below the two-class value.

(d) The bound double-counts: it adds the probability of losing to type $u$ and the probability of losing to type $v$ as though those were disjoint events, when in fact a cell with an unluckily low total count tends to look wrong against several types at once. The errors are positively correlated, so the true error is smaller than the sum. (A second, smaller reason: the pairwise error $\Phi(-d'/2)$ is itself the two-class optimal rule's error, and the multi-class arg-max rule is not the same rule restricted to a pair.)

Optional for M3 — do it only if the two-type work is complete. Its real value is Week 10: PBMC 3k has several types, most pairs of them easy and one or two hard, and part (b)'s symmetry is exactly the thing real data does not have. Knowing what the clean case looks like is what lets you see how messy the real one is.
