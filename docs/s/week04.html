Objectives

1  Where we are, and what is still missing

Last week you built the separation index. Two cell types express a gene at expected counts $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, and the amount by which they are distinguishable is

$$d' = \frac{|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda}}, \qquad \bar\lambda = \frac{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}{2},$$

which is a distance measured in units of the noise, and which grows like $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ because the Poisson standard deviation is $\sqrt{\lambda}$ and not $\lambda$.

That is a number, but it is not yet an answer. Nobody funds a sequencing run to raise a dimensionless index from 1.2 to 1.7. They want to know: if I sequence this deeply, what fraction of my cells will I label correctly? This week converts the index into that fraction, and the conversion turns out to be one of the tidiest results in applied probability:

$$\boxed{\ \text{accuracy} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{2}\right)\ }$$

There is no fitted parameter in that formula, no tuning constant, nothing you get to adjust after seeing the data. It is a prediction in the strong sense — it can be wrong, and in Week 9 and Week 11 you will find the two places where it is. Today's job is to derive it honestly, to know exactly what it assumes, and to build the yardstick that will later be used to convict it.

The setting, stated once

One gene. One cell. The cell is type 1 or type 2, each with probability $1/2$ (equal priors). You observe a single count $x$, which is $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_1)$ if the cell is type 1 and $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_2)$ if it is type 2. You know both rates. You must guess the type.

Throughout, take $\lambda_1 \gt \lambda_2$ — type 1 is the type that expresses the gene more strongly. Nothing is lost by this: if it is the other way round, swap the labels.

2  The decision rule, and where the threshold goes

A decision rule is nothing more than a list of which counts you will call type 1. Formally, choose a set $A$ of non-negative integers and say "type 1 if $x \in A$, type 2 otherwise". A rule is not an algorithm, not a program, not a model — it is a partition of the possible observations into two bins. There are $2^{\infty}$ of them, and exactly one family of them is best.

Your instinct is surely that $A$ should be a set of the form $\{x \ge t\}$: a big count means the strongly expressing type. That instinct is correct, but it is worth seeing why, because the argument is short, it is completely general, and it hands you the exact answer for free.

Theorem · the error-minimising rule proved

With equal priors, the rule that minimises the probability of a wrong answer is: call type 1 exactly when $P_1(x) \gt P_2(x)$, where $P_c(k)$ is the Poisson pmf under type $c$. (Ties may be broken either way; they contribute the same error.) Its error probability is $$\varepsilon^\star = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \min\big(P_1(k), P_2(k)\big).$$

Proof

Fix any rule $A$. Two ways to be wrong: the cell is type 1 and $x \notin A$, or the cell is type 2 and $x \in A$. Each type occurs with probability $\tfrac12$, so

$$\varepsilon(A) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k \notin A} P_1(k) \;+\; \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k \in A} P_2(k).$$

The first sum is over the complement of $A$, which is awkward. Replace it using $\sum_{k \notin A} P_1(k) = 1 - \sum_{k \in A} P_1(k)$:

$$\varepsilon(A) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k \in A}\big(P_2(k) - P_1(k)\big).$$

Now look at what you control. Every integer $k$ either is in $A$ or is not, and putting it in adds the single term $\tfrac12\big(P_2(k) - P_1(k)\big)$ to the error. To make the total as small as possible, include $k$ precisely when that term is negative — that is, when $P_1(k) \gt P_2(k)$. There is no interaction between the choices, since each count contributes its own separate term, so deciding each count on its own merits gives the global optimum.

With $A^\star = \{k : P_1(k) \gt P_2(k)\}$, the error becomes $$\varepsilon^\star = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k \in A^\star}\big(P_2(k) - P_1(k)\big) = \frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{k \in A^\star} P_2(k) + \sum_{k \notin A^\star} P_1(k)\right] = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k} \min\big(P_1(k), P_2(k)\big),$$ where the middle step used $1 = \sum_k P_1(k)$ again, and the last step is the observation that on $A^\star$ the smaller pmf is $P_2$ and off it the smaller pmf is $P_1$. ∎

Assumptions used: equal priors; both pmfs known exactly; the two errors cost the same. Nothing about Poisson, nothing about Gaussians. This argument is as true for beads in a jar as for transcripts in a cell.

So far the rule is "compare two probabilities". Now put the Poisson pmfs in and watch it collapse into a threshold.

Worked derivation · the optimal rule is a threshold, and here it is proved

Write out the comparison $P_1(k) \gt P_2(k)$ using $P_c(k) = e^{-\lambda_c}\lambda_c^k / k!$:

$$\frac{e^{-\lambda_1}\lambda_1^{k}}{k!} \;\gt\; \frac{e^{-\lambda_2}\lambda_2^{k}}{k!}.$$

The $k!$ cancels — it does not know which hypothesis it belongs to. (Remember that cancellation; in Week 5 it is the entire reason the multi-gene rule is linear.) Take logarithms of both sides, which is safe because both sides are positive and $\log$ is increasing:

$$-\lambda_1 + k\log\lambda_1 \;\gt\; -\lambda_2 + k\log\lambda_2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k\log\!\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} \;\gt\; \lambda_1 - \lambda_2.$$

Since $\lambda_1 \gt \lambda_2$ the logarithm is positive, so dividing does not flip the inequality:

$$k \;\gt\; k^\star \equiv \frac{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}{\log(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)}.$$

The optimal $A^\star$ is therefore everything above a single cut point: call type 1 if the count exceeds $k^\star$. Because counts are integers, the practical rule is "call type 1 if $x \ge \lceil k^\star \rceil$". ∎

The quantity $k^\star$ is called the logarithmic mean of $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, and it always lies strictly between them and strictly below their arithmetic mean $\bar\lambda$. Example: $\lambda_1 = 10.8$, $\lambda_2 = 9$ gives $k^\star = 1.8/\log(1.2) = 9.873$, against a midpoint of $9.9$. The gap is small here and it shrinks as the two rates get closer — which is exactly why the Gaussian derivation below, which puts the threshold at the midpoint, gets away with it.

3  The Gaussian approximation, and the formula

The threshold rule above is exact and it is also, in a sense, useless: it gives you a rule but not a number. To get an error probability in closed form you need to be able to integrate the Poisson, and you cannot. So you approximate it by something you can integrate.

The approximation everything rests on cited

For large $\lambda$, $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda) \approx \mathrm{Normal}(\lambda, \lambda)$ — same mean, same variance, in the sense that the probability of any interval agrees to a relative error that vanishes as $\lambda$ grows.

Why it is true, in one line: a $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ variable is the sum of $n$ independent $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda/n)$ variables for any $n$ (add the rates — Week 2), so it is a sum of arbitrarily many independent identically distributed pieces, and the central limit theorem applies. The mean and variance of the limit must be the mean and variance of the thing itself, both of which are $\lambda$.

When to trust it: the usual working rule is $\lambda \gtrsim 10$. Section 5 replaces that rule of thumb with a measurement.

Now the derivation. It is four short steps and you should be able to reproduce all four at a whiteboard by the end of the week; Week 5's milestone will ask you to.

Derivation · accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$ proved (given the approximation above)

Step 1 — replace both Poissons by normals. Under type 1 the count is approximately $\mathcal{N}(\lambda_1, \lambda_1)$; under type 2, $\mathcal{N}(\lambda_2, \lambda_2)$.

Step 2 — give them a common variance. Replace both variances by the pooled value $\bar\lambda = (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/2$. This is a genuine second approximation and deserves to be named: it is the equal-variance Gaussian model, and it is accurate when $|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2| \ll \bar\lambda$, because then $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are both close to $\bar\lambda$ anyway. Marker genes with a 20–50% fold change satisfy this comfortably; a gene that is ten times higher in one type does not, and you will see the consequence in Section 5.

Step 3 — standardise. Measure the count in units of the noise, from the midpoint: $$Z = \frac{X - \bar\lambda}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda}}.$$ This is a linear change of variable, so $Z$ is normal with unit variance under both hypotheses. Its mean under type 1 is $$\frac{\lambda_1 - \bar\lambda}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda}} = \frac{\lambda_1 - \frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}{2}}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda}} = \frac{(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)/2}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda}} = \frac{d'}{2},$$ and by the identical computation its mean under type 2 is $-d'/2$. So the whole problem has been reduced to one line: $$Z \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(+\tfrac{d'}{2},\, 1\right) \text{ under type 1}, \qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(-\tfrac{d'}{2},\, 1\right) \text{ under type 2}.$$ Two bell curves of width 1, separated by exactly $d'$, straddling zero. That picture is the entire content of the week; if you remember one image, remember that one.

Step 4 — find the threshold and integrate. By the theorem of Section 2 the optimal rule keeps the larger density. With $\varphi$ the standard normal density, $$\log\frac{\varphi(z - d'/2)}{\varphi(z + d'/2)} = -\frac{(z-d'/2)^2}{2} + \frac{(z+d'/2)^2}{2} = z\,d',$$ which is positive exactly when $z \gt 0$ (recall $d' \gt 0$). The optimal threshold is $z = 0$ — the midpoint, by symmetry, as it had to be. The accuracy is then the probability that a type-1 cell lands on the correct side: $$P(Z \gt 0 \mid \text{type 1}) = P\!\left(\mathcal{N}(d'/2, 1) \gt 0\right) = P\!\left(\mathcal{N}(0,1) \gt -\tfrac{d'}{2}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{2}\right).$$ By symmetry a type-2 cell is classified correctly with the same probability, so the overall accuracy — an average over two equally likely types — is $\Phi(d'/2)$ as well. ∎

Assumptions used, in order: (i) Poisson counts; (ii) $\lambda$ large enough for the normal approximation; (iii) the two variances close enough to pool; (iv) equal priors; (v) both rates known exactly; (vi) one gene. Assumption (vi) goes next week, (ii) and (iii) are measured in Section 5, (i) fails on real data in Week 11, and (v) is what Week 10 has to estimate rather than assume. Write this list in your notebook — it is the skeleton of your report's limitations section.

Definition · $\Phi$, and its relation to erf

$\Phi(z)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function: the probability that a $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ variable comes out below $z$, $$\Phi(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-u^2/2}\,du.$$ It has no expression in elementary functions, which is why it gets its own name. Software usually offers instead the error function $\mathrm{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^x e^{-s^2}ds$, and the two are the same object in different clothes: $$\Phi(z) = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right).$$ Exercise 4.2 asks you to derive that identity by substitution; it is four lines and you should do it once in your life. Two facts to carry around: $\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)$, and $\Phi$ is strictly increasing from 0 to 1.

You need a feel for $\Phi$ the way you have a feel for $\sin$. Learn the first four rows of this table; the rest you can reconstruct.

The last column is the one that will hurt. To get from 69% accuracy to 97.5% you must take $d'$ from 1 to 3.92 — a factor of about four — and since $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth}}$, that is sixteen times the sequencing. The formula is cheap; the biology it demands is not.

Three sanity checks, which you should run on every formula you ever derive
  • Chance level. $d' = 0$ means the two types have identical rates. Then $\Phi(0) = 0.5$: you are guessing. A formula that gave anything other than $1/2$ here would be indefensible, because with identical distributions no rule can beat a coin.
  • Saturation. As $d' \to \infty$, $\Phi(d'/2) \to 1$, and it approaches 1 from below and never reaches it. Correct: with Poisson counts, however far apart the rates are, a type-2 cell can always fluke a huge count. Perfect classification has probability zero of being achievable.
  • Monotonicity. $\Phi$ is increasing, so more separation is never worse. Also correct, and worth stating because it means the entire project can be phrased in $d'$ and translated to accuracy at the end.

The Python test test_accuracy_from_d_prime_endpoints_and_monotonicity checks all three. That is not a coincidence: a good test suite is a list of the sanity checks you would have done by hand.

5  The exact answer, and how good the approximation really is

Section 2 already gave you the exact error with no Gaussian anywhere in sight:

The yardstick proved (Section 2) $$\varepsilon^\star = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\min\big(P_1(k), P_2(k)\big), \qquad \text{accuracy}^\star = 1 - \varepsilon^\star.$$

At each possible count the optimal rule keeps the larger pmf, so the smaller pmf is precisely the probability mass it gets wrong; sum that over all counts and halve it for the equal priors. This is the best achievable accuracy for one gene — not the best achievable by a threshold rule, or by your favourite classifier, but by any rule whatsoever.

In principle the sum is infinite; in practice the Poisson tail dies faster than exponentially, so truncating at ten standard deviations past the larger mean leaves an error far below anything you would print. That is exactly what bayes_error_exact does, and it is why the function takes an optional max_k.

Now you have two numbers for the same quantity, one approximate and one exact, and comparing them is a small piece of research rather than a debugging exercise.

Result · where the Gaussian formula bends observed

Running experiments/week04_accuracy_roc.py sweeps $\lambda_2$ from $0.2$ to $300$ at a fixed fold change of $1.4$ and plots both answers. Three findings, and the third is the interesting one.

  • At high rate the two agree to three decimals. At $\lambda_2 = 200$, $\lambda_1 = 260$: $d' = 60/\sqrt{230} = 3.956$, so $\Phi(d'/2) = 0.9760$, while the exact sum gives $0.9763$. A difference of $0.0003$ — nothing you could measure without a million cells. The test test_accuracy_from_d_prime_matches_exact_bayes_at_high_rate asserts exactly this, with a tolerance of $0.01$.
  • At low rate and large fold change it visibly fails. At $\lambda_2 = 0.2$ against $\lambda_1 = 1.0$: $d' = 0.8/\sqrt{0.6} = 1.033$, so $\Phi(d'/2) = 0.697$, while the exact answer is $0.725$. The formula is pessimistic by about $0.03$. The test test_gaussian_approximation_degrades_at_low_rate asserts that the gap exceeds $0.02$ — a test that would fail if someone "fixed" the exact routine into agreement.
  • At low rate and small fold change it survives. Across the whole fold-1.4 sweep, including $\lambda_2 = 0.2$ where the expected count is a fifth of a molecule, the largest discrepancy is about $0.004$. This is more than the textbook rule of thumb ($\lambda \gtrsim 10$) would have led you to expect, and it is a result worth reporting.

So the honest statement is not "the Gaussian approximation needs $\lambda \gtrsim 10$". It is: the approximation degrades when the counts are small and the two rates differ by a large factor, because that is when pooling the two variances (Step 2) stops being defensible and the skewness of the Poisson stops cancelling between the two hypotheses.

One more feature of the comparison is worth naming, because a student who has not been warned will think their code is broken. The difference between the two curves does not decay smoothly: it wobbles, changing sign several times as $\lambda$ increases. That is not numerical noise. The exact answer is a sum of discrete lumps, and the optimal threshold can only sit between whole numbers, so as $\lambda$ grows continuously the exact accuracy moves in tiny steps while $\Phi(d'/2)$ glides. The wobble is discreteness, and it is real.

"My exact answer is worse than chance"

If bayes_error_exact ever returns more than $0.5$, you have summed the maximum instead of the minimum, or forgotten the factor of $\tfrac12$. The error of the optimal rule can never exceed $1/2$ — you could always ignore the data and flip a coin. Check the identity $\varepsilon^\star = \tfrac12$ when $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$ first; that single case catches most sign and factor errors, and test_bayes_error_exact_bounds checks it for you.

6  Sweeping the threshold: ROC curves and AUC

Everything so far has fixed one threshold and reported one accuracy. But a threshold is a choice, and different applications make it differently. If you are screening for a rare tumour cell you would rather flag a few extra normal cells than miss the one that matters; if you are building a reference atlas you might want the opposite. Reporting a single accuracy hides that choice. The ROC curve shows all of them at once.

Definition · ROC and AUC

Call type 1 the "positive" class. At a given threshold $t$ define $$\mathrm{TPR}(t) = P(\text{call positive} \mid \text{type 1}), \qquad \mathrm{FPR}(t) = P(\text{call positive} \mid \text{type 2}).$$ The ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic — the name is from 1940s radar) is the set of points $(\mathrm{FPR}(t), \mathrm{TPR}(t))$ as $t$ sweeps from $+\infty$ down to $-\infty$. It starts at $(0,0)$ (threshold so high nothing is called positive) and ends at $(1,1)$ (everything called positive).

The AUC is the area under that curve. A useless score gives the diagonal and $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.5$; a perfect score gives the corner and $\mathrm{AUC} = 1$.

The AUC has an interpretation that is far more useful than "area under a curve", and it is worth memorising in words:

What AUC means cited (Hanley & McNeil 1982; you prove the Gaussian case in exercise 4.5)

$\mathrm{AUC}$ is the probability that a randomly chosen positive scores higher than a randomly chosen negative, with ties counted as half: $$\mathrm{AUC} = P(X_1 \gt X_2) + \tfrac{1}{2}P(X_1 = X_2), \qquad X_1 \sim \text{type 1},\ X_2 \sim \text{type 2}.$$ It is a property of the ranking the score induces, and it does not depend on any threshold at all.

Under the equal-variance Gaussian model of Section 3 it takes a closed form. $X_1 - X_2$ is normal with mean $\lambda_1 - \lambda_2$ and variance $2\bar\lambda$, so $$\mathrm{AUC} = P(X_1 - X_2 \gt 0) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}{\sqrt{2\bar\lambda}}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{\sqrt{2}}\right).$$ Note the $\sqrt{2}$: two noisy draws are being compared, so the noise variance doubles. Confusing $\Phi(d'/\sqrt2)$ with $\Phi(d'/2)$ is the single most common slip of the week.

Because that relation is invertible, a measured AUC can be turned straight back into a $d'$:

$$d' = \sqrt{2}\,\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{AUC}),$$

which is what d_prime_from_auc computes, using $\Phi^{-1}(a) = \sqrt2\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}(2a-1)$. This little function does more work in this project than its three lines suggest, and here is why.

Why every comparison in this project is made in $d'$, not in accuracy

Suppose one method reaches 98.0% accuracy and another 99.0%. In accuracy space that is a one-point difference and looks negligible. In $d'$ space it is $4.11$ versus $4.65$ — a 13% difference in the underlying separation, and it corresponds to a 28% difference in the sequencing depth you would have to buy, since $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth}}$.

Accuracy saturates near 1 and squashes all large differences into an invisible sliver; $d'$ keeps growing without bound and stays linear in the thing you actually pay for. So: measure accuracy or AUC (they are what you observe), convert to $d'$ (it is what the theory predicts), and compare there. Every figure from Week 7 onwards obeys this rule.

Lab 2 · An ROC curve built from simulated cells
2000 cells of each type are sampled from two Poisson rates, the count is used directly as the score, and the ROC is built by sweeping the threshold down through every observed count. The orange curve is the Gaussian model's prediction, $\mathrm{TPR} = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{FPR}) + d')$. The staircase is coarse for a reason: counts are integers, so hundreds of cells share each score, and a threshold cannot cut between two cells that scored the same.
Ties: the bug that manufactures a perfect classifier out of nothing

Consider a classifier that assigns every cell the same score — say it is broken and always outputs 0. It contains no information, so its AUC must be exactly $0.5$.

Now build the ROC naively: sort the cells by score, walk down the sorted list, and step up for each positive and right for each negative. Sorting a list of identical scores leaves them in whatever order they arrived. If your data file happens to list all the type-1 cells first, the walk goes straight up to $(0,1)$ and then straight across: AUC $= 1$. Concatenate the file the other way round and you get AUC $= 0$. A completely uninformative classifier, reported as flawless, purely because of row order.

The fix is to consume all tied scores at once: a threshold either takes the entire group or none of it, so the ROC has one point per distinct score, not one per cell. That is what the last_of_tie step in roc_curve is doing, and why the scaffold insists on kind="mergesort" (a stable sort) — so that at least the failure is reproducible while you debug it.

The readout in Lab 2 shows both numbers: the tie-aware AUC, and what you would report if you broke ties by input order (which is what this site's small CD.roc helper does, since it walks the samples one at a time). At $\lambda = (1.5, 1.0)$ the honest AUC is about $0.62$ and the order-broken one about $0.75$. Integer counts tie constantly, so this is not an edge case in this project — it is the normal case.

The two that catch everyone in Week 4

In Week 1 you met two cell types with $\lambda_1 = 10.8$ and $\lambda_2 = 9$ at depth 3000, giving $d' \approx 0.572$. (a) What accuracy does $\Phi(d'/2)$ predict? (b) You are allowed to sequence 16 times deeper. What is the new $d'$, and the new accuracy? (c) How many errors per hundred cells did those fifteen extra sequencing runs remove?
Show solution

(a) $\Phi(0.286) \approx 0.613$. About 61% — barely better than a coin, from one gene.

(b) $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth}}$, so 16 times the depth gives $4 \times 0.572 = 2.29$. Then $\Phi(1.144) \approx 0.874$.

(c) From 39 errors per hundred cells down to about 13: twenty-six errors removed, for sixteen times the money.

Notice the shape of the return. The first doubling of depth is worth a lot; by the time you are at 87% the next doubling buys a couple of points. That diminishing return is not an artefact of $\Phi$ — it is what a square-root law feeding a saturating function always looks like, and it is the single most useful thing this project can tell an experimenter.

Exercise 4.2★
Derive the identity $\Phi(z) = \tfrac12\left(1 + \mathrm{erf}(z/\sqrt2)\right)$ from the two definitions $\Phi(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}du$ and $\mathrm{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^x e^{-s^2}ds$. Then verify it numerically at $z = 0$ and $z = 1$.
Show solution

Split the integral at 0 and use the fact that the standard normal density is symmetric, so the left half contributes $\tfrac12$: $$\Phi(z) = \frac12 + \int_0^z \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}\,du.$$ In the remaining integral substitute $u = s\sqrt2$, so $du = \sqrt2\,ds$ and $u^2/2 = s^2$, and the upper limit becomes $z/\sqrt2$: $$\int_0^z \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}du = \int_0^{z/\sqrt2} \frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-s^2}ds = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^{z/\sqrt2}e^{-s^2}ds = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt2}\right).$$ Adding the halves gives the identity. ∎

Check: $\mathrm{erf}(0) = 0$ so $\Phi(0) = 1/2$. And $\mathrm{erf}(1/\sqrt2) = \mathrm{erf}(0.7071) = 0.6827$, so $\Phi(1) = \tfrac12(1.6827) = 0.8413$ — the familiar "68% of a normal lies within one standard deviation", since $\Phi(1) - \Phi(-1) = 0.6827$.

This is exactly the body of normal_cdf, and doing the substitution by hand is what stops the $\sqrt2$ from becoming a magic constant you copy from a webpage. The same factor reappears — for a completely different reason — in $\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi(d'/\sqrt2)$, and being able to tell the two apart is worth the four lines.

Exercise 4.3★★
The exact optimal threshold is $k^\star = (\lambda_1-\lambda_2)/\log(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)$ and the Gaussian derivation puts it at $\bar\lambda = (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/2$. (a) Compute both for $(\lambda_1, \lambda_2) = (12, 8)$ and for $(\lambda_1, \lambda_2) = (6, 1)$, and in each case say whether the two rules actually differ on integer counts. (b) Write $\lambda_1 = \bar\lambda(1+\epsilon)$, $\lambda_2 = \bar\lambda(1-\epsilon)$ and expand $k^\star$ in $\epsilon$ to show the two agree to first order. (c) Which is always smaller, and what does that mean for the rule?
Show solution

(a) $(12,8)$: $k^\star = 4/\log 1.5 = 4/0.4055 = 9.865$ against a midpoint of $10.0$ — a gap of $0.135$. Both rules round to the same integer rule, "call type 1 if $x \ge 10$", so on actual data they are identical.

$(6,1)$: $k^\star = 5/\log 6 = 5/1.7918 = 2.791$ against a midpoint of $3.5$. Now they differ: the exact rule calls type 1 from $x = 3$ upwards, the midpoint rule only from $x = 4$. And $x = 3$ is not a rare event — under $\lambda_2 = 1$ it has probability $0.061$, under $\lambda_1 = 6$ probability $0.089$ — so choosing the wrong threshold costs measurable accuracy. A fold change of 6 at a rate of 1 is exactly the regime where Section 5 says the Gaussian picture stops being trustworthy.

(b) With $\lambda_{1,2} = \bar\lambda(1\pm\epsilon)$, $$k^\star = \frac{2\bar\lambda\epsilon}{\log\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}.$$ Now $\log\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon} = 2(\epsilon + \epsilon^3/3 + \cdots)$, so $$k^\star = \frac{2\bar\lambda\epsilon}{2\epsilon\left(1 + \epsilon^2/3 + \cdots\right)} = \bar\lambda\left(1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{3} + \cdots\right).$$ The two thresholds agree to first order — indeed the correction is only second order in $\epsilon$, which is why the Gaussian rule is so hard to beat at small fold change.

(c) $k^\star \lt \bar\lambda$ always (the logarithmic mean is below the arithmetic mean, for any distinct positive numbers). So the exact rule is slightly more willing to call type 1 than the midpoint rule is. That asymmetry is the Poisson's right skew showing up: the low-rate distribution has more mass pushed up towards the threshold than a symmetric bell would, so the cut has to move down to compensate.

Part (b) is the honest answer to "why does the Gaussian derivation work at all when $\lambda = 3$?" It is not that the Poisson looks normal at $\lambda = 3$ — it does not. It is that the two errors you are making, one under each hypothesis, are nearly the same error and largely cancel in the difference. Cancellation of errors is a recurring theme; Week 11's overdispersion result is where it stops happening.

Exercise 4.5★★
Under the equal-variance Gaussian model, prove $\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi(d'/\sqrt2)$ directly from the definition "$\mathrm{AUC}$ is the probability a random positive outscores a random negative". Then evaluate both $\Phi(d'/2)$ and $\Phi(d'/\sqrt2)$ at $d' = 2$ and say which is larger, with a one-sentence reason that does not mention algebra.
Show solution

Let $X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\lambda_1, \bar\lambda)$ and $X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\lambda_2, \bar\lambda)$ independently. The difference of independent normals is normal, with the means subtracting and the variances adding: $$X_1 - X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2,\ 2\bar\lambda\big).$$ Therefore $$\mathrm{AUC} = P(X_1 \gt X_2) = P(X_1 - X_2 \gt 0) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}{\sqrt{2\bar\lambda}}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{\sqrt2}\right),$$ using $P(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2) \gt 0) = \Phi(\mu/\sigma)$ and $d' = (\lambda_1-\lambda_2)/\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$. (In the continuous model $P(X_1 = X_2) = 0$, so the tie term is absent.) ∎

At $d' = 2$: $\Phi(1) = 0.841$ is the accuracy, and $\Phi(2/1.414) = \Phi(1.414) = 0.921$ is the AUC. The AUC is larger. Why, without algebra: accuracy is judged against a fixed line that must be crossed, but AUC only asks the classifier to rank two cells correctly, and ranking is an easier job than absolute placement — a positive that scores badly can still beat a negative that scores worse.

Because AUC is systematically the friendlier number, papers quote it. Whenever you read an AUC in the literature, convert it: $d' = \sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{AUC})$, and then $\Phi(d'/2)$ tells you the accuracy that AUC actually corresponds to. An AUC of 0.90 sounds excellent and means $d' = 1.81$ and 82% accuracy.

Exercise 4.6★★★
A classifier assigns the same score $s_0$ to all $n$ cells, of which $P$ are positive and $N$ negative. (a) What must its AUC be, on first principles? (b) Show that the naive "one step per cell in sorted order" construction gives AUC $=1$ if the positives happen to come first in the input and $0$ if they come last, and $\approx 0.5$ if the input order is random. (c) Show that collapsing ties — one ROC vertex per distinct score — gives exactly $0.5$ regardless of order. (d) Generalise: prove that the tie-collapsed AUC equals $P(X_1 \gt X_2) + \tfrac12 P(X_1 = X_2)$ for any pair of discrete score distributions.
Show solution

(a) The score carries no information about the label, so no threshold can do better than chance and every threshold does exactly as well as every other. The ROC must be the diagonal and the AUC exactly $0.5$.

(b) Walking the sorted list one cell at a time, each positive moves the pen up by $1/P$ and each negative moves it right by $1/N$. If all $P$ positives are consumed first, the path goes from $(0,0)$ straight up to $(0,1)$ and then straight across to $(1,1)$: the area beneath is the full unit square, $\mathrm{AUC} = 1$. Reversed, the path goes right first and then up, enclosing nothing: $\mathrm{AUC} = 0$. For a random interleaving the path is a random walk hugging the diagonal, giving $\approx 0.5$ with fluctuations of order $1/\sqrt{n}$ — which is arguably worse than being reliably wrong, because it looks plausible.

(c) With ties collapsed there is exactly one distinct score, so the curve has exactly two vertices, $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$, and the trapezoid rule between them gives the area of the triangle: $\tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 0.5$. Order cannot enter, because the construction never looks at individual cells, only at groups sharing a score.

(d) Let the distinct scores be $s_1 \gt s_2 \gt \cdots \gt s_m$, with $a_j = P(X_1 = s_j)$ and $b_j = P(X_2 = s_j)$. After consuming groups $1..j$ the curve is at $x_j = \sum_{i \le j} b_i$, $y_j = \sum_{i\le j} a_i$. The trapezoid rule gives $$\mathrm{AUC} = \sum_{j} (x_j - x_{j-1})\,\frac{y_j + y_{j-1}}{2} = \sum_j b_j\left(\sum_{i \lt j} a_i + \frac{a_j}{2}\right),$$ since $y_j = y_{j-1} + a_j$. The first term sums $b_j a_i$ over all pairs with $i \lt j$, i.e. over all pairs where the positive's score is strictly higher (scores are in decreasing order), giving $P(X_1 \gt X_2)$. The second term is $\tfrac12\sum_j a_j b_j = \tfrac12 P(X_1 = X_2)$. ∎

Part (d) explains why the trapezoid rule (and not the step-function rule) is the right integrator for an ROC: the diagonal segment across a tie group is precisely the "half credit for a tie" that the probabilistic definition demands. This is the derivation behind roc_curve's last_of_tie line and roc_auc's use of np.trapezoid, and it is worth a paragraph in your report, because integer counts tie constantly and any AUC you quote from real data depends on it.

10  Going deeper

  • Green & Swets, Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics (1966), chapter 1. The book that invented $d'$ and the ROC, written for psychologists rather than mathematicians. Read the first chapter only; it is the clearest statement of what "detection" means that anyone has written.
  • Wickens, Elementary Signal Detection Theory (2002), chapters 2–3. The modern textbook version, with the equal-variance model spelled out and the unequal-variance case done properly — which is exactly the assumption you pooled away in Step 2.
  • Fawcett (2006), An introduction to ROC analysis, Pattern Recognition Letters 27(8). Twenty pages, free online, and section 4 is the definitive treatment of ties and of why AUC equals the ranking probability. Read it before you write roc_curve.
  • Hanley & McNeil (1982), The meaning and use of the area under a ROC curve, Radiology 143. The original source for the ranking interpretation, in a medical-imaging context where the cost of a false negative is a missed tumour. Two pages of it will change how you read the word "accuracy" in a paper.
