1  What a buffer week is for

There are fifteen weeks in this schedule and only about thirteen weeks of material in it. The two spare weeks — this one and Week 13 — are not padding, and they are not rewards. They are the only mechanism in the entire plan that can absorb a bad week.

Something will go wrong. A derivation will not come out. A test will fail for two days for a reason that turns out to be a transposed array. You will have a week of exams, or flu, or a family trip. None of that is a project failure; all of it is completely ordinary, and a plan with no slack in it converts any of those ordinary events into a missed deadline in Week 14, when there is nothing left to cut. This is why professional project plans have float in them and why the first thing an inexperienced planner does is remove it.

Read this honestly, and choose

If Weeks 1–5 went well — the M2 verification passed, pytest tests/test_detection.py is fully green, the tag theory-complete is pushed — then spend this week on the closed-book challenge (Section 3) and the bead design (Sections 4–8). That is a full, satisfying week's work, and it is the week that makes Aim 3 possible.

If they did not — if M2 slipped, or two of the Week 5 tests are still red, or the quadrature proof is something you can follow but not produce — then this week is for catching up, and that is the intended use. Do Section 3 and Section 4 only, skip the rest, and pick the bead design up in the first spare hour of Week 7. Nothing downstream breaks: the beads are not drawn until Week 12, and Week 7 does not depend on them.

What you must not do is pretend. A buffer week spent half-heartedly on new material while the old material stays broken is the worst of the three options, because it hides the debt instead of paying it.

There is a second, quieter job for a week like this one. Weeks 1–5 built nine separate results, each introduced when it was needed and then left behind. Nobody holds nine things in their head simultaneously by accident. Consolidation is the deliberate act of laying them out together and seeing that they are not nine things but one argument with nine steps — and this is the last week in which you have the leisure to do it before real data starts arriving.

3  The closed-book challenge

Set a timer for 45 minutes. Close every book, tab and notebook. On one sheet of paper, derive the following, in order, with words between the equations:

The challenge

Starting from "a sequencer captures a small random sample of a cell's transcripts", derive

$$\text{accuracy} \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{D k}\;c}{2}\right), \qquad c = \frac{|p_1 - p_2|}{\sqrt{\bar p}},$$

where $D$ is the depth, $k$ the number of equally informative marker genes, and $p_1, p_2$ the per-molecule expression fractions of one marker in the two types. End with a list of the assumptions you used.

This is not a test. It is a diagnostic, and its only audience is you. The reason to do it closed-book and against a clock is that recognising a derivation and producing one are different skills, and only the second one survives contact with a whiteboard in Week 15 when somebody asks "so where does the square root come from?"

4  The bead experiment, and exactly what it is an analogy for

Aim 3 is two jars of beads on a table. A helper picks one of the two jars — you must not know which — draws $n$ beads from it, and shows them to you. You count how many are red and say which jar it was. Repeat a few hundred times, at several values of $n$, and plot accuracy against $n$.

That is the entire experiment, and the first question anyone will ask is why a biology project involves plastic beads. The answer is precise, and being able to give it precisely is most of the value of the arm.

The analogy, stated exactly · and its limits

What corresponds to what:

In the jarIn the cell
Which jar the helper picked, A or BWhich cell type the cell is
The fraction of red beads in that jar, $p_A$ or $p_B$The expression fraction of a marker gene, $p_1$ or $p_2$
Drawing $n$ beadsCapturing $D$ transcripts — the sequencing depth
The number of red beads you seeThe observed count for that marker gene
Discarding a fraction $q$ of the beads you drew, before lookingDropout: losing a fraction of captured molecules
Your guess of the jarThe classifier's call

What it is not. Say all three of these out loud before you buy any beads, and say them again in Week 12 when you present the result:

  1. It is not sequencing. No molecule is reverse-transcribed, amplified, barcoded or aligned. Every one of those steps has its own failure modes, and the bead arm is blind to all of them.
  2. There is no biology in it. The jars do not have cell cycles, batch effects, ambient RNA, doublets or transcriptional bursting. Nothing about real cells is being tested here, and no result from the beads is evidence about cells as such.
  3. It tests the sampling mathematics, and only that. The claim under test is narrow and clean: when a decision is made from a finite random sample, discriminability grows as the square root of the sample size. That claim is common to both settings, and it is the claim the beads can confirm or refute.

So why bother? Because it is the one line of evidence in the entire project in which nothing is simulated and no software can be wrong. If your simulator has a bug, it will agree with your theory — you wrote both. The beads cannot have that bug. They are physical, they are stubborn, and if the $\sqrt{n}$ law fails on a kitchen table then something is wrong with the law and not with your code.

The overclaim that this box exists to prevent

The tempting sentence — and a version of it appears in most student posters that include a physical analog — is "we validated our single-cell model with a physical experiment." You did not. You validated the sampling mathematics shared by both.

The correct sentence is longer and much stronger: "The bead experiment tests the sampling law in isolation, with no software and no biological assumptions. It agrees with the prediction to within its error bars, which rules out an error in the mathematics and localises any disagreement with real data to the biology." Write that sentence in your notebook now, in your own words, and reuse it in Week 12 and Week 15.

5  The binomial $d'$

The theory transfers, but not by magic: the counts in a jar are binomial, not Poisson, and you have to redo the three lines of Week 3 with the right variance. Fortunately it is the same three lines.

The setting Jar A contains a fraction $p_A$ red beads, jar B a fraction $p_B$, with $p_A \ne p_B$. A jar is chosen with probability $\tfrac12$ each; $n$ beads are drawn from it with replacement, so the draws are independent and identically distributed. Let $K$ be the number of red beads seen. Then $K \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, p)$ with $p \in \{p_A, p_B\}$.
Theorem · the binomial separation index proved With $\bar p = \tfrac12(p_A + p_B)$, $$\boxed{\;d' \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{n}\;|p_A - p_B|}{\sqrt{\bar p\,(1-\bar p)}}\;}$$ so $d' \propto \sqrt{n}$: exactly the same square-root law as sequencing depth, with $n$ in place of $D$.
Proof

Week 2 proved that a binomial has mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$, by writing $K$ as a sum of $n$ independent indicator variables. Apply the Week 3 definition of $d'$ — gap between the means, divided by the standard deviation at the pooled mean — to $K$ instead of to a Poisson count.

Numerator. The gap between the two expected red counts is linear in $n$:

$$|E_A[K] - E_B[K]| = |np_A - np_B| = n\,|p_A - p_B|.$$

Denominator. The pooled variance is also linear in $n$,

$$\mathrm{Var} = n\,\bar p\,(1 - \bar p),$$

but the noise scale is its square root:

$$\mathrm{sd} = \sqrt{n}\,\sqrt{\bar p (1-\bar p)}.$$

Divide.

$$d' = \frac{n\,|p_A-p_B|}{\sqrt{n}\,\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)}} = \sqrt{n}\;\frac{|p_A-p_B|}{\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)}}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

Again the whole theorem is $n/\sqrt{n} = \sqrt{n}$: signal grows linearly with the sample, noise grows as a square root, the ratio grows as a square root.

Assumptions used. (i) Draws are independent and identically distributed — which is what "with replacement" buys, and which is why the protocol insists on it. (ii) Both jars are sampled at the same $n$. (iii) The pooled variance $n\bar p(1-\bar p)$ is the right noise scale — the same modelling convention defended in Week 3, Section 6.

Two remarks, both worth a line in the notebook.

The binomial needs no extra parameter either. In Week 3 the crucial point was that Poisson noise is determined by the mean, so $d'$ has no free parameter to fit. The binomial's variance depends on $n$ and $p$ — but $n$ is chosen by you and $p$ is a property of the jar, so again there is nothing to fit. Ask "what would I have to measure that I do not already know?" and the answer in both cases is: nothing.

The two formulas agree when beads are rare. Set $p$ small in $np(1-p)$ and the factor $(1-p) \to 1$, leaving variance $\approx np$: the binomial has become Poisson, as Week 2 proved it must. Gene expression fractions are of order $10^{-3}$, so for sequencing the distinction is invisible. Bead jars are 30% red, so it is not: at $\bar p = 0.325$ the factor $\sqrt{1-\bar p} = 0.822$ makes the noise smaller than a Poisson of the same mean, and the bead $d'$ is $1/0.822 = 1.22$ times larger than the Poisson formula would have said. Use the right formula for the right experiment — that is exercise 6.3.

6  Sample-size planning, which is the point of the week

Now the calculation this chapter exists for. The plan says the jars will be 30% and 35% red. Before buying a single bead, ask what that experiment would produce if the theory is exactly right.

With $p_A = 0.30$ and $p_B = 0.35$: $|p_A - p_B| = 0.05$, $\bar p = 0.325$, and

$$\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)} = \sqrt{0.325 \times 0.675} = \sqrt{0.219375} = 0.46837,$$ $$d'(n) = \sqrt{n}\,\frac{0.05}{0.46837} = 0.10675\,\sqrt{n}.$$

If your code disagrees with your hand calculation, one of them is wrong and it is worth ten minutes to find out which — that habit is the entire subject of Week 7.

Read the accuracy column and feel the disappointment properly, because it is informative. Twenty times as many beads (10 → 200) buys you 21 percentage points, from 57% to 77%. That is the square-root law being unimpressive on purpose: the prediction is not that sampling more helps a lot, it is that sampling more helps as a square root, and a square root is a slow function.

Now the question nobody asks in time

Those are the true accuracies. You will not observe them. You will observe a proportion correct out of however many trials you run, and a proportion out of a small number of trials is itself extremely noisy — the lesson of Week 1's Lab 2, arriving with money attached.

The plan's default is 40 trials at each draw size. The 95% interval on a proportion from 40 trials has half-width about $$1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{40}} \approx 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{40}} = 1.96 \times 0.0791 = 0.155,$$ so each measured point comes with an error bar of roughly $\pm 0.15$ — an interval about $0.31$ wide.

The entire effect you are trying to measure, across the whole range of $n$, is $0.775 - 0.567 = 0.208$.

The error bar on a single point is wider than the whole signal. That is not a small problem to be fixed by care in the lab. It is a design that cannot answer its own question.

Be precise about how bad it is, because "the error bars overlap" is a slogan and a number is an argument. Comparing the two extreme points, $n = 10$ and $n = 200$, at 40 trials each, the standard error of the difference of two independent proportions is

$$\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_{200} - \hat p_{10}) = \sqrt{\frac{0.775(0.225)}{40} + \frac{0.567(0.433)}{40}} = \sqrt{0.00436 + 0.00614} = 0.1025.$$

The predicted difference is $0.208$, so the expected $z$ is $0.208/0.1025 = 2.03$ — a hair over the 1.96 needed for significance. The power of that comparison is about $\Phi(2.03 - 1.96) = \Phi(0.07) \approx 53\%$. Run the planned experiment and it is roughly a coin flip whether it shows anything at all, even though the theory is exactly right and the beads behave perfectly.

The sample-size formula

To pin a proportion near $p$ down to $\pm h$ with $z$-level confidence, invert the interval half-width $h = z\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$:

$$\boxed{\;n \;\ge\; \frac{z^2\,p(1-p)}{h^2}\;}$$

This is beads.trials_needed(p_true, half_width, z=1.96), which is [PROVIDED] — read it, do not write it. Two features to notice. The cost grows as $1/h^2$: halving the width of your error bar costs four times the trials, the same $4\times$ rule as depth and for the same reason. And $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$, so the harder the question, the more trials it takes — precisely backwards from what one would hope.

Putting numbers in: at $p = 0.65$ and $h = 0.05$, $n \ge 3.8416 \times 0.2275/0.0025 = 349.6$, so 350 trials. That is the number in the function's docstring, and the last column of the table above is the same calculation at each predicted accuracy: about 350 trials per draw size, or 1750 trials for the five-point plain arm alone, and 3500 with the dropout arm. At 200 beads a trial that is on the order of a quarter of a million bead draws. It is not going to happen.

What the planned budget actually is

week06_bead_design.py prints this so you cannot avoid it: 5 draw sizes $\times$ 40 trials $\times$ 2 arms (plain and dropout) $= 400$ trials, and $2 \times 40 \times (10+25+50+100+200) = 30{,}800$ individual bead draws. At a realistic pace — count, record, return, shake — that is somewhere between six and ten hours of table time, spread over Weeks 11 and 12. Four hundred trials is already a serious physical undertaking. Three thousand five hundred is not a student project; it is a summer job.

So the honest options are three, and there is no fourth.

OptionWhat it costsWhat it buys
A. Keep 30/35, raise the trial count to ~350 per point 1750 trials in the plain arm; roughly 9 times the planned effort. Not feasible in this schedule. Each point resolved to $\pm 0.05$; the curve's shape measurable, the fitted exponent meaningful.

At 30/40 the predicted accuracies run 0.630, 0.700, 0.771, 0.853, 0.931 across $n = 10 \dots 200$ — an effect of 0.30 instead of 0.21, and $\mathrm{SE}$ of the extreme difference falls to 0.086, giving $z = 3.5$ and about 94% power at the same 40 trials per point.

The rise becomes detectable.
The lesson of the week, and it is worth more than any formula in it

A design that cannot detect the effect is not an experiment. It is a way of spending a month and then writing "the trend was in the expected direction but did not reach significance", which is the sentence of a project that did its arithmetic too late.

The calculation that saves you takes twenty minutes and needs nothing you did not already have in Week 4. It is not a statistics-course formality. It is the difference between an experiment and a hope.

Notice also that option B is free. That is the usual shape of the answer: when a design is underpowered, the cheapest fix is almost never "collect more data", it is make the effect bigger. In sequencing you cannot choose your cell types; on a kitchen table you can choose your jars, and you should.

A recommendation, not a decree: run 30% vs 40% at 40 trials per point, with $n \in \{10, 25, 50, 100, 200\}$. It fits the planned budget, it has ~94% power for the headline comparison, and — unlike 30/50, which reaches 0.998 by $n=200$ and so pins its top three points against the ceiling where 40 trials cannot distinguish 0.98 from 1.00 — every point stays in the range where a proportion is informative. Whatever you choose, write the choice and the reason in the notebook; Section 12 asks for exactly that, and the mentor check-in asks you to defend it.

What this lab is for, and the trap it is designed to spring

Press "Run again" about ten times at 40 trials per point with the jars at 30/35. Somewhere in those ten runs you will see a sequence of points that goes down as $n$ increases, and you will see at least one run in which the whole thing looks convincingly flat.

9  The protocol, in brief

The full procedure — jar preparation, bead counts, the dropout arm, what to do when a bead rolls under the fridge — lives on the bead protocol page, and you should read it before Week 11. Five points belong here, because each one is a design decision rather than a housekeeping detail, and each one prevents a specific failure that cannot be repaired afterwards.

  • Pre-randomize the jar sequence. Not "the helper picks a jar at random each time" — humans are demonstrably bad random generators, and their characteristic failure is alternating too much. A guesser who unconsciously detects that pattern scores above chance without using the beads at all, which would corrupt every point in the experiment in the same direction. write_tally_template fills the true_jar column in advance from a seeded generator; the helper covers that column and follows it.
  • Blind the guesser. The guesser must not see the jar, the helper's hand, or the tally sheet. Blinding is about the true label only: the guesser should absolutely know the decision rule, and should have the cut card from the table below in front of them. Withholding the rule does not make the experiment purer, it just means you measure the guesser's intuition instead of the sampling law.
  • Draw with replacement. Every bead goes back and the jar is shaken before the next draw. Without replacement the draws are not independent and the counts are hypergeometric, not binomial — the variance acquires a finite-population correction factor and every formula on this page is slightly wrong. Replacement is free; use it.
  • Never delete a trial. Not the one where you miscounted and noticed, not the one where the helper laughed and gave it away, not the run where the results "looked wrong". Mark it, annotate it, exclude it explicitly in the analysis with a written reason. A deleted trial is invisible; an annotated one is data about your procedure. This is the single most important integrity rule in Aim 3.
  • Transcribe the same day. Paper tally into python/data/beads/ CSV within hours, while you can still read your own handwriting and remember which run was which. Then run validate_tally on it immediately — that is what it is for, and it is why you write it this week, before any data exists.

The two that catch everyone in Week 6
  • Using the Poisson $d'$ on the beads. $\sqrt{n}\,|p_A-p_B|/\sqrt{\bar p}$ forgets the $(1-\bar p)$ and understates $d'$ by 18% at $\bar p = 0.325$ — enough to move a predicted accuracy from 0.703 to 0.669 and to make a perfectly good experiment look like a disagreement with theory. The formulas are close cousins and are not interchangeable at bead-sized fractions.
  • Writing validate_tally after the data arrives. A validator written to accept the data you already have is not a validator; it is a rationalisation with a green test next to it. This is why the build order in beads.py puts validate_tally first, in Week 6, and accuracy_by_n six weeks later.

Exercise 6.1★
For jars at $p_A = 0.30$ and $p_B = 0.35$: (a) compute $d'$ and the predicted accuracy at $n = 100$, by hand; (b) how many beads would you need for $d' = 2$, and what accuracy is that? (c) Confirm that going from $n = 50$ to $n = 200$ exactly doubles $d'$, and say why that is the same statement as the "$4\times$ rule" of Week 3.
Show solution

(a) $\bar p = 0.325$, $\sqrt{0.325\times0.675} = 0.46837$, so $d' = \sqrt{100}\times 0.05/0.46837 = 0.5/0.46837 = 1.0675$. Accuracy $= \Phi(1.0675/2) = \Phi(0.5338) = 0.7032$.

(b) $d' = 0.10675\sqrt{n} = 2 \Rightarrow \sqrt{n} = 18.735 \Rightarrow n = 351$ beads per trial. That gives $\Phi(1) = 0.8413$. Three hundred and fifty beads counted, per trial, for 84%.

(c) $d'(200)/d'(50) = \sqrt{200/50} = \sqrt4 = 2$. Quadrupling the sample doubles the separation — the identical statement to "quadrupling the sequencing depth doubles $d'$", because both are $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{sample size}}$ and the sample is the only thing that changed.

Part (b) is the number that makes the whole week concrete. There is no cheap route to a good bead experiment; the law you are testing is precisely the law that makes it expensive. Quote it in Week 15 if anyone asks why you did not just use bigger draws.

Exercise 6.2★
Using $n \ge z^2p(1-p)/h^2$ with $z = 1.96$: (a) how many trials to pin an accuracy near $0.57$ to $\pm 0.05$? (b) To $\pm 0.10$? (c) By what factor does the requirement change if you halve $h$, and where have you seen that factor before? (d) For which true accuracy is the requirement largest, and why is that inconvenient?
Show solution

(a) $p(1-p) = 0.57\times0.43 = 0.2451$, so $n \ge 3.8416 \times 0.2451/0.0025 = 376.6$, i.e. 377 trials.

(b) With $h = 0.10$ the denominator is four times larger: $n \ge 94.2$, i.e. 95 trials.

(c) Halving $h$ multiplies the requirement by 4, because $n \propto 1/h^2$. This is the $4\times$ rule again — the same square root, seen from the other side: precision improves as $\sqrt{n}$, so cost grows as (precision)$^2$.

(d) $p(1-p)$ is maximised at $p = 0.5$, so the requirement is largest exactly when the classifier is at chance. Inconveniently, the low-$n$ end of the bead experiment — the end where the interesting behaviour lives — is the end nearest chance, and therefore the most expensive to measure.

Part (d) has a general moral worth carrying into Week 13: near-chance performance is the hardest thing in statistics to measure precisely, which is why "not significantly different from chance" is such a weak statement unless you also report the trial count.

Exercise 6.3★★
(a) Show that the binomial $d'$ reduces to the Poisson $d'$ of Week 3 when $p$ is small, by writing both and taking the limit. (b) Quantify the discrepancy: what is the ratio of the binomial $d'$ to the Poisson $d'$ at $\bar p = 0.325$, and at $\bar p = 0.005$? (c) A classmate says "the jars are just a Poisson process, so use the Poisson formula". Give the shortest correct reply.
Show solution

(a) Binomial: $d'_{\text{bin}} = \sqrt{n}\,|p_A-p_B|/\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)}$. Poisson with rate $\lambda = np$: $d'_{\text{Poi}} = n|p_A-p_B|/\sqrt{n\bar p} = \sqrt{n}\,|p_A-p_B|/\sqrt{\bar p}$. They differ only by the factor $\sqrt{1-\bar p}$ in the denominator, which tends to 1 as $\bar p \to 0$. That is the Week 2 statement "binomial becomes Poisson when $p$ is small" wearing detection-theory clothes.

(b) The ratio is $d'_{\text{bin}}/d'_{\text{Poi}} = 1/\sqrt{1-\bar p}$. At $\bar p = 0.325$: $1/\sqrt{0.675} = 1.217$, a 22% underestimate if you use the Poisson formula. At $\bar p = 0.005$: $1/\sqrt{0.995} = 1.0025$, a quarter of a percent — invisible.

(c) "It is not a Poisson process, it is $n$ independent Bernoulli draws with $p$ nowhere near zero; Poisson is the $p \to 0$ limit of that, and at $p = 0.325$ the limit is 22% wrong." Alternatively, one sentence of physics: a Poisson count is unbounded, but you can never see more than $n$ red beads.

This is a small piece of real research hygiene: two formulas that agree in the regime you usually work in can differ substantially in a new regime, and the only defence is to know which limit each formula came from. In Week 11 the same issue reappears with the negative binomial, where Poisson is the $\varphi \to 0$ limit and the discrepancy is the entire result.

Exercise 6.4★★
The dropout arm discards each drawn bead, independently, with probability $q$ before you look at it. (a) What is the distribution of the number of beads you actually see, and of the number of those that are red? (b) Show that to first order the effect is to replace $n$ by $n(1-q)$ in $d'$. (c) At $q = 0.30$, by what factor does $d'$ fall, and how many extra beads must be drawn to compensate? (d) Why does discarding beads at random not bias the estimate of the jar, only the precision?
Show solution

(a) The number surviving is $M \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, 1-q)$. Given $M = m$, the reds among them are $\mathrm{Binomial}(m, p)$, because each surviving bead is still an independent draw from the jar. Unconditionally the reds are $\mathrm{Binomial}(n, (1-q)p)$ — a thinning, exactly the Week 2 theorem in binomial clothing.

(b) Treat the surviving sample as a draw of size $n_{\text{eff}} = E[M] = n(1-q)$. Then $$d' = \sqrt{n(1-q)}\;\frac{|p_A-p_B|}{\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)}} = \sqrt{1-q}\;d'(q=0).$$ This is what bead_d_prime computes, and it is first order because it replaces the random $M$ by its mean rather than averaging $d'$ over the distribution of $M$.

(c) $\sqrt{0.70} = 0.8367$: $d'$ falls by 16%. To recover it you need $n' (1-q) = n$, i.e. $n' = n/0.7 = 1.43\,n$ — 43% more beads drawn. At $n = 100$ that is 143 drawn to see 100.

(d) Because the discard rule does not depend on the bead's colour. Each surviving bead is still a uniform draw from the jar, so the expected red fraction among survivors is still $p$; you have a smaller unbiased sample, and a smaller sample is less precise but not systematically wrong. If instead you were more likely to lose red beads — which is exactly what gene-specific dropout does in real data — the estimate would be biased, and that is a limitation worth naming in Week 14.

Part (d) is the honest boundary of the dropout analogy, and it is the sort of thing a good question in the Q&A will find. Real scRNA-seq dropout is not colour-blind: lowly expressed genes are lost preferentially. The bead arm models the colour-blind case, which is also the case the thinning theorem covers.

Exercise 6.5★★
You have a hard budget of 200 trials in the plain arm, jars fixed at 30/35. Compare two allocations: (i) five draw sizes $\{10,25,50,100,200\}$ at 40 trials each; (ii) two draw sizes $\{10, 200\}$ at 100 trials each. For each, compute the standard error of the measured difference between the extreme points and the resulting power to detect the predicted rise. Then say what allocation (ii) costs you, and give a compromise.
Show solution

Predicted accuracies: $0.567$ at $n=10$, $0.775$ at $n=200$, difference $0.208$.

(i) 40 trials each. $\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{0.567(0.433)/40 + 0.775(0.225)/40} = \sqrt{0.00614+0.00436} = 0.1025$. Then $z = 0.208/0.1025 = 2.03$, and power $\approx \Phi(2.03-1.96) = \Phi(0.07) = 0.53$.

(ii) 100 trials each. Every variance is multiplied by $40/100$, so $\mathrm{SE} = 0.1025\sqrt{0.4} = 0.0648$, $z = 3.21$, power $\approx \Phi(1.25) = 0.89$.

What (ii) costs: three of the five points. With two points you can test "is it rising?" but you cannot see the shape, and in particular you cannot fit an exponent — two points determine a line through them exactly, so a fitted slope of $0.5$ would be a tautology rather than a test. Since the project's actual hypothesis is about the exponent, that is a serious loss.

Compromise: three draw sizes $\{10, 50, 200\}$ at 67 trials each. Then $\mathrm{SE} = 0.1025\sqrt{40/67} = 0.0792$, $z = 2.63$, power $\approx \Phi(0.67) = 0.75$, and you retain a middle point, so a fitted exponent has one degree of freedom to be wrong in. Better still, combine it with option B of Section 6 and widen the jars, at which point everything works at once.

This trade-off — resolution of the shape against precision at each point — is a real experimental-design decision with no universally right answer, and articulating it is exactly the kind of thing that distinguishes a research project from a lab exercise. Whatever you choose, the decision and the reasoning go in the notebook before the data.

Exercise 6.6★★★
Derive the optimal decision rule for the jar problem from scratch, as Week 5 did for the Poisson case. (a) Write the two likelihoods for observing $K = j$ reds out of $n$, take logs and subtract, and show the rule is a threshold on $j$. (b) Give the threshold explicitly. (c) Evaluate it for $p_A = 0.30$, $p_B = 0.35$ and compare with the naive midpoint $\bar p n$. (d) Explain, in terms of the shape of the two binomial pmfs, why the two nearly coincide — and construct a case where they do not.
Show solution

(a) $P(K = j \mid A) = \binom{n}{j} p_A^j (1-p_A)^{n-j}$ and likewise for B. Take logs and subtract: $$\mathrm{LLR}(j) = \log\frac{P(j\mid B)}{P(j\mid A)} = j\log\frac{p_B}{p_A} + (n-j)\log\frac{1-p_B}{1-p_A},$$ and note that $\binom{n}{j}$ cancels for exactly the reason $\log(x!)$ cancelled in Week 5: it depends on the observation, not on the hypothesis. Collecting the terms in $j$, $$\mathrm{LLR}(j) = j\left[\log\frac{p_B}{p_A} - \log\frac{1-p_B}{1-p_A}\right] + n\log\frac{1-p_B}{1-p_A},$$ which is linear in $j$. With $p_B > p_A$ the bracket is positive, so $\mathrm{LLR}$ is increasing in $j$ and "call B when $\mathrm{LLR} > 0$" is a threshold rule on the red count.

(b) Setting $\mathrm{LLR} = 0$ and solving, $$j^\star = n\,\frac{\log\dfrac{1-p_A}{1-p_B}}{\log\dfrac{p_B(1-p_A)}{p_A(1-p_B)}},$$ and you call B when $j > j^\star$.

(c) $\log(0.70/0.65) = \log(1.076923) = 0.074108$. $\log\!\big(0.35\times0.70/(0.30\times0.65)\big) = \log(1.256410) = 0.228273$. So $j^\star = 0.32464\,n$, against the midpoint $\bar p n = 0.325\,n$ — a difference of $0.00036\,n$, which is 0.07 of a bead at $n = 200$. The cuts are 4, 9, 17, 33, 65 at $n = 10, 25, 50, 100, 200$, exactly the card in Section 9.

(d) The exact threshold sits where the two pmfs cross. Two binomials with the same $n$ and nearby $p$ have nearly the same width, so their crossing point is nearly midway between their means; the small offset comes from the fact that the jar-B distribution is slightly wider ($p(1-p)$ is larger at $0.35$ than at $0.30$ while both are below $0.5$), pulling the crossing very slightly towards A. Make the widths very different and the two separate: take $p_A = 0.05$ and $p_B = 0.50$ with $n = 20$. Then $\bar p n = 5.5$, while $j^\star = 20\log(0.95/0.50)/\log(0.5\times0.95/(0.05\times0.5)) = 20 \times 0.64185/2.94444 = 4.36$, so the optimal cut is 5 rather than 6 — a whole bead away, and at that contrast a whole bead matters.

Notice what has just happened: you re-derived Week 5's central theorem in a completely different distributional setting and got the same structural answer — the combinatorial factor cancels, the rule is linear in the count, the boundary is a threshold. That is what it feels like when a piece of mathematics is actually general rather than a coincidence of the Poisson, and it is worth saying so in the report.
