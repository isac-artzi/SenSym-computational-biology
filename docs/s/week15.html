Poster, Talk, and Submission

Three panels read from a metre away · twelve minutes, timed · an hour of adversarial questions · out the door · and what actually transfers

Objectives

  • Build a three-panel poster — question, result, limits — that a stranger can read from a metre away in thirty seconds.
  • Give a twelve-minute talk to a timed structure, and say for each slide what is on it and what stays in your mouth.
  • Survive an hour of adversarial questions, including the ones you would rather not be asked, without becoming defensive and without bluffing.
  • Say "I do not know, and here is how I would find out" at least once, deliberately, and mean it.
  • Choose a submission venue, meet its actual current requirements, and submit.
  • Leave the repository in a state where a stranger could pick the project up: v1.0, a final README, and a handover note.
  • Name three fields outside biology where this week's detection theory is the same theory, and say what the $\sqrt{n}$ law has in common across all of them.

1  What this week is

Three deliverables and one rehearsal. The poster and the talk are compressions of the report you froze last week — no new results, no new figures beyond the three poster panels, no new analysis. The submission is an act, not a plan. And the rehearsal is the most valuable hour of the semester, which is not a figure of speech: an hour of a mentor attacking your work will teach you more about it than the preceding two weeks of writing.

The frame for the whole week A poster is a conversation starter, not a paper on a wall. A talk is a guided tour, not a reading. And the Q&A is not an examination you pass or fail — it is the only part of the whole process where you get to find out what a smart stranger thinks of your reasoning. Treat the hard questions as the payoff rather than as the hazard, and the week gets much better.

2  The poster

Start from the physics of the situation. A poster session is a room of people walking past at about one metre, deciding in a few seconds whether to stop. If they stop, they will give you thirty seconds of reading before they either leave or start talking to you. Everything about the design follows from those two numbers.

The three-panel layout

A title bar across the top, then three columns, read left to right:

PanelHeadingFigureWordsThe one thing it must land
P1The questionP1_question.png≤ 80 Counting is sampling, so classifying a cell is a detection problem — and here is the question that follows.
P2The resultP2_result.png≤ 80 Four times the data, twice the separation — predicted from Poisson statistics with no fitted parameters, and confirmed three independent ways.
P3The limitsP3_limits.png≤ 80 Real counts are overdispersed, so there is a ceiling: past the knee, deeper sequencing buys almost nothing.

Under the three columns, a single strip: methods in four lines, the three or four references that matter, the repository URL, and the acknowledgement. Total word count for the entire poster: under 300. If you are over, you are writing a paper on a wall.

Type sizes, derived rather than looked up

A letter is legible when its cap height subtends about 5 arcminutes at the eye, and comfortable continuous reading wants roughly three times the threshold. At one metre:

$$5' = \frac{5}{60}\times\frac{\pi}{180} = 1.45\times10^{-3}\ \text{rad}, \qquad h_{\min} = 1000\,\text{mm} \times 1.45\times10^{-3} = 1.45\ \text{mm}.$$

Three times that is a cap height of about 4.4 mm. Cap height is roughly 0.7 of the nominal font size, so the font must be at least $4.4/0.7 = 6.3$ mm, and since 1 pt $= 0.353$ mm that is 18 pt — the absolute floor for body text. Design at 24–28 pt to have a margin.

The title must work from three metres, so triple again: 54 pt minimum, and 72–100 pt in practice. Panel headings sit between: 40–48 pt. Captions may drop to 20–24 pt, and nothing on the poster goes below 18 pt — including the axis labels inside your figures, which is why week15_poster_figures.py sets its own larger font sizes instead of scaling up the report's PNGs.

Check it, do not trust it: print one panel at final size, tape it to a wall, and stand a metre back. Five minutes, and it catches every mistake at once.

A poster is a conversation starter

The commonest poster failure is treating the board as the deliverable. It is not — you are the deliverable, and the board exists to get someone to stop and start talking to you, and then to give you something to point at.

Practical consequences. Leave things out on purpose and know what you left out, so that when someone asks "how did you pick the markers?" you have a real answer and a page in your folder rather than a paragraph on the wall they could have read without you. Do not put the derivation on the poster; put the result and derive it out loud on request. And have a one-sentence opener ready for the person who stops and says nothing: "It is about how much sequencing you need to tell two kinds of cell apart — do you want the thirty-second version or the three-minute one?"

3  The twelve-minute talk

Twelve minutes is a hard budget, and most first talks fail the same way: eight minutes of background, three minutes of rushed results, no limitations, and the buzzer during the conclusion. The structure below prevents that, because the section budgets are fixed before the slides are made.

SectionTimeSlidesWhat is on the slideWhat stays in your mouth
The question2:001–3 Title. One cell, one count, the number 41% (relative noise on a count of 6). The question in one line. Why anyone should care; the analogy to a poll of 6 people; the fact that most genes come back as zero.
The theory3:004–6 Poisson: variance $=$ mean, one line. $d'$ defined with a two-distribution picture. $d'\propto\sqrt{Dk}$ and accuracy $=\Phi(d'/2)$. F2. The derivation itself, delivered as a story of three steps, pointing at each line as you say it. Never read the algebra aloud symbol by symbol.
Three lines of evidence4:007–9 F3 simulation. F4 PBMC. F5 beads. One number with its interval per slide. What each line of evidence can and cannot rule out; why you did three instead of one; that the bead prediction was fixed before any bead was drawn.
Where it fails2:0010–11 The Fano panel: the data is not Poisson. F6: measured minus predicted with intervals. The one-donor limitation, the label-circularity limitation, and the sentence "this is the most interesting thing I found".
What next1:0012 Three bullets: more donors, effective $k$ from marker correlation, real deep sequencing rather than downsampling. Which of the three you would do first and why.
Backup slides, after the end: the full $d'$ derivation · the claim ledger · the Wilson coverage figure · the bead protocol photo · the marker-selection procedure. Never shown unless asked, always ready.
What goes on the slide, and what does not

A slide is a visual aid for a spoken argument. Two rules cover almost everything.

If it is on the slide, the audience is reading it and not listening to you. So a slide holds one figure, one equation, or at most three short lines — never a paragraph, and never a sentence you intend to say out loud, because they will read it faster than you can say it and then wait, irritated.

Caveats and nuance go in the mouth, not on the slide. The audience cannot hold a footnote in their head at 40 seconds a slide, but they will remember you saying "these are one donor, so the error bar is about cells, not people" — and they will trust you more for having said it while the figure was up rather than in a limitations slide at the end.

The corollary: rehearse out loud, standing, with a timer, at least three times. A talk rehearsed in your head runs 30–40% faster than the same talk delivered, which is exactly the error that produces the buzzer-during-the-conclusion ending.

4  Adversarial Q&A rehearsal

Book an hour with your mentor and ask them to be difficult. Not unkind — difficult: to interrupt, to ask the question you were hoping to avoid, to say "that is not what your figure shows". This hour is worth more than any other hour this week, for three reasons. You will discover which parts of your own argument you cannot actually reconstruct. You will hear your weakest claim said out loud by someone else, which is much easier to fix now than in front of a judge. And you will practise the single most valuable move in research conversation, which is saying "I do not know".

How to answer a hard question
  1. Say the honest thing first, in one sentence. "Yes, partly, and it is the limitation I would attack first." Concede immediately what is true; you cannot be cornered on ground you already gave up.
  2. Then give the substance — two or three sentences, with a number in them if a number exists.
  3. Then say what would settle it. This is the move that converts a weakness into evidence of judgement.

Never: bluff, argue about whether the question was fair, answer a different question you prefer, or say "that is a good question" while you think (everyone knows what it means). If you need a moment, ask them to repeat it — that is free, and it usually makes the question sharper.

The question bank

Fifteen real questions. Answer each out loud, in two to four sentences, before reading the sketch. The sketches are not scripts; the phrasing must end up being yours. The stars here mark how hard the question is to answer well, not how hard it is to understand — the three-star ones are the questions that decide how the rest of the conversation goes.

Q1★★
"Isn't this just saying more data is better? Everyone knows that."
Show a good answer

"More is better" makes no prediction. Mine does: discriminability grows as the square root of depth times marker count, so doubling how well you separate two cell types costs four times the data, and the accuracy is $\Phi(d'/2)$ with nothing fitted. That could have been wrong — an exponent of 1 would have falsified it. And the second half of the result is that more data eventually stops helping, which "more is better" gets exactly backwards.

Q2★★
"Why not use a neural network? Wouldn't that do better?"
Show a good answer

It could not do much better, and I checked rather than assuming. Under the model, the naive-Bayes rule is provably optimal, and in Week 8 logistic regression and kNN both came close to it, with the remaining gap a finite-sample effect rather than a modelling one — so the bottleneck is the information in the counts, not the classifier. A network could only close a gap that is already about zero, and it would not tell me why accuracy scales as the square root of depth, which is the actual question.

Q3★★★
"Your cell type labels come from clustering the same data. Isn't your accuracy measurement circular?"
Show a good answer

Partly, yes, and it is the limitation I would attack first. My "accuracy" is agreement with an algorithmic annotation, not with truth, and I say so in the report. Two things limit the damage: the labels come from clustering on all genes at full depth while I classify from 32 markers at up to 50-fold downsampling, so the tasks are not the same one; and what I am testing is the shape of the accuracy-versus-depth curve, which circularity gives no reason to look like $\Phi(d'/2)$. The clean fix is FACS-sorted cells, where the label comes from a protein measurement made independently of the RNA — that is the experiment I would run next.

Q4★★
"Why should I believe that beads in a jar have anything to do with sequencing?"
Show a good answer

They do not model sequencing. They model sampling, which is the only part of sequencing my theory is about. Drawing $n$ beads from a jar that is 30% red is the same mathematical problem as capturing $D$ molecules from a cell: a random sample from an unknown composition, and a decision from the tally. The beads cannot test anything biological — what they test is whether the $\sqrt{n}$ law and $\Phi(d'/2)$ survive contact with a physical process where none of my software can be wrong, which is exactly the failure mode the simulation cannot check.

Q5★★★
"The offsets in your three-ways figure are arbitrary — you slid the curves around until they lined up. Why is that figure not misleading?"
Show a good answer

Because the rescaling is stated in the caption and no part of the claim depends on it. The three lines of evidence have incomparable x-units — molecules, simulated depth, beads — so their absolute positions mean nothing; only the slopes are comparable, and the slope is what the theory predicts. I rescaled each series to a common starting point so slopes can be compared by eye, and the fitted exponents with their intervals are printed on the figure so you do not have to take the eye's word for it. The un-rescaled version is in the appendix, and I would much rather show it to you than have you find it.

Q6★★★
"You have one donor. What does your error bar actually cover?"
Show a good answer

Resampling of cells within one blood sample, and nothing else. It does not cover variation between people — genotype, age, health, batch — and with a single donor that variation cannot be estimated at all, because a sample of one has no measurable spread. So every real-data claim in my report should be read as being about this sample rather than about T cells and monocytes in general. Fixing it needs several donors analysed with the donor as the unit rather than the cell; I costed it, and about nine donors would pin the mean accuracy to $\pm 0.02$ if between-donor variation is around 0.03.

Q7★★★
"You assumed genes are independent. They are not — genes are co-regulated. How wrong does that make you?"
Show a good answer

Wrong in a direction I can name and a size I have not measured. Independence is what let me add $d'$ in quadrature, so positive correlation means the effective number of independent genes is below the nominal $k$ and my $\sqrt{k}$ law is an upper bound on what extra markers buy. It does not touch the depth law, which is per-gene and does not use independence. Measuring the correlation among my 32 markers and turning it into an effective $k$ is a week of work and it is second on my list.

Q8★★
"Your bead exponent is $0.42 \pm 0.20$. Isn't that consistent with almost anything?"
Show a good answer

Nearly — it is consistent with 0.3 and with 0.6 as well as with 0.5, so it is weak support and the report says so in those words. The width comes from 40 trials per draw size, which is what one person can do by hand in an afternoon; quartering the interval would take sixteen times the draws, which I costed in Week 6 and could not afford. What the beads do establish strongly is something else: accuracy rises with $n$ along a curve computed before any bead was drawn, with no parameter fitted to the bead data at all.

Q9★★★
"What would have falsified your hypothesis?"
Show a good answer

Several things, all fixed in advance. In simulation, a fitted exponent whose interval excluded $1/2$, or measured accuracy departing from $\Phi(d'/2)$ by more than 0.02 in the clean regime — that was a written gate in Week 9. In the dropout arm, curves that failed to collapse onto effective depth. And on real data the Poisson version of the prediction was falsified: the measurements sit below it systematically, the gap closes when I use the measured overdispersion, and that is the main finding rather than a disappointment.

Q10★★
"What is the practical recommendation for someone designing a sequencing run tomorrow?"
Show a good answer

Three things. Below the overdispersion knee, depth follows the square-root law, so budget four times the reads for twice the separation and decide whether that is worth it. Past the knee, extra depth buys almost nothing for cell typing, and the same money spent on more cells or — better — more donors buys a great deal. And extra markers help as $\sqrt{k}$ only if they carry independent signal, so adding co-regulated genes to a panel is close to free of benefit. Where the knee sits depends on your genes' dispersion, which a pilot run and the Fano computation in my repository will tell you in an afternoon.

Q11★★
"Did you use AI to do this?"
Show a good answer

Yes, and it is disclosed in every weekly log under "Tools used", against the policy in the course repository. I used it the way I would use a tutor and a rubber duck: explaining concepts I was stuck on, finding bugs, and criticising drafts. The derivations are mine in the sense that matters — ask me to do the $d'$ derivation at the board right now and I will. The code I can explain line by line, and the tests were written from the specification before the implementations, so they check the maths rather than agreeing with my code.

This answer only works if it is true. That is the entire point of the disclosure habit: it costs nothing when you have been honest all semester and cannot be manufactured in Week 15.

Q12★★
"What would you do with another semester?"
Show a good answer

Three things, in this order. Multiple donors, so that an error bar on a real-data number means something about people instead of about cells. Measuring the correlation among my markers, so that $\sqrt{k}$ becomes a prediction in terms of an effective $k$ rather than a nominal one. And testing the depth law against genuinely deeper sequencing rather than downsampling, because thinning a shallow library and paying for a deep one are not the same experiment and I have been assuming they are.

Q13★★
"How do you know your simulator isn't just producing whatever you told it to produce?"
Show a good answer

To a large extent it is, and that is what it is for: the simulator tests the algebra, not the biology. It samples Poisson counts, so the only thing it can check is whether $\Phi(d'/2)$ is the right consequence of Poisson counting — which is a real thing to be wrong about, and in fact it fails at low depth, where the Gaussian approximation breaks. The tests of the model are the PBMC data and the beads, and I wrote neither of those.

Q14★
"Why 30/70 and 35/65 for the jars? Those look like convenient numbers."
Show a good answer

They were designed in Week 6, before a single bead was drawn, to put the interesting part of the curve inside the number of trials I could do by hand: a difference small enough that ten beads tell you almost nothing and large enough that a couple of hundred gets you near certainty. The sample-size calculation is in the repository, and the draw sizes and trial counts came out of it rather than out of the data.

Q15★★
"What is $d'$, and why not just report accuracy? Accuracy is what people care about."
Show a good answer

$d'$ is the gap between the two cell types' expected counts, measured in units of the counting noise. It is the natural variable because it is the one with the simple law — it grows as $\sqrt{Dk}$ — whereas accuracy is that quantity squashed through $\Phi$, which compresses everything near 1 and hides large changes at the top end. So I report both: $d'$ when I am testing the scaling law, accuracy when I am saying what it means for someone designing an experiment.

5  Where to submit

Read this before the list Deadlines, eligibility rules, word limits and paperwork requirements change every year, and several of these programmes have altered their rules substantially in recent cycles. Everything below is orientation, not instruction. Check the current rules on the organisation's own site, and check them with your fair's Scientific Review Committee before you commit — especially anything involving prior approval, which several fairs require before the research is done and cannot grant retroactively.
VenueWhat it wantsNotes
Regional science fair A board, an abstract (usually ~250 words), a research plan and forms, and a 10–15 minute interview with several judges. The natural first stop, and the best rehearsal available: several rounds of exactly the Q&A you drilled in Section 4. Usually held between January and March.
State fair The same, at a higher standard, usually by advancing from a regional. Judges are more likely to know the field. Expect harder versions of Q3 and Q6.
ISEF (via an affiliated fair) You cannot enter directly: you advance from an affiliated regional or state fair. Strict forms, an abstract, a research plan, and category selection. This project fits Computational Biology and Bioinformatics. The forms are genuinely strict about prior approval and about human-subjects rules — a public de-identified dataset such as PBMC 3k is normally straightforward, but your SRC decides, not you.
JEI (Journal of Emerging Investigators) A real manuscript with a mentor as co-author, genuine peer review, and revision rounds. Free to submit. The best fit for what you have written, because your Week 14 report is already almost the right document. Expect reviewers to ask precisely the questions in Section 4 — in writing, and with time to answer them properly. Being reviewed is the most educational outcome available this semester.
Regeneron STS US high-school seniors; a research report, essays, and recommendations; deadline typically in November. Timing matters: a semester ending in December is a year early or a year late depending on your school year. Plan the calendar backwards from it if you intend to enter.
Davidson Fellows A substantial piece of work with a portfolio and a written description of its significance; deadline typically in February. Highly selective, and explicitly looks for work that is significant rather than merely competent. Worth reading past winners' abstracts to calibrate.
arXiv q-bio preprint A formatted manuscript and an endorsement from an established author (your mentor may be able to endorse or arrange it). Only with your mentor's explicit agreement, and only after they have read the final version. A preprint is permanent and public; that is the point of it and also the risk. Check whether any competition you plan to enter treats prior publication as disqualifying.

Choose one, possibly two. A fair plus JEI is a strong pair: the fair gives you the spoken defence and JEI gives you the written one. Submitting to nothing is the only wrong answer, because the submission is what converts a semester of work into something that exists outside your own laptop.

8  What actually transfers

Here is the part worth carrying out of the semester. Almost none of it is about cells.

Detection theory is everywhere

The structure you derived — two hypotheses, overlapping distributions, a threshold, an error probability governed by a separation index — is not a fact about sequencing. It is the same structure in:

  • Radar, where it was worked out in the 1940s and 1950s and where the notation $d'$ comes from. Is that blip an aircraft or noise? Same two distributions, same threshold, same trade-off between missing a target and crying wolf.
  • Medical testing. Sensitivity and specificity are the two tail probabilities you computed; an ROC curve is the same curve; and "should we lower the screening threshold?" is the same question as where to place the decision boundary.
  • Spam filters. Naive Bayes on word counts is literally the classifier in Week 5 — a sum of per-feature log-likelihood ratios, thresholded — with genes replaced by words and depth by message length.
  • Particle physics. The five-sigma discovery convention is a statement about $d'$: it fixes the false-positive rate for a signal separated from background by five standard deviations, and it exists because physicists run enormous numbers of comparisons and know what Section 7 of Week 13 does to you if you do not correct for it.

You now have the machinery for all four, and you will recognise it on sight for the rest of your life. That recognition is worth more than the specific result you proved.

The $\sqrt{n}$ law is one law, wearing five costumes

Count them up. The relative noise on a Poisson count falls as $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$ (Week 1). $d'$ grows as $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ (Week 3) and as $\sqrt{k}$ (Week 5). The bead accuracy rises with $\sqrt{n}$ (Week 12). And the width of your own Wilson error bars shrinks as $1/\sqrt{n}$ (Week 13) — four times the trials, half the width, which the test suite checks explicitly.

All five are one argument: independent things add, their means add linearly, their variances add linearly, so their standard deviations add as a square root, and any ratio of the two grows as $\sqrt{n}$. Every square root in this project descends from the single line in Week 1's second worked derivation. Once you see that, you will start predicting the exponent before doing the algebra — and being right, and knowing why.

The habit that will still be useful in ten years

Labelling claims. proved · cited · observed · conjectured.

It felt like bureaucracy in Week 1 and like a chore in Week 13. What it actually is, is a discipline for keeping the difference between the data agrees with the theory and I want the data to agree with the theory visible to yourself. Nobody else can do that for you, and no amount of statistical machinery substitutes for it — you can compute a perfectly correct p-value about a claim you have quietly promoted two levels.

Ten years from now you will not remember the coefficient in the Wilson half-width. You will still, in a meeting, hear someone say "we showed that…" and find yourself asking whether they mean proved, cited, observed or conjectured. That question is the most portable thing this course had to give you.

9  Exercises

Stars mark difficulty: ★ routine · ★★ requires an idea · ★★★ challenge. These produce material you will actually use this week.

Exercise 15.1★
Using the arcminute argument in Section 2, compute the minimum comfortable font size for text read at (a) 1 m, (b) 2 m, (c) 3 m. Then measure the smallest type on your drafted poster and say whether it passes.
Show solution

The chain is: comfortable cap height $= 3 \times 5' \times$ distance; font size $=$ cap height $/\,0.7$; points $=$ mm $/\,0.353$.

At 1 m: cap height $3 \times 1.45 = 4.36$ mm, font $6.23$ mm $= 17.6$ pt, so 18 pt.

The result is linear in distance, so (b) 2 m gives 35 pt and (c) 3 m gives 53 pt.

These are floors, not targets. Design body text at 24–28 pt (a 1.4× margin at one metre), headings at 40–48 pt, and the title at 72–100 pt. The check that matters is physical: print one panel at final size, tape it up, walk back a metre.

The commonest violation is not the body text — it is the axis labels inside a figure that was designed for a page and then dropped onto a poster. Run week15_poster_figures.py rather than resizing.

Exercise 15.2★
Your talk has 12 slides plus 5 backups. (a) What is the average time per shown slide? (b) Which two slides should get roughly double, and which two should get half? (c) Your first rehearsal runs 15:30. Which section do you cut, and by how much?
Show solution

(a) $12{:}00 / 12 = 60$ seconds per slide. Backup slides get zero by definition.

(b) Double (about two minutes each): the slide carrying $d' \propto \sqrt{Dk}$ and $\Phi(d'/2)$, because it is the entire contribution and the audience needs time on it; and the divergence slide (F6), because it is the most interesting result and the one that invites questions. Half (about 30 seconds each): the title slide and the "what next" slide, which is three bullets you say faster than they read.

(c) 3:30 over. Cut it from the sections that grow by accident, not from the ones that carry the argument: about 1:00 from the question section (background always inflates), about 1:00 from theory by moving the full derivation to a backup slide, and about 1:30 from the evidence section by giving each of the three lines one number rather than three. Do not cut "where it fails" — that section is why the talk is worth listening to, and cutting it is the change that most damages the impression you leave.

Rehearsing at 15:30 on the first pass is normal and is not a problem. Being at 15:30 on the third pass is a problem, because it means you are cutting words rather than material.

Check: concedes in the first four words; substantiates with two specific, checkable facts; names the experiment that would settle it. Nothing defensive, nothing bluffed, and it fits in one breath.

Sixty words is roughly 25 seconds of speech. Any answer that runs past a minute has stopped being an answer and started being a second talk — and the questioner usually only wanted to know whether you had thought about it.

A defensible choice, picking the limitations that are both severe and comprehensible at a glance:

Exercise 15.6★★★
Design the multi-donor follow-up. (a) If between-donor standard deviation in accuracy is about 0.03, how many donors are needed to pin the mean to $\pm 0.02$ at 95%? (b) What is the unit of analysis and what changes in the code? (c) Give one result that would make you abandon the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ framing entirely. (d) Why is this a better use of a semester than adding a neural network?
Show solution

(a) The half-width of a 95% interval for a mean is $1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{m}$. Setting $1.96 \times 0.03/\sqrt{m} = 0.02$ gives $\sqrt{m} = 2.94$ and $m = 8.6$, so 9 donors. (With $m$ that small you would use a $t$ interval, which pushes it to eleven or twelve — worth saying out loud, because it is the kind of correction a judge notices.)

(b) The unit of analysis is the donor, not the cell. In code: compute one accuracy per donor, assemble a vector of nine numbers, and put the interval on that — bootstrap_ci over donors, or a $t$ interval. Cell-level bootstrapping stays, but only as a within-donor diagnostic. Everything else, including the classifier, is unchanged; the whole difference is which axis you resample along.

(c) If the per-donor accuracy-versus-depth curves had different shapes — not merely different heights — then no single exponent describes the population and the framing is wrong rather than imprecise. A shared shape with donor-specific offsets would instead mean the law holds with a per-donor constant, which is a much weaker and much more repairable finding.

(d) Because the bottleneck is not the classifier. Week 8 established that logistic regression and kNN both come close to the provably optimal naive-Bayes rule, so a better classifier has very little left to win. The bottleneck is that every real-data claim currently rests on one person's blood. The next experiment should attack the largest source of uncertainty, and that is not the model.

Part (d) is the answer to Q2 in a stronger form, and it is also the habit worth taking out of the semester: when choosing what to do next, ask what dominates your uncertainty rather than what would be interesting to build.
