How to use this course

The weekly rhythm · a realistic time budget · what to do when a week runs long · and what "verification" feels like from your side of the table

This course is a research project with a textbook wrapped around it, and it only works if the weekly loop actually closes. This page describes that loop in enough detail that you never have to guess what to do next. Read it before Week 1, and skim it again around Week 6, when the habits are either forming or not.

1  The shape of a week

Seven steps, in this order. The order is not decoration: each step produces the input the next one needs, and doing them out of order is the most common way a week turns into ten hours with nothing committed at the end.

#StepWhat "done" means
1Read the chapter (docs/s/weekNN.html), playing with the labs as you meet them.You can state the week's main result in one sentence without looking.
2Work the exercises in the paper notebook, before opening the solutions.Every exercise attempted in full sentences; the ones that failed written down as failures.
3Implement the week's TODOs in python/celldetect/.No NotImplementedError left in this week's functions.
4Get pytest green. cd python && pytest tests/test_<module>.py, then the whole suite.Green, and you can say in one sentence what each test is checking.
5Run the experiment. python experiments/weekNN_*.py, which writes into figures/.The figure exists, carries its seed in the corner, and is not stamped "not a result".
6Commit code AND figure, with the seed recorded.One commit, message naming the week and the seed. A figure with no seed is not a result.
7Write the progress log: copy progress/TEMPLATE.md to progress/weekNN.md, fill it in, commit.All seven headings filled, including "Where I'm stuck" and the "Tools used" disclosure line.

And every two weeks, an eighth step: open a check-in issue from .github/ISSUE_TEMPLATE/checkin.md, fill in the links and the agenda, and meet your mentor. Section 8 says what happens there.

Why step 2 comes before step 3

The exercises are where the derivation becomes yours; the code is where it becomes executable. If you code first, you will write something that passes the tests and never notice that you could not have derived the formula you typed. The tests are deliberately not able to detect that, and the mentor check-in deliberately is.

The one exception is when the code is what unsticks the derivation — computing a small case numerically to see what the answer should look like is a legitimate move and is step 3 of the unstuck protocol. The difference is whether you are using the computer to see or to avoid seeing.

Two commands you will type a hundred times

cd python && pytest tests/test_detection.py — one module's tests. The conftest.py at python/ anchors pytest's rootdir there, so import celldetect resolves to your package and not to something else.

pytest -k "d_prime" — run only the tests whose names contain a string. Early in a week, when eleven tests are red because you have not written the functions yet, narrowing to the one you are working on is the difference between a signal and a wall of text.

Add -x to stop at the first failure and -q to quieten the output. From the repository root the whole suite is pytest python/tests/, which is what the check-in template asks about.

2  What a week actually costs

Plan on 6 to 10 hours per week. Here is where it goes in a typical, non-milestone week:

ActivityTypicalRangeNotes
Reading the chapter and playing with its labs1.5 h1–2 hNot one sitting. Two sittings with a night in between is measurably better.
Exercises in the notebook2.5 h2–3.5 hThe ★★★ exercise alone can be an hour, and is optional in a hard week.
Implementing the TODOs and getting pytest green2 h1.5–3 hThe variance here is enormous. One dtype bug is forty minutes.
Running the experiment, checking the figure, committing0.5 h0.5–1 hLonger the first time a script needs real parameters rather than fast=True.
Progress log0.5 h0.3–0.75 hDo it the same day. Written on Wednesday about last Saturday, it is fiction.
Bead runs (Weeks 6–12 only)1 h0.5–1.5 hAbout six to ten hours of table time in total, spread over those weeks.
Total7–8 h6–10 hMilestone weeks (3, 5, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15) run at the top of the range.

Two structural facts about that budget. First, roughly half of it is writing — notebook, log, eventually report. That is not overhead added to the research; in a research project it is a large part of what the research is. Second, the estimate assumes you stop when the time is spent. A week that runs to fifteen hours is not heroism, it is a signal, and Section 6 is about what to do with that signal.

The schedule failure that actually happens Not "I fell behind in Week 4". It is: you skip the progress log for three weeks because nothing interesting happened, then in Week 14 you need to write a materials-and-methods paragraph and cannot remember which seed produced the figure, whether the Fano factor was 1.4 or 1.6, or why you switched marker panels. The log takes thirty minutes a week and saves a day in Week 14. This is the single highest-return habit in the course.

3  How to read a chapter

Chapters have a fixed structure, and the most efficient path through them is not top to bottom.

  1. Objectives first, properly. There are five to seven of them, each concrete and checkable, and together they are the answer to "what am I supposed to be able to do on Friday?". They are checkboxes on the page and the state is remembered in your browser; tick them as they become true. If an objective is still unticked on Friday, that is your agenda item, not a vague feeling of unease.
  2. Then the labs — before the prose around them. Move every slider to both ends. Try to predict what will happen before you drag, and notice when you are wrong.
  3. Then the prose, from the top, including the boxes. Definitions and theorems are boxed so you can find them again in Week 14; pitfalls are boxed because somebody predicted your specific mistake.
  4. Then the exercises, in the notebook, solutions closed.
  5. Then the Python section, which names the exact functions, the exact pytest command, the exact experiment script and its seed.
Why doing the labs before reading works better

A lab gives you a surprise: the Fano factor sits at 1 no matter where you put the slider, the histogram gets absolutely wider and relatively narrower, the fitted exponent wanders wildly at 40 trials per point. A surprise is a question your brain has already asked, and the prose that follows is then an answer to a question rather than an unrequested fact. Reading first and then confirming with the lab produces recognition, which feels like understanding and is not the same thing.

There is a second reason, specific to this project. Several chapters put a measurement in the lab (blue) and a prediction in the prose (orange). Meeting the measurement first means you spend the derivation asking "will it really come out to that?" — which is exactly the posture Week 12 needs from you, when three lines of evidence have to be compared without flinching.

3.1  The exercise star ratings

RatingMeansExpectIf you are short of time
★Routine. Applies a definition or does an arithmetic substitution.5–15 minutes. If it takes an hour, the definition has not landed — go back to it.Do all of them. They are the cheapest possible check that you read the chapter.
★★Requires an idea: a step you have to supply, a case you have to construct, an argument you have to assemble.20–45 minutes, with a stuck stretch in the middle. That stretch is the exercise.Do them. This is where most of the learning is.
★★★Challenge. Open-ended, or requires combining two chapters, or asks you to guess a result and then test it.45 minutes to well over an hour, and sometimes no clean answer.The one to defer in a bad week. Note in the log that you deferred it, so it can come back in Week 6 or 13.

Every exercise has a complete worked solution behind a Show solution toggle, followed by a grey paragraph saying why the exercise was worth doing and where it reappears. Read the grey paragraph even when your answer was right — it is usually a forward reference that tells you which result is load-bearing later.

How to use the solutions without wasting them A solution can be read once per exercise, and only after a real attempt: you have re-read the statement, tried a small case, and can say which step you are stuck on. Read it, close it, and then write your own version from understanding, on paper. The same policy governs python/solutions/: it is not cheating to read it, it is cheating to present it as yours at a milestone — and the check-in asks you to explain your code line by line, which code you pasted does not survive.

4  Commits, seeds, and figures

Three conventions, all of them small, all of them about being able to reproduce your own work in ten weeks.

  • Every experiment has a SEED constant at the top — 101 for Week 1, 102 for Week 2, up to 115 for Week 15. The seed is part of the result. It goes in the progress log and it is stamped on the figure by _common.stamp_seed.
  • Figures are committed. They are deliverables, not build artefacts. figures/week03_dprime_depth.png is a thing you will point at in a check-in and paste into a report.
  • Code and figure go in the same commit. If they drift apart, the figure stops being evidence about the code, and in Week 14 you will not be able to tell which version produced it.
The red stamp Before the PBMC download exists, Weeks 10–11 run on a synthetic surrogate; before your bead runs exist, Week 12 simulates the draws. Both stamp the figure with a large red "not a result" and set a module flag USED_STAND_IN, and week14_report_figures.py refuses to copy a stamped figure into the report set. If a figure carries the stamp, the claim it supports does not exist yet. Do not put one in a slide, and do not let one sit in your progress log without a sentence saying it is a stand-in.

5  Milestones and tags

Eight milestones, five git tags. A milestone is a verification event: you demonstrate something to your mentor, live, and the outcome is recorded. A tag is a permanent marker in the repository's history saying "this state of the repository passed that verification".

MilestoneWeekGit tagWhat it certifies
M13—The $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth}}$ derivation, defended at the whiteboard
M25theory-completeAim 1 closed: the $\sqrt{k\cdot\text{depth}}$ law and the accuracy formula
M39simulator-validatedSimulation matches theory, or the disagreement is characterized
M411—The real PBMC downsampling curve, produced end to end
M512evidence-completeAll three lines of evidence on one axis
M613—Every claim labelled, every proportion with an interval
M714report-draftThe 6–8 page report, figures frozen
M815v1.0Poster, talk, submission
The tag rule, and how to tag

Tag only when pytest python/tests/ is fully green and the relevant figures regenerate identically from a clean checkout. Not "mostly green". Not "green except the one I know about".

git tag -a theory-complete -m "M2: Aim 1 closed, all detection tests green" then git push origin theory-complete.

If the gate does not pass, tag nothing and write the reason in the week's progress log. A tag you talked your way into is worse than no tag, because in Week 14 you will trust it. The chapters say this explicitly at M3, M5 and M7, and they mean it.

6  When a week runs long

It will. Assume it. The plan is built for it in three layers, and the skill is using the cheapest layer that solves the problem.

6.1  Layer one: triage inside the week

If Thursday arrives and you are at eight hours with the tests still red, cut in this order. The order is chosen so that what survives is what the next week actually depends on.

Cut firstThenNever cut
The ★★★ exercise.The "Going deeper" reading.The progress log. It is thirty minutes and it is the record.
Re-running the experiment at full parameters (use fast=True and note it).The optional second figure.Getting this week's tests green, because next week's code imports this week's functions.
Polishing the notebook write-up.One or two ★★ exercises, named in the log.The derivation the next milestone will ask you to defend.

Whatever you cut, write down that you cut it, in the log, in one line. An acknowledged gap is a schedule item. An unacknowledged one is a surprise in Week 12.

6.2  Layer two: the buffer weeks

There are fifteen weeks in the schedule and about thirteen weeks of material in it. Week 6 and Week 13 are the difference, and they exist for exactly this situation.

  • Week 6 comes right after Aim 1 closes. If Weeks 1–5 went well, it is a closed-book consolidation plus the bead design — a full and satisfying week. If they did not, it is a catch-up week, and that is its intended use: do the closed-book sheet and the bead-design section only, and pick up the rest in a spare hour of Week 7. Nothing downstream breaks, because no bead is drawn until Week 11.
  • Week 13 comes after all the evidence is in and before a sentence of the report is written. It is the designed place to absorb a Week 12 slip.

Using a buffer as intended is not failure; it is the plan working. What is failure is spending a buffer week half-heartedly on new material while the old material stays broken, because that hides the debt instead of paying it.

6.3  Layer three: the fallback ladder

If a whole milestone slips — M3 in Week 9, M4 in Week 11, M5 in Week 12 — the escalation is the fallback ladder F1–F4 in plans/. It specifies in advance how the project's scope contracts, so that the decision is made from a document rather than from panic in Week 13. Invoking it is a mentor decision and it belongs under "Decisions needed" on the check-in agenda.

The rule about borrowing time Do not borrow time from Week 12. It is the centrepiece — the week where the beads, the simulation and the real data land on one pair of axes — and it is the one week that cannot be compressed. If something has to give in Weeks 9–11, contract scope through the ladder and protect Week 12. The same goes for the Week 14 figure freeze: everything upstream of it is negotiable, and the freeze itself is not.

8  What verification actually looks like, from your side

Every chapter ends with a box verify listing four to six concrete items. At the check-in your mentor works down that list. Here is what that is like in practice, because the phrase "milestone verification" sounds far more formal than the event.

The item saysWhat actually happens
"You state the research question in two sentences, without saying machine learning."You say it out loud. If you hedge, you get asked again. Ninety seconds.
"You reproduce the $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$ derivation at the whiteboard."Marker, blank board, no notes, about ten minutes. You are allowed to get stuck and think. You are asked "why is the denominator a square root?" and you answer without reaching for the page.
"pytest tests/test_detection.py is green and you can say what each test checks."You run it in front of your mentor, then are asked about one test at random: what would break it?
"Your figure is committed with the seed recorded."You open the file in the repository, point at the SEED line, and point at the stamp in the figure's corner.
"You can explain your code line by line."Your mentor picks one function and one line in it, usually a line where something subtle is going on, and asks why it is there.
"Every new claim carries a status label."A claim from your log is read back to you and you say whether it is proved, cited, observed or conjectured, and why.
Being unable to defend something is information, not failure

The point of verification is measurement. If you cannot reproduce the quadrature proof at the board, the check-in has just discovered a specific, fixable gap in Week 5 rather than in Week 14, when the report depends on it and there is no time. That is the system working exactly as designed, and it is why the buffer weeks exist immediately after the two heaviest theory blocks.

What is a failure is bluffing: talking around a step you cannot do, or presenting code you did not write as your own. It is also strictly worse strategy, because the gap stays and now nobody is looking for it. The correct move, always, is the sentence: "I can show this far, and I cannot get past this step." That sentence is a research question, and your mentor can do something useful with it in four minutes.

Bring the stuck points in the same three-line form the progress log and the check-in template both use:

The gap template 
I can show X: ______________________
I need Y:     ______________________
The gap is Z: ______________________

Fill it in before the meeting, using the unstuck protocol. A filled-in template turns "I'm stuck on Week 5" into "I can get the LLR to be linear, I need the variance of a weighted sum, and I do not see why the cross terms vanish" — which is a question with an answer.

The two-week time-box If anything has been stuck for two weeks, it goes on the next check-in agenda automatically — not because you decided it was serious enough, but because two weeks is the rule. The check-in template has a checkbox for it. Most things that take four weeks would have taken twenty minutes of somebody else's time in week one, and the whole purpose of a mentored project is to make that trade.

9  A short honest word about pace

Fifteen weeks is not long, and the middle of a semester is where projects quietly die: the novelty is gone, the report is far away, and one skipped week becomes three. Two things reliably prevent it. The first is the weekly loop above, closed every single week even when the week was bad — a bad week that ends in a committed log is not a lost week. The second is the check-in, which is a fixed external event with a date on it. Neither requires motivation, which is the point.
