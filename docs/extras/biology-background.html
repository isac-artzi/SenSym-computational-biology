Biology background

Everything the chapters assume, and nothing more · what the machine actually does · and the four biological facts that break this project's model

This page assumes high-school biology: you know that DNA carries genes, that cells are the units of living things, and roughly what a protein is. It supplies the rest, and it deliberately stops there. Everything below is here because some chapter needs it; nothing is here for completeness. Where a number is uncertain or contested, this page says so and labels it, because the honest range matters more to this project than a memorable point value.

1  RNA, and what messenger RNA does

A gene is a stretch of DNA. DNA itself never leaves the nucleus and is never used directly to build anything. Instead the cell makes a working copy of a gene in a related molecule, RNA, in a process called transcription. The copy of a protein-coding gene is called messenger RNA (mRNA): it carries the message out of the nucleus to the ribosomes, which read it and build the corresponding protein. That step is translation.

Three consequences matter for this project.

  • mRNA is transient. Molecules are made and destroyed continually, with typical lifetimes of minutes to hours. So the set of mRNA in a cell right now is a snapshot of what the cell is currently doing, not a fixed property like its genome. This is why mRNA is worth measuring: every cell in your body has essentially the same DNA, and it is the RNA that differs.
  • Most RNA in a cell is not mRNA. The large majority is ribosomal RNA, part of the protein-building machinery itself. mRNA is a small fraction of total RNA — commonly quoted as a few percent. cited Sequencing protocols therefore need a way to select the mRNA, which Section 4 describes.
  • mRNA is a proxy for protein, not a measurement of it. The correlation between a gene's mRNA level and its protein level is real but imperfect. When this project says a cell "expresses CD3D", it means mRNA molecules from that gene were counted, and nothing stronger.

2  What "gene expression" means, quantitatively

Informally, a gene is "expressed" in a cell if the cell is making RNA from it. That is a yes/no statement and it is not enough for us. The quantity this project needs is:

Expression, as this project defines it

For a cell, and for each gene $g$, let

$$p_g = \frac{\text{number of mRNA molecules from gene } g}{\text{total number of mRNA molecules in the cell}}.$$

So $p_g$ is a fraction, between 0 and 1, and $\sum_g p_g = 1$ over all genes. Equivalently: $p_g$ is the probability that a molecule picked at random from this cell came from gene $g$.

A well-expressed gene might have $p_g = 0.002$ — two molecules in a thousand. The most extreme genes in a specialized cell can be a percent or more on their own; the great majority of genes are far below $10^{-4}$, and thousands are simply absent at any given moment.

Two things follow from defining expression as a fraction rather than a count.

First, it is exactly the right object for a probability model. "Capture $D$ molecules at random from a cell whose composition is $p$" is a standard sampling problem the moment $p$ is a probability vector, and that is the sentence Week 1 turns into a Poisson.

Second, it is compositional: the fractions must sum to 1, so if one gene goes up, everything else must go down slightly, even if nothing changed about those genes in absolute terms. This is a real subtlety in the field and this project mostly sidesteps it, because the marker genes we use are a tiny share of the total and the induced shifts are negligible. Worth knowing, and worth a sentence in a limitations section, not worth a chapter here.

A definition you will meet elsewhere and should not confuse with ours Published single-cell work almost never reports $p_g$. It reports normalized, log-transformed values — "counts per 10,000, then $\log(1+x)$" is the near-universal default. Those numbers are good for clustering and visualization and catastrophic for us: normalization divides by each cell's library size and turns integers into real numbers whose variance no longer equals their mean, which destroys exactly the Poisson structure this project is about. Week 10 is emphatic about this. Know which of your matrices are raw counts and which are not, and never let them mix.

3  The transcriptome, and how many molecules a cell holds

The transcriptome of a cell is the complete collection of RNA molecules in it at one moment — both the identities and the amounts. It is what a single-cell RNA-seq experiment is trying to sample. Note the word: sample. The transcriptome is the population; your count matrix is a draw from it.

The number that sets the scale of this entire project cited

A typical mammalian cell contains on the order of $10^5$ mRNA molecules. Published estimates commonly fall in the range 50,000 to 300,000, and the spread is not sloppiness: the number genuinely varies with cell type, cell size, and how metabolically active the cell is. A large secretory cell holds far more than a small resting lymphocyte, and the PBMCs used in Week 10 are small, mostly quiescent cells that likely sit at the lower end.

It is also hard to measure, which is why the range is wide. You cannot count the molecules in a cell directly. Estimates come from methods like adding a known quantity of synthetic RNA ("spike-ins") and comparing, and each such method carries its own assumptions about how the synthetic molecules behave relative to real ones.

Treat "about $10^5$" as an order of magnitude, quote a range rather than a point value if anyone asks, and label it cited. Nothing in this project depends on the exact figure — which is a design choice, not luck.

Now put that beside the measurement. A standard droplet experiment counts a couple of thousand molecules per cell. So:

$$\frac{\text{molecules counted}}{\text{molecules present}} \;\approx\; \frac{2{,}000}{100{,}000} \;=\; 2\%.$$

Ninety-eight percent of the cell is never seen. That single ratio is the reason this project exists, and it is the sentence to lead with if you have thirty seconds to explain the work to someone.

4  What single-cell RNA sequencing actually does

The dominant technology, and the one behind PBMC 3k, is droplet-based: 10x Genomics Chromium. Here is the whole process at the level of detail this project needs. You will never perform these steps; you need to know what each one does to the counts.

StepWhat happensDoes it lose molecules?
1. Dissociation The tissue or blood sample is broken into a suspension of single cells. For blood this is comparatively gentle; for solid tissue it is not. It loses cells, and not at random: fragile cell types are destroyed preferentially, and the stress can change what a surviving cell is transcribing.
2. Droplet formation Cells, barcoded gel beads and reaction mix are pushed through a microfluidic junction into oil, making tens of thousands of tiny droplets. The aim is one cell and one bead per droplet. Loses cells (many droplets get no cell). Also creates doublets — two cells in one droplet, which then look like one impossible hybrid cell.
3. Lysis and capture The cell bursts inside its droplet. Its mRNA floats free and binds to the bead's oligos: each oligo ends in a stretch of Ts (oligo-dT) that pairs with the poly-A tail almost every mRNA carries. Yes, heavily. Not every molecule finds an oligo. This is thought to be one of the biggest losses in the whole protocol, and it is not identical for every gene.
4. Reverse transcription An enzyme copies each captured mRNA into cDNA (DNA is far more robust than RNA). The bead's cell barcode and the oligo's UMI are copied along with it. Yes. Reverse transcriptase is efficient but not perfect, and it falls off long or structured molecules more often.
5. Amplification (PCR) The emulsion is broken, everything is pooled, and the cDNA is copied many times over so there is enough material to sequence. Not directly, but it distorts: some molecules are copied far more than others. This is precisely the distortion UMIs were invented to undo (Section 5).
6. Library preparation and sequencing Fragments are prepared and run on a sequencer. One read gives the cell barcode and UMI; the other gives a piece of the transcript, from the 3' end. Yes, at finite sequencing depth: a molecule present in the library but never sequenced is a molecule you do not have. This is the "reads per cell" budget line.
7. Alignment and counting Reads are matched to genes; reads sharing a cell barcode and a UMI are collapsed to one molecule. The output is the count matrix: cells down the rows, genes across the columns, integers inside. Loses ambiguous reads (multi-mapping, barcodes with errors). Also merges genuinely distinct molecules that happened to draw the same UMI — a UMI collision.

Every "yes" in the right-hand column is a place where molecules disappear between the cell and your matrix. Multiply them together and you get the capture efficiency of Section 7.

Two consequences of the 3' protocol worth knowing

Only the 3' end is sequenced. 10x counts molecules per gene; it does not read the whole transcript, so it generally cannot tell which splice isoform a molecule was. That is fine here — this project is about counting, not about isoforms — but it is a real limitation of the data, and saying so when asked is better than discovering it in the Q&A.

Capture depends on the poly-A tail. RNAs without one are largely invisible to the protocol. That is a systematic gap, not a random loss, and it is one of the reasons the phrase "the transcriptome" is a slight overstatement of what you have measured.

5  What a UMI is, and why nothing works without it

Step 5 above is a problem. PCR amplifies molecules unevenly — one molecule might end up with 200 copies and its neighbour with 20, for reasons having to do with sequence and luck. If you simply counted reads, you would be measuring the amplification, not the biology.

Definition · UMI (unique molecular identifier)

A UMI is a short random barcode — a stretch of about a dozen random bases — attached to each mRNA molecule before amplification. Every oligo on the bead carries a different one.

Amplification copies the UMI along with the molecule. So afterwards, all the reads descended from one original molecule carry the same UMI, and you can collapse them back to a single count. Counting distinct UMIs counts original molecules; counting reads counts PCR.

This is why UMIs are load-bearing for this project specifically, and not just a technical nicety. Everything from Week 1 onwards depends on the counts being counts of molecules sampled from the cell, so that the Poisson law applies and the variance equals the mean. Read counts have no such property: their variance is inflated by amplification noise, and a Fano factor computed on them would measure the PCR machine.

The caveat, which belongs in your limitations section "One UMI equals one molecule" is standard and approximately right, not exact. Two different molecules of the same gene in the same cell can draw the same random barcode (a UMI collision), which undercounts, and this gets worse for very highly expressed genes. Sequencing errors inside a barcode can split one molecule into two apparent ones, which overcounts. Standard pipelines correct for both, and the residual error is generally small at PBMC 3k's counts per gene. cited Say the sentence out loud before anyone asks you: "I am treating UMI counts as molecule counts, which is standard and approximately right."

6  What a cell type is, honestly

A cell type is a group of cells that share a function, an origin, and a characteristic pattern of gene expression: T cells, monocytes, neurons, hepatocytes. Immunologists have classified blood cell types for decades using surface proteins, and those classifications are real and useful.

For this project, though, the working definition is narrower and entirely quantitative:

A cell type, as this project uses the term A cell type is a probability distribution over which gene the next transcript belongs to: a vector $p^{(1)}$ or $p^{(2)}$ over genes, summing to 1. Two types are distinguishable exactly to the extent that those two vectors differ.

That definition makes the whole project possible, and it is also a simplification with a name attached, so name it now:

The boundary between cell types is fuzzy, and often there is no boundary
  • Cells of one type are not identical. Two T cells from the same donor have genuinely different expression profiles: different points in the cell cycle, different recent stimulation, different sizes. The model says they share one $p$ vector; they do not. This is the assumption Week 11 kills, and it is what overdispersion is.
  • Many differences are continuous, not categorical. Cells differentiate along trajectories, and activation states grade smoothly. Where along a continuum you draw a line and call one side "type A" is a decision, sometimes a useful one and sometimes an artefact of the clustering algorithm's settings.
  • Types nest. "T cell" contains CD4 and CD8, which contain naive, memory and effector states, which contain finer distinctions still. The right level of granularity depends on the question, and the answer to "how many cell types are there?" is "at what resolution?".

This project studies two well-separated types — CD14+ monocytes and CD4 T cells — precisely so that these difficulties are not the limiting factor. That is a legitimate simplification and it needs to be stated as one, out loud, in the talk. The claim being tested is about how accuracy scales with depth, not about how hard cell typing is in general.

Where the labels in PBMC 3k come from, and why it matters

Nobody looked at those cells and identified them. There is no independent measurement of what type each barcode is: no flow sorting, no imaging, no second assay. The labels are produced by computing on the same counts you then analyse — normalize, log, pick highly variable genes, PCA, build a neighbour graph, run Leiden clustering, then name each cluster after whichever marker panel it scores higher on.

So every real-data conclusion in this project is conditional on those annotations being right, and that clause belongs in the report as a clause, not a footnote. It is also exactly why the project has a simulation arm (where you chose each cell's type) and a bead arm (where the tally sheet recorded the jar): those are the two arms in which the truth is known rather than inferred.

7  Marker genes

A marker gene is a gene whose expression differs substantially between the cell types you are trying to tell apart. In this project's language: a gene where $p_g^{(1)}$ and $p_g^{(2)}$ are noticeably different. Markers are how cell types are recognized in practice, and the panels for common blood cells have been known for years.

GeneMarksWhat the protein does, roughly
LYZMonocytes (and other myeloid cells)Lysozyme: an enzyme that attacks bacterial cell walls. Very highly expressed — one of the strongest signals in the dataset.
S100A8, S100A9Monocytes, neutrophilsThe two halves of calprotectin, an abundant antimicrobial and inflammatory protein.

The monocyte-versus-T-cell pair is deliberately easy. The genes above are strongly and consistently different between the two populations, which keeps the classification accuracy inside the range where a scaling law can be measured: a hopeless pair would sit at chance at every depth, and a trivial pair at 1 everywhere. Neither can show you a curve.

Fold change is not $d'$, and this is where the biology and the statistics part company Biologists rank markers by fold change: how many times higher a gene's expression is in one type than the other. A gene at $p = 10^{-6}$ against $p = 10^{-7}$ has a spectacular tenfold change and contributes essentially nothing to classifying a single cell, because at a depth of 2,000 molecules its expected count is 0.002 against 0.0002 and you will almost always see zero from both. $d'$ knows this and fold change does not, because $d'$ has $\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$ in its denominator. Week 10 makes you compute both rankings on real data and measure how much they disagree.

8  Capture efficiency and dropout

Section 4 listed the steps at which molecules go missing. Their combined effect has a name.

Capture efficiency, and how uncertain it is cited

The molecular capture efficiency is the fraction of a cell's mRNA molecules that end up as counted UMIs. For droplet protocols it is usually quoted as somewhere in the range of roughly 10% to 30%. So a cell containing 300,000 transcripts contributes something like 30,000 to 90,000 counted molecules — before you account for how deeply the library was actually sequenced, which is what brings a typical PBMC 3k cell down to a couple of thousand.

Treat that range with suspicion and say so out loud. The number is protocol-dependent (plate-based protocols such as Smart-seq are generally credited with higher efficiency than droplet ones), estimate-dependent (most measurements come from spiked-in synthetic RNA of known concentration, which may not behave like cellular mRNA), and version-dependent within a single vendor's chemistry. Published values for nominally the same platform differ by more than a factor of two. See Svensson et al. (2017) and Zheng et al. (2017) in the resource library.

Dropout is the word the field uses for the consequence: a gene that is genuinely being expressed shows up as a zero in a particular cell, because none of its molecules happened to be captured. In this project dropout is written as a probability $q$ that a given molecule is lost, and the keep probability is $1-q$.

Why the uncertainty in that 10–30% figure does not sink the project

Week 9 proves — from the thinning theorem — that losing each molecule independently with probability $q$ produces exactly the distribution you would get by sequencing at depth $D(1-q)$ with no losses at all. Dropout is not a new phenomenon; it is the depth axis with different tick labels.

So you do not need to know $q$ in order to test the theory. Whatever it is, it slides you along a curve whose shape is the thing being predicted. That is a small piece of research design worth appreciating: an honest admission of ignorance about a parameter was made harmless by arranging that no conclusion depends on it.

The assumption that does the work is that the loss probability is the same for every molecule — the same for every gene, every expression level, every cell. Section 9 says why that is the suspicious part.

9  The four biological facts that break this project's model

The model is: a cell type is a fixed probability vector, genes are independent, capture is a uniform random sample, and the labels are known. All four are false. That is not a defect to be hidden; the schedule is built around measuring how false each one is, and by Week 14 this table is the skeleton of your limitations section.

#The biological factWhat it breaksAttacked in
1 Cells of one type differ from one another. Cell size, cell-cycle stage, recent stimulation and micro-environment all shift a cell's expression profile, so there is no single $p^{(1)}$ shared by all type-1 cells. Poisson counts. Mixing Poissons with varying rates gives $\mathrm{Var} = m + \varphi m^2$ — a negative binomial, Fano factor above 1. $d'$ then saturates at $|p_1-p_2|/(\bar p\sqrt\varphi)$ and deeper sequencing stops buying anything past a knee at mean count $1/\varphi$. Week 11, where you measure $F$ and $\hat\varphi$ on real PBMC counts and derive the ceiling. The headline negative result of the project.
2 Genes are co-regulated. Genes work in programmes: transcription factors switch sets of genes together, and the monocyte markers in Section 7 rise and fall as a group rather than independently. The quadrature step. Adding $d'$ values in quadrature needs independence across genes; with correlated markers the tenth gene repeats what the first nine already said, and the true $d'$ grows more slowly than $\sqrt k$. Week 5 names the assumption where it is used and shows that a fixed marker budget cut into more pieces gains exactly nothing; Week 10 measures the shortfall on real panels as $k$ grows.
3 Capture is gene-dependent. Oligo-dT capture depends on the poly-A tail; reverse transcription depends on length, GC content and secondary structure. Molecules are not lost with one uniform probability. The thinning theorem's third assumption, and with it the clean claim that dropout is just reduced depth. Gene-dependent capture is a per-gene rescaling of $p_g$, which also means the rates you estimate are the rates as captured, not as present. Week 9, which states the assumption explicitly in the proof and argues about how suspicious it is; Week 10's rate estimation inherits the same caveat.
4 The labels come from clustering. Cell-type annotations in PBMC 3k are computed from the same counts you go on to classify, using a pipeline with tunable settings. The meaning of "accuracy" itself. A cell with atypical counts is likely to be clustered wrongly and classified wrongly, so the two errors correlate and cancel, biasing measured accuracy upwards. The absolute number is optimistic. Week 10, which is blunt about it and gives you the 150-word answer to the reviewer's question; Week 12, which puts the three arms' truth sources side by side; Week 13, where the claim ledger records what is conditional on what.
Why the project survives all four

Because the claim being tested is about a slope, not a level. Facts 3 and 4 shift the accuracy curve up or down; they do not change how it scales with depth, because the depth sweep downsamples the same cells with the same fixed labels. Fact 2 costs you some of the $\sqrt k$ and is measurable as a shortfall. Only fact 1 attacks the exponent itself — which is precisely why it is the interesting one, and why it gets a whole chapter rather than a caveat.

Knowing which of your defects damages your headline claim, and which merely make the numbers less pretty, is most of what separates a strong limitations section from an apologetic one.
