Notation card

Every symbol in the project · what it means · where it is introduced · and its name in the code

Print this, or keep it open in a second tab. Two thirds of the confusion in a mathematical project is not conceptual: it is not remembering whether $\lambda$ was a rate per cell or per gene, or whether $q$ was the probability a molecule survives or the probability it is lost. This page is the single place where each of those questions has an answer.

The last column matters as much as the others. A symbol that has a Python name has a definition you can execute and a test that pins it down; keeping the two vocabularies aligned is what stops a derivation and its implementation from quietly drifting apart.

1  Conventions

Read these four rules once and most of the table becomes predictable
  • Subscript $g$ indexes genes. $p_g$ is one gene's share of the transcriptome; $x_g$ is one gene's observed count; $\lambda_g$ is one gene's expected count. A symbol with no $g$ is either a scalar or a whole vector over genes, and the sentence around it will say which.
  • Superscript $(1)$ and $(2)$ indexes cell types. $p^{(1)}$ is type 1's whole expression profile. Where both indices appear the type comes first and the parentheses are often dropped in subscripts: $\lambda_{1g}$ is "type 1, gene $g$". In code the two types are rows 0 and 1 of a profile matrix, and the labels in y are 0 and 1 — so type 1 in the maths is index 0 in the code. Every chapter that mixes them says so; do not let it bite you at a check-in.
  • A bar means "pooled over the two types". $\bar\lambda = (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)/2$, $\bar p = (p_1 + p_2)/2$. It is an average over types, never over cells or genes.
  • A hat means "estimated from data". $\hat d'$ is the separation index measured from a sample; $d'$ is the one the theory predicts. $\hat\varphi$ is the method-of-moments dispersion estimate; $\varphi$ is the quantity it is trying to estimate. When you compare a hat to a non-hat you are comparing measurement to prediction, which is what the entire project consists of.

Two smaller conventions. $\log$ with no base is the natural logarithm throughout (the only base-10 logs are on log–log plot axes, where they are written $\log_{10}$ explicitly). And a primed symbol is not a derivative anywhere in this project: $d'$ is a single name, pronounced "d-prime", inherited from the psychophysics literature of the 1950s.

2  The master table

SymbolMeaningIntroducedPython name
$D$ Depth, also called library size: the number of molecules counted from one cell. A few thousand for 10x data. Week 1 depth (argument of simulate_cells, expected_rates); measured from data by total_counts(matrix)
$D_{\text{eff}}$ Effective depth after dropout, $D_{\text{eff}} = D(1-q)$. The thinning theorem says a dropout experiment is a shallower experiment. Week 9 depth * (1 - dropout), computed inside expected_rates
$p_g$ The fraction of a cell's transcripts belonging to gene $g$; equivalently the probability that a randomly captured molecule is gene $g$. Sums to 1 over all genes. Week 1 entries of profile; the arrays p1, p2 returned by marker_profiles
$p^{(1)}, p^{(2)}$ The two cell types' expression profiles, as vectors over genes. In this project a "cell type" is such a vector. Week 1 profiles, a (2, n_genes) array
$\bar p$ Pooled fraction, $(p_1 + p_2)/2$, per gene. Week 3 — (appears inside d_prime)
$\lambda$ Poisson rate = expected count. For one gene in one cell, $\lambda = D p_g$. Mean and variance are both $\lambda$. Week 1 lam, lam1, lam2
$\lambda_{cg}$ Expected count of gene $g$ in a cell of type $c$: $\lambda_{cg} = D\,p_g^{(c)}$. Week 5 rows of expected_rates(profiles, depth, dropout); estimated from real data by estimate_rates(X, y)
$\bar\lambda$ Pooled expected count, $(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/2$ — the pooled variance, and therefore the right noise scale. Week 3 — (the denominator inside d_prime)
$k$ Number of marker genes in the panel. The $\sqrt{k}$ of the headline law. see the collision warning in Section 4 Week 5 n_genes in marker_profiles; n_markers in select_markers
$x_g$ The observed count for gene $g$ in one cell — an integer, the thing the sequencer actually hands you. Week 1 an entry of X; one cell is a row X[i]
$X$ The count matrix, cells $\times$ genes. Raw integers, never normalized. Week 7 X (from simulate_dataset or load_pbmc)
$y$ Cell-type labels, one per cell, values 0 and 1. Week 8 y
$F$ Fano factor, variance divided by mean. Exactly 1 for Poisson data; above 1 means overdispersed. Week 1 fano_factor(samples); per gene on a matrix, gene_fano(X)
$q$ Dropout probability: the probability a captured molecule is lost. $q = 0.6$ means 60% of molecules disappear. Week 9 dropout (in apply_dropout, simulate_cells, bead_d_prime)
$1-q$ Keep (survival) probability. The thinning theorem is stated in terms of this, not of $q$. Week 2 keep_prob (in downsample_counts, downsample_matrix)
$s$ Generic survival probability in the statement of the thinning theorem: $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ thinned by $s$ is $\mathrm{Poisson}(s\lambda)$. Week 2 — (instantiated as keep_prob)
$d'$ The separation index, $d' = |\lambda_1-\lambda_2| / \sqrt{\bar\lambda}$: a difference of means measured in units of its own noise. The centre of the project. Week 3 d_prime(lam1, lam2)
$d'_g$ The separation index contributed by a single gene $g$. Week 5 an element of the array d_prime returns when given vectors
$d'_{\text{total}}$ Separation from the whole panel: independent genes add in quadrature, $d'_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{\sum_g (d'_g)^2}$, giving $\sqrt{k}$ for $k$ equal genes. Week 5 combine_d_prime(dprimes); from rates directly, d_prime_total(lam1, lam2)
$d'_\infty$ The ceiling on $d'$ under overdispersion: $d'_\infty = |p_1-p_2| / (\bar p \sqrt{\varphi})$. No depth appears in it. Week 11 — (the large-$D$ limit of d_prime_overdispersed)
$\hat d'$ The separation index measured from a sample: $|\bar x_1 - \bar x_2| / \sqrt{\tfrac12(s_1^2+s_2^2)}$, with the sample variances at ddof=1. Week 3 computed inline in experiments/week03_dprime_depth.py
$\Phi$ Standard normal CDF: $\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z)$ for $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$. Accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$. Week 4 normal_cdf(z); in JavaScript, CD.Phi(z)
$\mathrm{erf}$ The error function, $\mathrm{erf}(u) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^u e^{-t^2}dt$. Related to $\Phi$ by $\Phi(z) = \tfrac12\big(1 + \mathrm{erf}(z/\sqrt2)\big)$ — bookkeeping, not new mathematics. Week 4 scipy.special.erf; its inverse erfinv is used by d_prime_from_auc; in JavaScript CD.erf(x)
$\varphi$ Negative-binomial dispersion: the excess variance parameter in $\mathrm{Var} = m + \varphi m^2$. $\varphi = 0$ is Poisson. read Section 4 before using this symbol Week 11 argument phi of d_prime_overdispersed(lam1, lam2, phi)
$\hat\varphi$ Method-of-moments estimate of $\varphi$ from data: $\hat\varphi = (s^2 - \bar x)/\bar x^2$. The raw value can come out negative, which is not an error but a gene that is Poisson within noise; the function clips at 0. Week 11 negative_binomial_dispersion(X)
$\mathrm{LLR}$ Log-likelihood ratio, $\log \frac{P(x\mid 1)}{P(x\mid 2)}$. For Poisson genes it is linear in the counts: $\sum_g\big[x_g\log(\lambda_{1g}/\lambda_{2g}) - (\lambda_{1g}-\lambda_{2g})\big]$. Positive means type 1. Week 5 log_likelihood_ratio(X, lam1, lam2); in JavaScript CD.nbLogRatio(x, l1, l2)
$w$, $b$ Weight vector and offset of a linear decision rule, "call type 1 if $w\cdot x + b > 0$". For naive Bayes, $w_g = \log(\lambda_{1g}/\lambda_{2g})$ and $b = -\sum_g(\lambda_{1g}-\lambda_{2g})$. Week 5 clf.w, clf.b on a fitted LogisticRegression
$\pi_1, \pi_2$ Prior probabilities of the two types. Equal priors ($\pi_1=\pi_2=\tfrac12$) put the decision threshold at $\mathrm{LLR} = 0$; unequal priors move it to $\log(\pi_2/\pi_1)$. Week 4 priors argument of posterior_log_odds(X, rates, priors)
AUC Area under the ROC curve: the probability that a random type-1 cell scores above a random type-2 cell. Related to $d'$ by $\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi(d'/\sqrt2)$. Week 4 roc_auc(scores, labels); inverted by d_prime_from_auc(auc)
TPR, FPR True- and false-positive rates: the vertical and horizontal axes of the ROC curve. Week 4 the two arrays returned by roc_curve(scores, labels)
$z$ A standard-normal quantile used as a multiplier for confidence intervals. $z = 1.96$ gives 95% because $\Phi(1.96) = 0.975$ and $2(1-0.975) = 0.05$. Also written $z$ for a generic standardized value. Week 13 z argument of wilson_interval(k, n, z=1.96) and trials_needed(p_true, half_width, z=1.96)
$\alpha$ Significance level; $\alpha = 0.05$ pairs with $z = 1.96$. Divided by the number of tests under a Bonferroni correction. Week 13 alpha in bonferroni(alpha, n_tests)
$p_A, p_B$ Bead fractions: the proportion of red beads in jar A and jar B. The planned design is $p_A = 0.30$ against $p_B = 0.35$ or $0.40$. Week 6 p_a, p_b in bead_d_prime(n, p_a, p_b, dropout) and predicted_accuracy
$n$ (beads) The number of beads drawn in one trial. It plays exactly the role $D$ plays for cells: $d'(n) = \sqrt n\,|p_A-p_B| / \sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)}$. Week 6 n in bead_d_prime, and the n column of a tally CSV
$n$ (sample size) The number of independent observations behind a statistic: cells in a Fano estimate, trials in a proportion. collision — see Section 4 Weeks 1, 13 n in wilson_interval(k, n, z); n_samples, n_cells, n_trials elsewhere
$f$ Fold change between the two types on a marker gene, $f = p^{(1)}_g / p^{(2)}_g$. A 1.5-fold marker is modest; a 10-fold marker is spectacular and rare. Week 7 fold_change in marker_profiles(n_genes, fold_change, marker_mass)
$m$ Marker mass: the fraction of the transcriptome the whole marker panel accounts for, averaged over the two types. Default 0.02. Week 7 marker_mass
$N$ Number of cells in a dataset (as opposed to molecules in a cell). Cells and depth are interchangeable for estimating a population quantity and not for classifying one cell. Week 1 n_cells, n_cells_per_type, n_samples
$\mu$, $\sigma^2$, $\sigma$ Generic mean, variance and standard deviation. For Poisson counts $\mu = \sigma^2 = \lambda$, which is the coincidence the whole project rests on. Week 1 .mean(), .var(ddof=1), .std(ddof=1)
$E[\cdot]$, $\mathrm{Var}(\cdot)$ Expectation and variance. $E$ is linear always; variances add only for independent terms, and that asymmetry is what the $\sqrt k$ law turns on. Weeks 1–2 —
$h$ Half-width of a target confidence interval, used when planning how many bead trials to run. Week 6 half_width in trials_needed(p_true, half_width, z)
$j^\star$ The bead-count cut: call jar B if the number of red beads exceeds $j^\star$. The exact optimal cut from the likelihood ratio, which is very close to $\bar p\, n$. Week 6 — (the decision card in the Week 6 chapter)

4  Collisions and false friends

Four places where one glyph means two things, or two glyphs look alike. Every one of them has caused a real error somewhere; read them once now and again before Week 11.

$\varphi$ is not $\Phi$, and both appear in Week 11

$\Phi$ (capital, "Phi") is the standard normal CDF. It is a function; it eats a number and returns a probability between 0 and 1. It appears in $\text{accuracy} = \Phi(d'/2)$.

$\varphi$ (lower case, "phi") is the negative-binomial dispersion parameter. It is a number, typically between 0 and about 2 for real gene counts, and it says how much a gene's variance exceeds its mean: $\mathrm{Var} = m + \varphi m^2$.

They have nothing to do with each other, and Week 11 contains sentences with both in them — for instance, the accuracy at the ceiling is $\Phi(d'_\infty/2)$ with $d'_\infty = |p_1-p_2|/(\bar p\sqrt{\varphi})$. This course writes the dispersion as \varphi ($\varphi$) rather than \phi ($\phi$) precisely to widen the visual gap. Note that the literature is not so careful: papers write the same dispersion as $\phi$, $\alpha$, or $\theta$ (and some use $\theta = 1/\varphi$, so the larger value means less variability). Whenever you read a negative-binomial formula elsewhere, find the variance equation before trusting the letter.

In code there is no ambiguity: $\Phi$ is normal_cdf and $\varphi$ is the argument phi. If you are unsure which one a line of maths means, ask which function you would call.

$n$ means two different things, and context disambiguates

$n$ = beads drawn in one trial (Weeks 6 and 12). This is a quantity of data per decision. It is the bead experiment's analogue of $D$, and it is the axis of the headline bead plot: accuracy against $n$, predicted to rise like $\Phi(\sqrt n\,c/2)$.

$n$ = sample size (Weeks 1, 6, 13). This is a quantity of repetitions: how many cells went into a Fano estimate, how many trials went into a measured proportion. It controls the width of your error bar, not the accuracy itself.

How to tell them apart: ask what gets bigger when $n$ does. If the accuracy improves, $n$ is beads per draw. If the error bar shrinks while the accuracy stays where it is, $n$ is a sample size. In the bead chapters both are present in one sentence — "40 trials at each of $n = 10, 25, 50, 100, 200$" — and there the sample size is always called trials. In code they never collide, because the tally CSV column is n and the interval function's is also n but they are never passed to the same function.

$k$ means three things, two of them only in code

In the mathematics, $k$ is the number of marker genes — the $\sqrt k$ of the scaling law. That is the only meaning in the chapters' derivations.

In the code it is also: the number of successes in wilson_interval(k, n, z) (as in "$k$ correct out of $n$ trials"); the count value at which a pmf is evaluated in poisson_pmf(k, lam); and the number of neighbours in knn_predict(..., k=5). All three are standard conventions in their own contexts, which is why they were kept.

If you find yourself writing $\sqrt k$ in a comment above a call to wilson_interval, stop and rename a variable.

$q$ versus keep_prob: the inversion that produces a plausible wrong figure

$q$ is the probability a molecule is lost. keep_prob is the probability it survives. They sum to 1, and apply_dropout(X, dropout, rng) exists precisely to do the subtraction once, in one place: it calls downsample_counts(X, 1 - dropout, rng).

Get it backwards and nothing crashes. The counts are still integers, the curve is still smooth, the tests you happen to run may still pass — and every point is at the wrong depth. This is the single most likely silent error in Weeks 9 and 11. The check that catches it in ten seconds: set dropout to 0.9 and confirm the counts get much smaller, not much larger.
