Problem bank

Consolidation problems by topic and difficulty — for buffer weeks, for check-in preparation, and for the evenings when a chapter has not landed

These are not the weekly exercises. Those are in the chapters and they are mandatory. These are extra, and they exist for three specific situations: a buffer week (Week 6, Week 13) with time to fill; the two days before a milestone check-in; and the evening after a chapter that did not quite land, when the honest thing to do is more problems rather than more reading.

How to use this page

Difficulty: ★ routine, you should be able to do it in five minutes · ★★ requires an idea or a decision · ★★★ challenge — several are open-ended, several require you to notice that the question as asked is ill-posed, and a few have no single right answer.

Problems marked check-in are the ones most likely to be recognizable in a milestone conversation. If you are short of time, do those.

Three kinds of problem appear here that do not appear in the chapters. Find the error problems give you a piece of confident, plausible, wrong reasoning and ask you to locate the flaw — this is the skill a reviewer uses on you, and practising it from the other side is the fastest way to acquire it. Design problems give you a budget and ask what to spend it on; they have defensible answers rather than correct ones, and the defence is the answer. Ill-posed problems look like calculations and are not.

Attempt before opening the solution. A worked solution read before an attempt teaches roughly nothing; read after a real attempt it teaches a great deal. This is not a moral point, it is an empirical one about how memory works.

Values of $\Phi$ used throughout are from a standard normal table to four places; your code will agree to the last digit or two. Where a solution quotes a number your Python will also produce, it is worth checking one against the other — a disagreement between your hand calculation and your code means one of them is wrong, and finding out which is the entire habit this project is trying to build.

A  Counting statistics and the Poisson

A.1★
A gene makes up $0.15\%$ of a cell's transcripts. The sequencer captures $D = 4000$ molecules. Give the expected count, the standard deviation, and the relative noise.
Show solution

$\lambda = Dp = 4000 \times 0.0015 = 6$. Poisson variance equals the mean, so $\sigma = \sqrt{6} = 2.449$, and the relative noise is $2.449/6 = 0.408$, i.e. 40.8%.

Note that $p = 0.0015$ makes this a well-expressed gene. The great majority of genes in a real cell are an order of magnitude rarer than this, and come back as a zero or a one. This single number is why the project is not trivial.

A.2★
Five cells give counts $3, 7, 5, 9, 6$ for one gene. Compute the sample mean, the unbiased sample variance, and the Fano factor. Is this evidence of underdispersion?
Show solution

Mean $= 30/5 = 6$. Deviations $-3, 1, -1, 3, 0$; squares $9, 1, 1, 9, 0$, summing to $20$. The unbiased variance divides by $n - 1 = 4$: $s^2 = 5$. So $F = 5/6 = 0.833$.

No. The Fano factor of a five-cell sample is itself a random variable with standard deviation roughly $\sqrt{2/n} = \sqrt{0.4} = 0.632$. An observed $0.833$ is $0.26$ standard deviations below $1$, which is what a coin flip looks like. You have measured nothing.

If you divided by $n = 5$ instead you got $F = 0.667$, which looks much more like underdispersion and is a bias, not a finding. counting.fano_factor uses ddof=1 for this reason, and a test in tests/test_counting.py is calibrated to catch it.

A.3★★
You want the relative noise on a gene at $p = 5 \times 10^{-4}$ down to $5\%$. What depth does that require? Compare with a typical 10x library size of a few thousand UMIs, and say what the comparison implies about single-cell measurements of individual genes.
Show solution

Relative noise is $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$, so $5\%$ needs $\lambda = 400$, and $D = \lambda/p = 400 / (5\times10^{-4}) = 800{,}000$ molecules captured per cell.

A 10x cell yields a few thousand. You are asking for roughly $250\times$ the depth that exists, and no amount of money buys it — the limit is how many molecules can be captured from one cell, not how many reads you pay for.

Implication: a single-cell measurement of a single gene in a single cell is not a measurement of that gene's expression in any useful sense. It is one draw from a distribution. Everything in single-cell analysis that appears to be precise is precise because it pools — over cells, over genes, or both. That is why this project is about detection (a decision from one noisy sample) rather than about estimation.

A.4★★ check-in
Find the error. A student writes: "For a Poisson variable the variance equals the mean. So the relative noise is $\text{variance}/\text{mean} = \lambda/\lambda = 1$: every Poisson count has 100% relative noise, no matter how deep you sequence. Depth cannot help." Locate the error precisely and give the correct statement.
Show solution

The error is in one word: they used the variance where the standard deviation belongs. "Relative noise" means $\text{sd}/\text{mean}$, not $\text{variance}/\text{mean}$. The quantity they computed, $\text{variance}/\text{mean}$, is the Fano factor, which is indeed exactly $1$ for a Poisson — that part is right, and it is why the mistake is seductive.

Correctly: $\text{sd} = \sqrt{\lambda}$, so $$\frac{\text{sd}}{\text{mean}} = \frac{\sqrt\lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\sqrt\lambda},$$ which falls as depth rises. At $\lambda = 6$ it is $41\%$; at $\lambda = 600$ it is $4.1\%$.

This is worth doing because the two quantities — Fano factor and relative noise — are both "spread over centre", both equal to something memorable for a Poisson, and constantly confused. The Fano factor is dimensionally strange (it has units of counts) and is useful precisely because it is 1 for a Poisson; the relative noise is dimensionless and is what determines whether you can see anything. See the commonly confused pairs in the glossary.

A.5★★★
Prove that if $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_1)$ and $Y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_2)$ are independent, then $X + Y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$. Then: the library size of a cell is the sum of its counts over all genes. Does the theorem imply that library sizes are Poisson distributed with mean $D$? Answer carefully.
Show solution

The proof. Condition on how the total splits:

$$P(X + Y = n) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} P(X = k)\,P(Y = n-k) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{e^{-\lambda_1}\lambda_1^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{e^{-\lambda_2}\lambda_2^{\,n-k}}{(n-k)!}.$$

Pull out $e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}$ and multiply and divide by $n!$:

$$= \frac{e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\lambda_1^{k}\lambda_2^{\,n-k} = \frac{e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^{n}}{n!},$$

by the binomial theorem. That is the Poisson pmf at rate $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. ∎

The second part, which is the real question. Formally the theorem gives you the answer you expect: if each gene's count is independently $\mathrm{Poisson}(Dp_g)$ and $\sum_g p_g = 1$, then the total is $\mathrm{Poisson}(D)$. But the independence assumption is doing something suspicious. The physical process is: a fixed-ish number of molecules gets captured, and each captured molecule is assigned to one gene. That is a multinomial, in which the counts are negatively correlated (a molecule counted for gene $g$ is not available for gene $h$) and the total is fixed by construction.

The two pictures are reconciled by a standard fact: a multinomial is exactly what you get from independent Poissons conditioned on their total. So the independent-Poisson model is the unconditional version, and it predicts a random library size; the multinomial model is the conditional version, and it fixes it.

Which is right depends on the experiment. In practice, real library sizes vary enormously across cells — far more than $\mathrm{Poisson}(D)$ allows, because cells differ in size, capture efficiency and RNA content. So neither idealization is literally true, and this is one of the assumptions that Week 11 comes back and breaks.

Worth doing because the standard convention in this project — condition on the observed library size and treat per-gene counts as independent Poissons at rate $D p_g$ — is a modelling choice, not a theorem, and being able to say which parts of it are which is exactly what a "state your assumptions" line is for.

B  The thinning theorem

B.1★
A count is $\mathrm{Poisson}(50)$. Each molecule is independently kept with probability $0.4$. Give the distribution, mean, variance and relative noise of the kept count.
Show solution

By the thinning theorem the kept count is $\mathrm{Poisson}(0.4 \times 50) = \mathrm{Poisson}(20)$: mean $20$, variance $20$, sd $\sqrt{20} = 4.472$, relative noise $4.472/20 = 22.4\%$.

The point to notice: the result is still Poisson. It is not "Poisson with extra loss noise". Thinning a Poisson does not add a second source of randomness on top; it produces exactly the distribution you would have got from a shallower experiment in the first place.

B.2★★ check-in
Prove the thinning theorem: if $N \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ and, given $N = n$, $Y \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, c)$, then $Y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(c\lambda)$.
Show solution

Sum over the possible totals, of which only $n \ge k$ contribute:

$$P(Y = k) = \sum_{n \ge k} P(N = n)\binom{n}{k}c^{k}(1-c)^{n-k} = \sum_{n \ge k} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{n}}{n!}\cdot\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}c^{k}(1-c)^{n-k}.$$

The $n!$ cancels. Substitute $m = n - k$:

$$= \frac{e^{-\lambda}(\lambda c)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{m \ge 0}\frac{\big(\lambda(1-c)\big)^{m}}{m!} = \frac{e^{-\lambda}(\lambda c)^{k}}{k!}\,e^{\lambda(1-c)} = \frac{e^{-\lambda c}(\lambda c)^{k}}{k!}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

Assumptions used: the original count is Poisson; each molecule is kept or lost independently of the others; and — the one that matters biologically — the keep probability does not depend on which gene the molecule belongs to.

This one theorem does three separate jobs in the project: it justifies downsampling real data as a stand-in for shallower sequencing (Weeks 2 and 10), it proves dropout is equivalent to reduced depth (Week 9), and it is the reason the bead experiment's dropout arm should collapse onto the plain arm when plotted against $n(1-q)$ (Week 12). If you can produce it at a whiteboard you can defend a third of the project.

B.3★★
Explain why downsampling a deep dataset is a legitimate stand-in for having run a shallower experiment — and name three things it does not reproduce.
Show solution

Why it is legitimate. By B.2, binomially thinning a Poisson count gives a Poisson count at the reduced rate. So at the level of an individual gene's count in an individual cell, a downsampled deep measurement and a genuinely shallow measurement have exactly the same distribution — not approximately, not in the limit, exactly. That is a strong statement and it is what makes the depth sweep in Week 10 an experiment rather than an analogy.

Three things it does not reproduce:

  1. Protocol-level effects. A genuinely shallower run has different PCR duplication rates, a different fraction of reads lost to sequencing quality filters, and possibly different cell-calling behaviour. Downsampling a finished count matrix reproduces none of that; it starts after all of it has happened.
  2. Ambient RNA and background. The contaminating background in a droplet does not scale the way you would want it to; downsampling scales signal and background together, whereas a shallower real experiment might not.
  3. Overdispersion. If the original counts are not Poisson — and Week 11 shows they are not — then the thinning theorem's premise fails. Thinning still reduces the mean, but it does not restore Poisson behaviour. B.4 makes this precise.

The honest sentence for the report: "downsampling reproduces the sampling statistics of a shallower experiment exactly, under the Poisson model, and reproduces nothing about the protocol." Both halves.

B.4★★★
Suppose a gene's count is gamma–Poisson (negative binomial) with mean $m$ and variance $m + \varphi m^{2}$. Thin it with keep probability $c$. Show that the Fano factor falls but the dispersion $\varphi$ is unchanged. What does that imply about which quantity to report?
Show solution

Model the gamma–Poisson as $X \mid M \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(M)$ with $E[M] = m$ and $\mathrm{Var}(M) = \varphi m^{2}$. Thinning acts on the Poisson layer: given $M$, the kept count is $\mathrm{Poisson}(cM)$. So the thinned variable is a gamma–Poisson with latent rate $cM$, which has

$$E[cM] = cm, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(cM) = c^{2}\varphi m^{2} = \varphi (cm)^{2}.$$

The thinned count therefore has mean $cm$ and variance

$$\mathrm{Var}(Y) = E[cM] + \mathrm{Var}(cM) = cm + \varphi (cm)^{2},$$

which is the same functional form with $m$ replaced by $cm$ and the same $\varphi$. The Fano factor, meanwhile, is

$$F = \frac{cm + \varphi c^{2}m^{2}}{cm} = 1 + \varphi c m,$$

which falls from $1 + \varphi m$ to $1 + \varphi c m$ as you thin.

Implication. The Fano factor is depth-dependent: quoting "$F = 3.4$" without saying at what mean count is not a reportable number, because someone sequencing half as deep would measure $F = 2.2$ on the same biology. The dispersion $\varphi$ is depth-independent and is the quantity to quote — which is exactly what Week 11 says, and this is the derivation behind it.

A useful corollary for the report: if you measure $\varphi$ at two depths and get different answers, that is evidence against the gamma–Poisson model itself, not just noise. That is a real diagnostic you can run in an afternoon with realdata.negative_binomial_dispersion and realdata.downsample_matrix.

C  $d'$ and the square-root law

C.1★
Two cell types give expected counts $\lambda_1 = 12$ and $\lambda_2 = 18$ for a marker gene. Compute $d'$.
Show solution

$\bar\lambda = 15$, so $$d' = \frac{|18 - 12|}{\sqrt{15}} = \frac{6}{3.873} = 1.549.$$

Note the denominator uses the pooled rate $\bar\lambda$, not $\lambda_1$ or $\lambda_2$ separately. The two distributions have different widths ($\sqrt{12} = 3.46$ and $\sqrt{18} = 4.24$), and $d'$ as defined here summarizes them with one number at the pooled mean. That is a modelling convention, defended in Week 3 §6, and it is a fair question at a check-in.

C.2★
Double the depth in C.1, so the rates become $24$ and $36$. Compute the new $d'$ and check it against the square-root law.
Show solution

$\bar\lambda = 30$, and $$d' = \frac{12}{\sqrt{30}} = \frac{12}{5.477} = 2.191.$$

Check: $1.549 \times \sqrt{2} = 1.549 \times 1.4142 = 2.191$. ✓

The mechanism, in one line: the gap between the means grew by a factor of 2, and the noise grew by a factor of $\sqrt2$, so the ratio grew by $2/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2$. Every square root in this project is that division.

C.3★★ check-in
Design. You have budget for either $2\times$ the sequencing depth per cell, or $2\times$ as many cells, but not both. Which do you choose, and for what question? Give a quantitative answer for each of two questions: (a) estimating the average expression of a marker gene in a cell type; (b) classifying one particular cell.
Show solution

(a) Estimating a population mean: they are equivalent. The estimate is built from $N \times D$ molecules in total, and its standard error falls as $1/\sqrt{ND}$. Doubling either doubles $ND$ and improves the standard error by $\sqrt2$. Choose on other grounds — more cells also gives you a better view of cell-to-cell variability, which is a bonus that depth does not provide, so in practice more cells wins here.

(b) Classifying one cell: depth, and it is not close. The decision about this cell uses only the molecules captured from this cell. Doubling depth takes $d'$ from $d'$ to $d'\sqrt2$ — for instance from $1.55$ to $2.19$, moving accuracy from $\Phi(0.775) = 0.781$ to $\Phi(1.095) = 0.863$. Doubling the number of cells does nothing at all for the per-cell decision, except indirectly: it pins down the rates $\lambda^{(1)}, \lambda^{(2)}$ that the classifier uses. Once those are already well estimated — and with a thousand cells they are — additional cells buy nothing.

The answer to give at a check-in is that the question is incomplete until you say whether the target is a population quantity or a per-cell decision, and that these two have opposite answers. That distinction is the most common misunderstanding you will have to correct.

Two caveats you should raise yourself before someone raises them for you. First, if the cell type is rare, cells win even for a per-cell question, because you need enough examples to characterize the type at all. Second, if you are past the overdispersion knee (Section I), depth buys nothing and the whole answer flips — see L.4.

C.4★★
Find the error. "I quadrupled the depth and $d'$ went from $1.0$ to $2.0$. So discriminability doubled, which means my accuracy doubled too."
Show solution

The first sentence is right; the second does not follow. Accuracy is $\Phi(d'/2)$, which is not linear in $d'$ and is bounded above by $1$ and below (for the optimal rule with equal priors) by $0.5$.

$$\Phi(0.5) = 0.6915, \qquad \Phi(1.0) = 0.8413.$$

So quadrupling the depth moved accuracy from $69\%$ to $84\%$ — a gain of 15 points, not a doubling. And "doubling accuracy" is not even a coherent target: doubling $0.69$ would be $1.38$.

The right way to talk about it: the error rate is the quantity that behaves multiplicatively, and even then not linearly. Here it fell from $0.3085$ to $0.1587$, roughly halving. For large $d'$ the error rate falls much faster than that — roughly like $e^{-d'^2/8}$ — which is why the interesting regime for this project is the messy middle where accuracy is between $0.55$ and $0.95$.

Related and worth internalizing: because $\Phi$ is flat at both ends, a fixed change in $d'$ matters enormously in the middle and almost not at all near $0.99$. This is exactly why Week 12 converts accuracy back to $d'$ before fitting anything.

C.5★★★
Ill-posed? "Which gene is the better marker: gene X, expressed at $\lambda = 1$ in type 1 and $\lambda = 3$ in type 2 (a 3-fold change), or gene Y, at $\lambda = 20$ versus $\lambda = 30$ (a 1.5-fold change)?" Answer the question, and then say what is wrong with it.
Show solution

The calculation.

$$d'_X = \frac{|3-1|}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2}{1.4142} = 1.414, \qquad d'_Y = \frac{|30-20|}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{10}{5} = 2.000.$$

Gene Y wins, despite having half the fold change. Fold change is a ratio of means and says nothing about noise; $d'$ divides by the noise, and the noise on a count of 25 is proportionally much smaller than on a count of 2. Fold change is not $d'$, and a marker chosen by fold change is not the marker that classifies best. This is Week 10's central practical lesson.

Now what is wrong with the question. Three things, in increasing order of seriousness.

  1. The comparison is depth-dependent and no depth was given. The $\lambda$'s are $Dp$, so the answer above holds at whatever depth produced those numbers. Both $d'$ values scale as $\sqrt D$ together, so the ranking happens to be depth-invariant here — but only because $d'$ scales the same way for both, which is a fact you have to notice rather than assume.
  2. "Better marker" is undefined. Better for per-cell classification? Then $d'$ is the right criterion and Y wins. Better for a differential-expression test across two populations of cells? Then the relevant quantity involves the number of cells and the biological variability between them, and gene X's larger fold change may well win. Better for a human reading a heatmap? Different again.
  3. At $\lambda = 1$ the Gaussian machinery behind $d'$ is not trustworthy. $d'$ was defined by comparing a gap of means to a standard deviation, and $\Phi(d'/2)$ then assumes the count distributions are roughly normal. A Poisson with $\lambda = 1$ is violently non-normal — it puts $37\%$ of its mass on zero. For gene X you should compute the exact Bayes error by summation (detection.bayes_error_exact) rather than trusting $\Phi(d'/2)$.

The response that earns full marks at a check-in is not the number. It is: "Y, by $d'$ — but the question is underspecified, because 'better marker' depends on whether we are classifying cells or comparing populations, and at $\lambda = 1$ I would not trust the Gaussian approximation anyway."

D  From $d'$ to accuracy

D.1★
$d' = 1.5$. What accuracy does the theory predict for the optimal rule with equal priors?
Show solution

$\text{accuracy} = \Phi(d'/2) = \Phi(0.75) = 0.7734$, so about $77\%$.

The factor of two catches people. It is there because the optimal threshold sits halfway between the two means, so each distribution has only $d'/2$ standard deviations of room on its own side of the cut. Being able to say that sentence unprompted is worth more than remembering the formula.

D.2★★ check-in
What $d'$ is needed for $90\%$, $95\%$ and $99\%$ accuracy? By what factor must the depth increase to go from $90\%$ to $95\%$, and from $95\%$ to $99\%$?
Show solution

Invert: $d' = 2\Phi^{-1}(\text{accuracy})$.

Since $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$, the depth ratio is the square of the $d'$ ratio:

$$90\% \to 95\%: \quad \left(\frac{3.290}{2.563}\right)^{2} = (1.2837)^{2} = 1.65,$$ $$95\% \to 99\%: \quad \left(\frac{4.653}{3.290}\right)^{2} = (1.4143)^{2} = 2.00.$$

So $65\%$ more depth for the first step and a full doubling for the second. Getting from $99\%$ to $99.9\%$ ($d' = 6.180$) costs another factor of $1.76$.

This is the table to have in your head, because it is the answer to "so what?" in a talk. The last few percentage points of accuracy are where the money goes, and the square-root law is why. Notice also that the cost per point of accuracy accelerates — which is the practical statement of the fact that $\Phi$ flattens.

D.3★★
At $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$, compute (a) the Gaussian prediction $\Phi(d'/2)$ and (b) the exact accuracy of the optimal rule, by summation. Comment on the size and direction of the discrepancy.
Show solution

(a) Gaussian. $\bar\lambda = 1.5$, $d' = 1/\sqrt{1.5} = 0.8165$, so $\Phi(0.4082) = 0.6584$.

(b) Exact. The optimal rule for Poisson counts thresholds the log-likelihood ratio, $$\log\frac{P(k\mid\lambda_2)}{P(k\mid\lambda_1)} = k\log\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} - (\lambda_2 - \lambda_1) = k\log 2 - 1 > 0 \iff k > \frac{1}{\log 2} = 1.4427,$$ so guess type 2 when $k \ge 2$. With equal priors,

$$\text{accuracy} = \tfrac12 P(k \le 1 \mid \lambda = 1) + \tfrac12 P(k \ge 2 \mid \lambda = 2).$$ $$P(k \le 1 \mid 1) = e^{-1}(1 + 1) = 0.7358, \qquad P(k \ge 2\mid 2) = 1 - e^{-2}(1 + 2) = 1 - 0.4060 = 0.5940.$$ $$\text{accuracy} = \tfrac12(0.7358 + 0.5940) = 0.6649.$$

Comment. Exact $0.6649$, Gaussian $0.6584$: the Gaussian approximation is low by $0.0065$, about two-thirds of a percentage point, at rates as small as 1 and 2 — which is remarkably good given that a Poisson with $\lambda = 1$ looks nothing like a bell curve.

The direction is worth noting: at very low rates the Gaussian formula tends to be pessimistic, because the discreteness of the counts lets the optimal rule exploit the exact shape of the distributions in a way the smooth approximation cannot represent. Do not assume this direction holds everywhere — check it with detection.bayes_error_exact at the rates you actually care about, which is what Week 4 and Week 9 both ask you to do.

This is the calculation that lets you say "the Gaussian approximation is fine here" as a measured claim rather than a hope, and it is the reason Week 9's dropout collapse can be attributed to depth rather than to the approximation breaking.

D.4★★★
Find the error. A student measures $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.85$ on a held-out set and writes: "$\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi(d'/2)$, so $d' = 2\Phi^{-1}(0.85) = 2 \times 1.0364 = 2.073$." Find the error, give the right answer, and explain why the two conversions are different.
Show solution

The error: they used the accuracy-to-$d'$ conversion on an AUC. Those are two different relationships.

  • $\text{accuracy} = \Phi(d'/2)$ — the probability that a single observation falls on the correct side of the optimal threshold.
  • $\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi(d'/\sqrt2)$ — the probability that a randomly chosen type-2 observation scores above a randomly chosen type-1 observation. That is a statement about a difference of two independent draws, whose variance is twice one draw's variance, hence the $\sqrt2$.

Correct answer: $d' = \sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(0.85) = 1.4142 \times 1.0364 = 1.466$. That is what detection.d_prime_from_auc computes.

Why it matters. The student's value ($2.073$) is $\sqrt2$ times too large — a $41\%$ overstatement of $d'$, which in a log-log fit against depth is a constant vertical offset. It would not change their fitted exponent, so no plot would look wrong, and the error would survive all the way into the report as an inflated intercept.

The derivation of the AUC relation, which is worth being able to produce. Let $S_1 \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1, \sigma^2)$ and $S_2 \sim \mathcal N(\mu_2, \sigma^2)$ be the scores. Then $$\mathrm{AUC} = P(S_2 > S_1) = P(S_2 - S_1 > 0),$$ and $S_2 - S_1 \sim \mathcal N(\mu_2 - \mu_1,\ 2\sigma^2)$, so $$\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_2-\mu_1}{\sigma\sqrt2}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{\sqrt2}\right). \qquad \blacksquare$$

A quick sanity check you can do in your head, and should: accuracy and AUC are never equal for the same $d'$, and AUC is always the larger of the two (since $d'/\sqrt2 > d'/2$). If a script ever reports them as equal, something is wired wrong.

E  Combining evidence over genes

E.1★
Three independent marker genes have $d' = 0.4$, $0.5$ and $0.3$. Compute the combined $d'$ and the predicted accuracy.
Show solution

$$d'_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{0.4^2 + 0.5^2 + 0.3^2} = \sqrt{0.16 + 0.25 + 0.09} = \sqrt{0.50} = 0.7071,$$ $$\text{accuracy} = \Phi(0.3536) = 0.6382.$$

Note that the combined $d'$ ($0.707$) is larger than any individual gene but much smaller than the sum ($1.2$). That gap is the entire content of "adds in quadrature", and it is the reason the $\sqrt k$ law is a square root rather than a straight line. This is detection.combine_d_prime.

E.2★★
You have access to genes that each give $d' = 0.3$. How many do you need, assuming independence, for $90\%$ accuracy?
Show solution

$k$ identical independent genes give $d'_{\text{total}} = 0.3\sqrt k$. From D.2, $90\%$ needs $d' = 2.5631$, so

$$\sqrt k = \frac{2.5631}{0.3} = 8.544, \qquad k = 72.99 \to \textbf{73 genes}.$$

Two things to take from the number. First, 73 is a lot, and real marker panels are that size for exactly this reason. Second, the assumption is doing enormous work: 73 independent genes is a strong claim about biology, and genes in the same pathway are correlated. E.3 shows what correlation costs you, and the answer is "potentially all of it".

E.3★★ check-in
Explain why independent evidence adds in quadrature rather than linearly. Then work out what happens at the opposite extreme: $k$ genes that are perfectly correlated (identical copies of the same measurement).
Show solution

Why quadrature. The optimal statistic is the log-likelihood ratio, which for independent genes is a sum of per-gene terms, $L = \sum_g L_g$. Then:

  • Means add: the gap between $E_2[L]$ and $E_1[L]$ is $\sum_g (\text{gap}_g)$ — linear in $k$ for identical genes.
  • Variances add, because of independence: $\mathrm{Var}(L) = \sum_g \mathrm{Var}(L_g)$ — so the standard deviation grows as $\sqrt k$.

Discriminability is gap over sd, so it grows as $k/\sqrt k = \sqrt k$. For non-identical genes the same argument gives $d'_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{\sum_g d_g'^{\,2}}$, which is quadrature.

Perfect correlation. Suppose all $k$ genes are literally the same measurement repeated. The gap still grows linearly, $k \times \text{gap}$ — but so does the standard deviation, because $\mathrm{Var}(kX) = k^{2}\mathrm{Var}(X)$ and the sd is $k\,\mathrm{sd}(X)$. So

$$d'_{\text{total}} = \frac{k \cdot \text{gap}}{k \cdot \text{sd}} = d'_1,$$

unchanged. Copying a measurement adds no information — which is obviously true and is a good check that the framework is not lying to you.

Between the extremes, partial correlation interpolates: the general formula picks up covariance terms, $\mathrm{Var}(L) = \sum_g \mathrm{Var}(L_g) + 2\sum_{g<h}\mathrm{Cov}(L_g, L_h)$, and positive covariance inflates the denominator and blunts the $\sqrt k$ growth.

This is the assumption most likely to be attacked in Week 15, and the right response is not to defend independence — it is false — but to say what direction the violation pushes: positively correlated genes make the true $d'$ smaller than $\sqrt k$ predicts, so the naive Bayes prediction is an optimistic bound. An honest bound with a known direction is a usable result.

E.4★★★
Design. You may use either ten genes with $d' = 0.5$ each, or one gene with $d' = 1.5$. Which do you choose? Give the calculation, then give three reasons the calculation might be the wrong basis for the decision, and state a condition under which you would take the single gene.
Show solution

The calculation. Ten genes: $d' = 0.5\sqrt{10} = 1.581$, accuracy $\Phi(0.7906) = 0.7854$. One gene: $d' = 1.5$, accuracy $\Phi(0.75) = 0.7734$. The panel wins, by about 1.2 accuracy points.

Three reasons the calculation might be the wrong basis.

  1. The margin is inside the assumption's error. A 1.2-point difference is well within what a modest positive correlation among the ten genes would erase. If the ten genes are in one pathway — which is exactly why they are all markers of the same type — they are correlated, and the effective $k$ is less than 10.
  2. Ten genes means twenty rate parameters to estimate instead of two. Every one of those estimates has error, and errors in the rates degrade the classifier's performance below the theoretical $d'$. With limited training cells the ten-gene rule can perform worse in practice than its $d'$ says, while the one-gene rule is nearly free of estimation error.
  3. Selection leakage. If the ten genes were chosen for having large $d'$ on the same data you then evaluate on, their measured $d'$ values are biased upward — you selected the ones that happened to look good. Week 10 shows this can be a large effect. The single gene is not immune, but with one gene the bias is easier to bound and easier to explain.

When to take the single gene. When the panel's genes are strongly correlated with each other, when training data is scarce enough that estimating twenty rates is unreliable, or when the marker set was selected on the evaluation data and you have no clean way to correct for it. A concrete threshold you can state: if a fair estimate of the ten-gene panel's effective independent count $k_{\text{eff}}$ is below $(1.5/0.5)^{2} = 9$, the single gene is at least as good.

Design problems have defensible answers, not correct ones, and the defence is the answer. A response that gives only the arithmetic gets half marks; a response that names correlation, estimation error and selection leakage gets full marks even if it chooses the other option.

F  The simulator and generative models

F.1★
simulate.marker_profiles(n_genes, fold_change, marker_mass=0.02) puts an average of marker_mass on the marker set, split equally, with type 1 expressing each marker fold_change times more strongly than type 2. Derive the two per-gene rates for $k = 4$ genes and a fold change of $2$.
Show solution

The per-gene average rate is $\text{base} = 0.02/4 = 0.005$. Writing $a$ for type 1 and $b$ for type 2, the two conditions are

$$\frac{a}{b} = 2, \qquad \frac{a+b}{2} = 0.005.$$

Substituting $a = 2b$ into the second gives $3b/2 = 0.005$, so $b = 0.003\overline{3}$ and $a = 0.006\overline{6}$. In general

$$a = \frac{2\,\text{base}\cdot f}{1+f}, \qquad b = \frac{2\,\text{base}}{1+f}.$$

Check: $a + b = 2\,\text{base}$ regardless of $f$, as required. ✓

The reason the convention pins the average rather than one type's mass is that it keeps $d'$ symmetric between the two types. Also note the deliberate design decision in the docstring: the pooled mass is held fixed as $k$ grows, so the per-gene signal shrinks as $1/k$ and the $\sqrt k$ law is tested against a genuine trade-off rather than a free lunch. If each added gene were free, the "law" would be uninteresting.

F.2★★
In the simulator, what exactly does the depth $D$ multiply, and why must the profile be a set of fractions rather than counts? What would break if a profile summed to $1.4$?
Show solution

$D$ multiplies the fractions to give rates: gene $g$'s count is $\mathrm{Poisson}(D p_g)$. The profile has to be fractions of the transcriptome because that is the physical picture — a cell has a composition, and the sequencer draws $D$ molecules from it, each landing on gene $g$ with probability $p_g$.

If a profile summed to $1.4$, then the expected library size would be $1.4 D$ rather than $D$, so the "depth" parameter would no longer mean depth. Everything downstream that compares across depths — the accuracy-versus-depth curve, the $\sqrt D$ fit, the comparison against theory — would be plotted against the wrong $x$ axis, by a constant factor. A constant factor on a log-log plot is a vertical shift, so the fitted exponent would be unaffected and nothing would look wrong. That is what makes it dangerous.

In practice marker_profiles returns only the marker genes, whose masses sum to about marker_mass, not to 1 — the remaining mass belongs to the thousands of non-marker genes the simulation does not track. So the check is not "does it sum to 1" but "does it sum to what I think it does", and knowing which is which is the point of this problem.

F.3★★
Find the error. A student simulates a cell by drawing $X_g \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(p_g)$ for each gene and then multiplying by the depth: counts = D * rng.poisson(p). What is wrong, and what is the observable signature of the mistake?
Show solution

The error: scaling a Poisson variable is not the same as scaling its rate. The correct construction is $\mathrm{Poisson}(Dp_g)$; the student built $D \cdot \mathrm{Poisson}(p_g)$.

Why they differ. If $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)$ then

$$E[DX] = D\mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(DX) = D^{2}\mu,$$

so the mean is right but the variance is $D$ times too big. The Fano factor of the simulated data is $D$ rather than $1$.

The observable signature, and it is loud: with $p_g = 0.002$ and $D = 3000$, the correct simulation gives counts scattered around 6 with values like 3, 5, 8, 9. The broken one gives $\mathrm{Poisson}(0.002)$, which is zero for 998 cells in 1000, multiplied by 3000 — so almost every cell has a count of exactly $0$ and the occasional cell has exactly $3000$. Every simulated count is a multiple of $D$.

How the project catches it: compute counting.fano_factor on the simulated counts. It must be $1$ to within sampling error. This is the first check to run on any simulator, and it costs one line.

Worth doing because the mistake is a type mistake rather than an arithmetic one — the code runs, the shapes are right, the means are right, and only the second moment is wrong. The general lesson: check a second moment, not just a mean, whenever you write a sampler.

F.4★★★ check-in
Open. Your simulator's accuracy-versus-depth curve lies on top of the theory curve. Why is that not strong evidence that the simulator is correct? Propose three checks that would be, and say what class of error each one can catch.
Show solution

Why agreement is weak evidence. You wrote the theory and you wrote the simulator, from the same understanding. If that understanding is wrong, both are wrong in the same way and they will agree beautifully. Agreement between two implementations of one belief tests the implementations, not the belief. It is the software equivalent of proofreading your own writing.

Concretely: if you had the wrong variance convention in your head, both the theory and the simulator would use it, the curves would match, and the match would tell you nothing.

Three checks that are informative, each catching a different class of error.

  1. The Fano factor of the raw simulated counts must be $1$. This does not use the theory at all — it tests the generative model against a property of the Poisson distribution. Catches: F.3's scaling error, wrong rate parameterization, an accidental extra source of variability.
  2. The empirical mean count for gene $g$ must equal $D p_g$, and the total must be about $D \sum_g p_g$. Again theory-free. Catches: normalization errors, the profile summing to the wrong thing (F.2), off-by-one indexing between profiles and genes, the two cell types being swapped.
  3. Compare the simulator's measured optimal-rule accuracy against the exact Bayes error by summation (detection.bayes_error_exact) for a single gene at a low rate. This is an independent route from the model to the answer that does not pass through $\Phi(d'/2)$ at all. Catches: everything the Gaussian approximation might be hiding, including the low-depth regime where $\Phi(d'/2)$ genuinely fails — which is Week 9's result and would otherwise look like a simulator bug.

The general principle, worth stating in the report: a check is informative to the extent that it could fail for reasons independent of the thing you are trying to confirm. That principle is also the entire argument for the bead experiment, which is the ultimate independent check — no software at all.

G  Classifiers and generalization

G.1★
You split 1000 cells with classify.train_test_split(X, y, test_fraction=0.3, rng) and measure $90\%$ accuracy on the test set. Give the standard error of that estimate and a rough 95% interval.
Show solution

The test set has $0.3 \times 1000 = 300$ cells. The standard error of a proportion is

$$\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.9 \times 0.1}{300}} = \sqrt{0.0003} = 0.0173,$$

so about $\pm 0.034$ for a 95% interval: roughly $[0.866,\ 0.934]$. (Use stats.wilson_interval(270, 300) for the interval you should actually report; at $n = 300$ and $p$ away from the edges, Wilson and the normal approximation nearly coincide.)

Notice the tension this sets up. A larger test fraction gives a tighter accuracy estimate; a smaller one gives a better-trained classifier. At 1000 cells this trade-off is real, and there is no formula that resolves it — which is why Week 8 uses cross-validation (classify.cross_validated_accuracy) to get both.

G.2★★ check-in
Find the error. "I ranked all 20,000 genes by the difference in mean expression between the two cell types, kept the top 20, then split the cells 70/30 into train and test, trained on the training set and got 97% on the test set. So my classifier generalizes."
Show solution

The error is selection leakage: the gene ranking used the labels of all the cells, including the ones later placed in the test set. The test set is therefore not held out — information from it leaked into the model through the choice of features, before the split ever happened.

Why this inflates the score. Out of 20,000 genes, some will show a large between-type difference by chance, and selecting the top 20 preferentially picks up those chance differences. Those chance differences are properties of this particular sample of cells — including the test cells — so they reproduce on the test set and do not reproduce on new data. The inflation can be large, and it is largest exactly when it is most tempting: many genes, few cells, weak real signal.

The fix: split first, then select markers using only the training fold. realdata.select_markers(X, y, n_markers, min_cells=10) must be called inside the training fold, never on the full matrix. Under cross-validation, the selection must be repeated separately in every fold, which means the chosen gene set differs from fold to fold — and that instability is itself informative about how real your markers are.

The diagnostic that reveals it: run the whole pipeline on data with the labels permuted. With correct discipline you should get $50\%$. If you get $70\%$ on shuffled labels, you have measured your leakage directly. That is a permutation test (stats.permutation_test) used as a bug detector, and it is worth an hour of anybody's time.

This is Week 10's central methodological hazard and one of the most common ways published analyses go wrong. Being able to describe it, and to describe the permutation check, is a strong answer at any check-in.

G.3★★
A 1-nearest-neighbour classifier gets $100\%$ accuracy on its training data, always. Explain why, and say what it does and does not tell you.
Show solution

Why: for a training point, the nearest training point is itself, at distance zero. So the predicted label is its own label, always, and training accuracy is $100\%$ by construction — regardless of whether the data has any signal in it at all. Run 1-NN on pure noise with random labels and you still get $100\%$ training accuracy.

What it tells you: essentially nothing, other than that your implementation includes each point in its own neighbour search. It is not a measurement of anything about the data.

What it does illustrate: training accuracy is not a performance estimate, for any classifier. 1-NN is simply the most extreme case, which is what makes it a useful teaching example. The general statement is that any model flexible enough to memorize its training set will report a training score that measures its flexibility rather than the data's separability.

Practical consequence in this project: classify.knn_predict(X_train, y_train, X_query, k) should never be evaluated on X_query = X_train. And when you increase $k$, training accuracy falls while test accuracy typically rises then falls — the classic picture, and worth generating once for yourself.

G.4★★
Design. Same budget question as C.3 — $2\times$ depth or $2\times$ cells — but now the goal is to train the best classifier you can. Does the answer change? Give a diagnostic that tells you which you need.
Show solution

The answer can change, because two different things limit you.

  • Depth raises the Bayes error floor — the best accuracy any classifier could achieve, $\Phi(d'/2)$. No classifier, however clever, beats it.
  • Cells reduce the gap between your classifier and that floor, by giving you better estimates of the decision boundary (or, for naive Bayes, of the rates).

So the right question is: are you limited by the floor or by the gap?

The diagnostic. Compute the theoretical prediction $\Phi(d'/2)$ from the estimated rates, and compare it with your classifier's cross-validated accuracy.

  • Small gap (say your classifier is within 1–2 points of $\Phi(d'/2)$): you are at the floor. More cells buy almost nothing. Buy depth.
  • Large gap (your classifier is 10 points below the prediction): you are limited by estimation, not by the data's separability. Buy cells — or, first, check that the gap is not a bug, because a 10-point gap on simulated data usually is one.

The answer for this project specifically: depth, almost always. Week 8's headline result is that logistic regression, kNN and naive Bayes all land within a couple of points of $\Phi(d'/2)$ once there are a few hundred training cells — the classifier is not the bottleneck. That finding is what makes the whole project a study of the data rather than of the algorithm.

One honest caveat: the argument above assumes you are past the overdispersion knee's near side. If you are past the knee, depth buys nothing either, and the answer becomes "buy more genes" — see I.5 and L.4.

G.5★★★
Ill-posed. "What is the best classifier for distinguishing these two cell types?" Explain why this question, as asked, has a trivial answer that is useless, and reformulate it into a question worth answering.
Show solution

The trivial answer. If the true rates $\lambda^{(1)}$ and $\lambda^{(2)}$ are known, the best possible classifier is the likelihood-ratio rule, and its accuracy is the Bayes error — the theoretical optimum, which no classifier can beat and which is what $\Phi(d'/2)$ approximates. Under the Poisson model with independent genes, that rule is naive Bayes with the true rates. So the question is answered before it is asked, and the answer contains no information about logistic regression, kNN, random forests, or anything else.

What makes the question look non-trivial is that you do not know the true rates. So every real classifier is doing two things at once: estimating something about the distributions from a finite training set, and then making a decision. The interesting variation between classifiers is entirely in the first part.

Reformulations worth answering:

  • "Given $n$ training cells, which classifier gets closest to the Bayes error, and how does the answer depend on $n$?" — an empirical question with a real answer, and the one Week 8 actually asks.
  • "How many training cells does each classifier need before it is within one point of the Bayes error?" — sharper, and directly useful for design.
  • "Under what departures from the Poisson-independent model does a classifier that does not assume it (kNN, logistic regression) beat naive Bayes?" — the most interesting version, because it turns the classifier comparison into a probe of the model's assumptions. A kNN that beats naive Bayes is telling you your generative model is wrong.

This reframing is worth carrying into Week 15. When someone asks "why didn't you use a neural network", the answer is not defensive: it is that the Bayes-optimal rule is known in closed form for this model, every classifier is competing for the same ceiling, and the project measured the ceiling instead. That is a stronger position than any accuracy number.

H  Dropout

H.1★
At depth $D = 3000$ with dropout $q = 0.4$, give the effective depth and the factor by which $d'$ falls.
Show solution

Effective depth $D(1-q) = 3000 \times 0.6 = 1800$. Since $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$, the factor is $\sqrt{0.6} = 0.7746$: $d'$ falls by about $22.5\%$.

Notice how gentle that is. Losing $40\%$ of your molecules costs you only $22.5\%$ of your discriminability, because the square root is a forgiving function. The same fact read the other way is much less cheerful: recovering that $22.5\%$ by sequencing deeper requires a $67\%$ increase in depth.

H.2★★
A measurement with no dropout gives $d' = 1.6$. Give the accuracy with and without $30\%$ dropout, and state the depth increase that would exactly compensate.
Show solution

Without: $\Phi(0.8) = 0.7881$.

With $q = 0.3$: $d' = 1.6\sqrt{0.7} = 1.6 \times 0.8367 = 1.3387$, so accuracy $= \Phi(0.6693) = 0.7483$. A loss of about 4 accuracy points.

Compensation: you need $d'$ back to $1.6$, and $d' \propto \sqrt{D(1-q)}$, so you need $D' (1-q) = D$, i.e. $D' = D/0.7 = 1.4286\,D$ — a $43\%$ increase in depth to undo $30\%$ dropout.

The asymmetry is worth a sentence in the report: dropout costs you less than it sounds (30% loss, 4 accuracy points), but fixing it costs more than it sounds (43% more sequencing). Both facts come from the same square root, read in opposite directions.

I  Overdispersion and the negative binomial

I.1★
A gene has mean count $m = 20$ and dispersion $\varphi = 0.5$, so $\mathrm{Var} = m + \varphi m^{2}$. Compute the variance, the Fano factor, the standard deviation and the relative noise. Compare with Poisson.
Show solution

$\mathrm{Var} = 20 + 0.5 \times 400 = 220$. Fano $= 220/20 = 11$. sd $= \sqrt{220} = 14.83$, relative noise $= 74.2\%$.

Poisson at the same mean: variance $20$, sd $4.47$, relative noise $22.4\%$. So the real data is $\sqrt{11} = 3.3$ times noisier than the counting-statistics floor.

And note where that extra noise comes from: not from the sequencer, but from cells of the "same type" not actually being identical. This is the assumption from your Week 1 list — "cells of one type are identical" — arriving with a number attached.

I.2★★ check-in
Derive the depth ceiling: show that with $\mathrm{Var} = \bar m + \varphi \bar m^{2}$, $d'$ approaches a constant as depth grows, and give that constant. Then compute it for $p_1 = 0.004$, $p_2 = 0.006$, $\varphi = 0.5$, and give the corresponding accuracy ceiling and the depth at the knee.
Show solution

The derivation. With $m_i = D p_i$ and $\bar m = D\bar p$,

$$d'(D) = \frac{|m_1 - m_2|}{\sqrt{\bar m + \varphi\bar m^{2}}} = \frac{D|p_1-p_2|}{\sqrt{D\bar p + \varphi D^{2}\bar p^{2}}} = \frac{D|\Delta p|}{D\bar p\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{D\bar p} + \varphi}} = \frac{|\Delta p|}{\bar p}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi + 1/(D\bar p)}}.$$

As $D \to \infty$ the $1/(D\bar p)$ term vanishes and

$$\boxed{\;d'_{\max} = \frac{|\Delta p|}{\bar p \sqrt{\varphi}}.\;}$$

At small $D$ the $1/(D\bar p)$ term dominates and $d' \approx |\Delta p|\sqrt{D/\bar p}$, recovering the $\sqrt D$ law. The knee is where the two terms are equal: $\varphi = 1/(D\bar p)$, i.e. at a mean count $\bar m = 1/\varphi$, or $D_{\text{knee}} = 1/(\varphi\bar p)$.

The numbers. $\Delta p = 0.002$, $\bar p = 0.005$, $\sqrt\varphi = 0.7071$:

$$d'_{\max} = \frac{0.002}{0.005 \times 0.7071} = \frac{0.002}{0.0035355} = 0.566, \qquad \text{accuracy}_{\max} = \Phi(0.283) = 0.611.$$ $$\bar m_{\text{knee}} = 1/0.5 = 2 \text{ counts}, \qquad D_{\text{knee}} = \frac{1}{0.5 \times 0.005} = 400 \text{ molecules per cell}.$$

Read that carefully. Past 400 molecules per cell — a fifth of a typical 10x library — extra depth buys essentially nothing for this gene, and no amount of money gets you past $61\%$ accuracy from it. That is the sharpest and most useful claim in the project, and it has direct budget consequences for anyone designing an experiment.

Assumptions used: gamma–Poisson counts with a single depth-independent $\varphi$; the same $\varphi$ in both cell types; equal priors; and the Gaussian approximation behind $\Phi(d'/2)$, which is fine at these means but should be checked at $\bar m = 2$.

Notice what $d'_{\max}$ depends on: the relative difference $|\Delta p|/\bar p$ — which is essentially the fold change — divided by $\sqrt\varphi$. Once you are past the knee, fold change is the right currency after all. The whole project's insistence that fold change is not $d'$ is a statement about the counting-limited regime, and one of the more elegant things you can say in Week 14 is that the two regimes have different natural currencies.

I.3★★
You measure sample mean $20$ and sample variance $180$ for a gene across many cells. Estimate $\varphi$ by the method of moments. Then explain why this estimator can return a negative number, and what to do about it.
Show solution

Setting $s^{2} = \bar m + \varphi \bar m^{2}$ and solving,

$$\hat\varphi = \frac{s^{2} - \bar m}{\bar m^{2}} = \frac{180 - 20}{400} = \frac{160}{400} = 0.40.$$

Why it can go negative. If the true $\varphi$ is zero or near zero, the sample variance $s^{2}$ fluctuates around $\bar m$ and will fall below it about half the time — giving $\hat\varphi < 0$, which is not a possible value of a variance-inflation parameter. This is not a bug; it is what an unbiased estimator of a non-negative quantity does when the quantity is near the boundary.

What to do. Clamp at zero and report the clamping: "$n$ genes of $N$ returned a negative moment estimate and were set to $\varphi = 0$." Do not silently drop those genes — they are systematically the low-dispersion ones, so dropping them biases your reported dispersion distribution upward, which is precisely the direction that would flatter your Week 11 conclusion.

How much noise is on $\hat\varphi$? It inherits the noise of a sample variance, which for $n$ cells has relative sd of roughly $\sqrt{2/n}$ for Poisson-ish data. At $n = 50$ cells that is $20\%$ on $s^{2}$; with $s^{2}$ near $\bar m$, the noise on the difference $s^2 - \bar m$ is proportionally much larger. So per-gene $\hat\varphi$ from few cells is very noisy — which is exactly why the professional tools (DESeq2, edgeR) shrink per-gene dispersion estimates toward a trend.

Week 1's Lab 2 is this problem in disguise, three months early: a statistic computed from a small sample is itself a random variable. The realdata.negative_binomial_dispersion docstring makes the same point.

I.4★★
Find the error. "The Fano factor on my real data is $3.4$, so my data is $3.4$ times noisier than Poisson. To get back to the discriminability I would have had under Poisson noise, I need $3.4$ times the depth." There are two errors. Find both.
Show solution

Error 1: variance is not noise. A Fano factor of $3.4$ means the variance is $3.4\times$ Poisson, so the standard deviation — which is what $d'$ divides by — is $\sqrt{3.4} = 1.84\times$ Poisson. The data is $84\%$ noisier, not $240\%$ noisier. Naively, then, you might think you need $3.4\times$ the depth to recover a factor $1.84$ in $d'$ (since $d' \propto \sqrt D$). That reasoning is arithmetically consistent, which is what makes error 2 so easy to miss.

Error 2, the fatal one: extra depth does not fix overdispersion. The whole assumption behind "more depth restores $d'$" is that the noise stays proportional to $\sqrt{\text{mean}}$. Under overdispersion it does not: the excess variance term is $\varphi m^{2}$, which grows as $D^{2}$ while the signal grows only as $D$. From I.2, $d'$ saturates. If you are already past the knee, buying $3.4\times$ the depth buys essentially nothing, and the student would have spent the money and measured no improvement.

The correct diagnosis. Compute $\varphi$, not $F$ — $F$ depends on depth (B.4) and $\varphi$ does not. Then find the knee, $\bar m = 1/\varphi$. If your current mean count for that gene is well below $1/\varphi$, depth still helps roughly as $\sqrt D$. If it is above, depth is the wrong purchase and you should buy genes or cells instead.

This is the most expensive mistake on the page, in the literal sense: it is a mistake that causes people to buy sequencing.

I.5★★★
Design. You are past the overdispersion knee. Budget for either $2\times$ depth or $2\times$ as many independent marker genes. Which, and why? Then give the strongest objection to your own answer.
Show solution

Genes, decisively. Past the knee, $d'$ per gene has saturated at $|\Delta p|/(\bar p\sqrt\varphi)$ and doubling the depth moves it by almost nothing. Doubling the number of independent genes, by contrast, multiplies the combined $d'$ by $\sqrt2$ — and that quadrature argument makes no reference to the per-gene noise model at all. It works for Poisson genes, for negative binomial genes, for any genes, provided they are independent.

Numerically, with the I.2 example: at the ceiling each gene gives $d' = 0.566$. Ten such genes give $0.566\sqrt{10} = 1.790$ and accuracy $\Phi(0.895) = 0.815$; twenty give $2.531$ and $\Phi(1.266) = 0.897$. Whereas doubling depth from $D = 5000$ to $10{,}000$, with $D_{\text{knee}} = 400$, changes the per-gene $d'$ only from $$\frac{0.4}{\sqrt{0.5 + 1/25}} = 0.5443 \quad\text{to}\quad \frac{0.4}{\sqrt{0.5 + 1/50}} = 0.5547,$$ a gain of $1.9\%$ — against the $41\%$ that doubling the gene count delivers.

The strongest objection to my own answer: overdispersion is unlikely to be independent across genes. A large share of a cell's "extra" variability is a single shared factor — cell size, total RNA content, capture efficiency — that inflates or deflates every gene together. If the dominant component of $\varphi$ is shared, then adding genes adds strongly correlated evidence, and E.3 shows that perfectly correlated evidence adds nothing at all. The $\sqrt2$ would be an illusion.

What to do about the objection, which is the part that earns the marks. The shared component is exactly what library-size normalization removes: dividing each cell's counts by its total, or conditioning on the observed library size, strips out the common scaling factor and leaves the gene-specific dispersion. So the answer becomes conditional and testable:

  • Estimate the correlation of residual expression between marker genes after normalizing for library size.
  • If it is small, buy genes and expect close to $\sqrt2$.
  • If it is large, buy genes and expect less than $\sqrt2$ — but still more than the essentially zero that depth would give.

Note that in both branches the answer is still "genes". Past the knee, depth is dominated in every scenario, which is a robust conclusion and the kind worth putting in a report.

This problem is the reason Week 11 is a milestone. The scaling law you spent five weeks deriving is true and has a domain, and knowing the domain is worth more than the law.

J.2★★
Show that using the Poisson $d'$ formula on the bead problem understates $d'$, derive the exact factor, and give the resulting error in predicted accuracy at $n = 100$.
Show solution

The Poisson formula divides by $\sqrt{\bar\lambda} = \sqrt{n\bar p}$; the binomial one divides by $\sqrt{n\bar p(1-\bar p)}$. The ratio is

$$\frac{d'_{\text{Poisson}}}{d'_{\text{binomial}}} = \sqrt{1 - \bar p} = \sqrt{0.675} = 0.8216,$$

so the Poisson formula understates $d'$ by $17.8\%$. Numerically at $n = 100$:

$$d'_{\text{Poisson}} = \frac{10 \times 0.05}{\sqrt{0.325}} = \frac{0.5}{0.57009} = 0.8771, \qquad \Phi(0.4386) = 0.6695,$$

against the correct $0.7032$. An error of $3.4$ accuracy points — enough to make a perfectly good experiment look like a disagreement with theory.

Why the binomial is less noisy than the Poisson at the same mean: a binomial count is bounded above by $n$, and that boundedness suppresses variance. As $\bar p \to 0$ the factor $(1-\bar p) \to 1$ and the two coincide — which is why the distinction is invisible for gene expression fractions of order $10^{-3}$ and glaring for bead fractions of $0.325$.

Use the right formula for the right experiment. The two are close cousins and are not interchangeable at bead-sized fractions.

J.3★★
How many trials would it take to pin an accuracy near $0.70$ to $\pm 0.03$ at 95% confidence? Compare with the project's budget of 40 trials per point and say what $\pm$ that budget actually delivers.

$$n \ge \frac{z^{2}p(1-p)}{h^{2}} = \frac{3.8416 \times 0.70 \times 0.30}{0.03^{2}} = \frac{3.8416 \times 0.21}{0.0009} = 896.4 \to \textbf{897 trials}.$$

J.4★★ check-in
Find the error. "I plotted $\log(\text{accuracy})$ against $\log n$ for my bead data and fitted a slope of $0.11$. The theory predicts $0.5$. My experiment refutes the square-root law."
Show solution

The error: accuracy is not a power law and never can be, because it saturates at $1$ while a power law $c\,n^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha > 0$ grows without bound. Fitting one gives you a number, but the number is not an estimate of anything — it depends on where along the saturating curve your points happen to sit.

The decisive demonstration. Take the exact theoretical accuracies for the 30/35 jars, generated by a law that is precisely $d' \propto \sqrt n$: $0.5670$, $0.6052$, $0.6471$, $0.7032$, $0.7748$ at $n = 10, 25, 50, 100, 200$. Fit $\log(\text{accuracy})$ on $\log n$ through those five perfect values and the slope comes out at $0.104$ — essentially exactly what the student measured. Their data agrees with the theory; their fit does not measure the theory.

Worse, the number is not even stably wrong. Change the jars to 30/40, leaving the underlying law untouched, and the same fit through perfect values returns $0.132$. A quantity that moves when you change the beads is not a measurement of the law.

The fix: undo the saturation before fitting. Accuracy is a squashed $d'$ and you know the squashing function exactly, so invert it: $d' = 2\Phi^{-1}(\text{accuracy})$. Then fit $\log d'$ against $\log n$, because $d' = c\sqrt n$ genuinely is an unbounded power law, and that slope means something.

Two consequences to state: points at or below chance give $d' \le 0$, which has no logarithm and must be dropped — and the number dropped, and which ones, must appear in the caption, because they are systematically the low-$n$ points and a silent drop biases the slope. And the sentence to have ready for the talk: "accuracy saturates and $d'$ does not, so I convert every measured accuracy back to $d'$ before fitting the exponent; that is also what makes the exponent comparable across experiments with completely different units of data."

J.5★★★
Open design. You have six hours of table time and nothing else is fixed — jars, draw sizes, trial counts are all yours. Design the best bead experiment you can. State what you are optimizing, give the design, and justify each choice numerically.
Show solution

There is no single right answer; there is a right way to answer. Here is one defensible design and the reasoning behind it.

Step 1: say what you are optimizing. The arm exists to test whether $d' \propto n^{1/2}$ on a physical system. So the objective is the precision of the fitted exponent, not the accuracy at any single point. Naming that changes every subsequent decision.

Step 2: what determines the precision of a slope? For a least-squares fit of $\log d'$ on $\log n$ with per-point noise $\sigma$,

$$\mathrm{SE}(\hat\alpha) \approx \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{P}\ \mathrm{sd}(\log n)},$$

where $P$ is the number of distinct draw sizes. Three levers: reduce $\sigma$ (more trials per point, or a wider jar contrast), increase $\mathrm{sd}(\log n)$ (a longer lever arm in $n$), or increase $P$.

Step 3: spend the free lever first. Widening the jars from 30/35 to 30/40 doubles the effect at zero time cost and is strictly dominant. Do not go further than 30/45 or so: at 30/50 the predicted accuracy at $n = 200$ is above $0.99$, where 40 trials cannot distinguish $0.98$ from $1.00$ and the point contributes nothing. Choose 30/40.

Step 4: the lever arm. $\mathrm{sd}(\log n)$ is maximized by pushing the extremes apart, so keep $n = 10$ at the bottom. At the top, the time model from the protocol page says a trial costs about $n + 40$ seconds, so $n = 200$ trials are five times more expensive than $n = 25$ ones. Going to $n = 400$ would extend the lever arm by $0.3$ in $\log_{10}$ but cost twice as much per trial and push the accuracy to $0.98$, where the point is nearly uninformative. Keep $10$ to $200$.

Step 5: allocate the six hours. Six hours is 21,600 seconds. Consider four draw sizes $\{10, 25, 75, 200\}$ with $t$ trials each, costing $t(50 + 65 + 115 + 240) = 470t$ seconds. Six hours gives $t = 46$. Compare with five draw sizes $\{10,25,50,100,200\}$, costing $585t$, so $t = 37$. The four-point design buys $24\%$ more trials per point at the cost of one point — and since $\mathrm{SE}(\hat\alpha)$ improves as $1/\sqrt{t}$ and as $1/\sqrt P$, those roughly cancel. Either is defensible; take the five-point version, because a fifth point also lets you see curvature rather than only fit through it, and a physical experiment that can reveal an unexpected shape is worth more than one that can only estimate a parameter.

Step 6: the dropout arm. It is a second, independent prediction (the collapse onto $n(1-q)$) and it costs half your budget. With only six hours, run the plain arm properly at $t = 37$ rather than both arms at $t = 18$ — at 18 trials the error bar is $\pm 0.23$ and neither arm says anything. Drop the dropout arm, and say so, with this calculation.

The design: jars 30/40; $n \in \{10, 25, 50, 100, 200\}$; 37 trials per point; plain arm only; pre-randomized sheet; cut card at $\lceil 0.348889\,n\rceil$.

What it delivers: per-point error bars of about $\pm 0.15$, a predicted effect of $0.301$ across the range, and — from repeated runs of Week 12's Lab 1 — a fitted exponent with a standard error in the region of $0.2$. That is enough to distinguish $0.5$ from $0$ and not enough to distinguish $0.5$ from $0.7$. Say that in advance, because a design whose resolution is stated before the run converts a wide interval from an embarrassment into a prediction that came true.

Full marks here are not for matching this design. They are for naming the objective, identifying the free lever, doing the time arithmetic, and stating the achievable resolution before running anything.

K  Intervals and error bars

K.1★
You get 28 correct out of 40. Compute the 95% Wilson interval by hand.
Show solution

$\hat p = 0.700$, $n = 40$, $z = 1.96$, $z^{2} = 3.8416$.

$$\text{denominator} = 1 + \frac{3.8416}{40} = 1.09604,$$ $$\text{centre} = \frac{0.700 + 3.8416/80}{1.09604} = \frac{0.74802}{1.09604} = 0.68248,$$ $$\text{half-width} = \frac{1.96\sqrt{\dfrac{0.7\times0.3}{40} + \dfrac{3.8416}{6400}}}{1.09604} = \frac{1.96 \times 0.076487}{1.09604} = 0.13678.$$

Interval: $[0.546,\ 0.819]$.

Read it out loud: your measured $70\%$ is consistent with anything from $55\%$ to $82\%$. That is what 40 trials buys, and it is why the Week 6 design calculation mattered.

K.2★ check-in
You observe 0 successes in 20 trials. Give the Wald interval and the Wilson interval, and say which is usable.
Show solution

Wald: $\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n} = 0 \pm 1.96\sqrt{0} = [0,\ 0]$. The textbook formula asserts, with 95% confidence, that the probability is exactly zero. From twenty trials.

Wilson: denominator $1 + 3.8416/20 = 1.19208$; centre $= (0 + 3.8416/40)/1.19208 = 0.09604/1.19208 = 0.08056$; half-width $= 1.96\sqrt{0 + 3.8416/1600}/1.19208 = 1.96\times0.049/1.19208 = 0.08056$. Interval: $[0,\ 0.161]$.

Which is usable: Wilson. It says the true probability could plausibly be anything up to about $16\%$, which is the honest reading of "we tried twenty times and it never happened".

Why Wald collapses: it estimates the standard error at $\hat p$, and $\hat p(1-\hat p) = 0$ at the boundary. Wilson instead asks which values of $p$ are consistent with the observation, evaluating the standard error at each candidate $p$ rather than at the estimate — which is why it never collapses and why it is the right default everywhere in this project.

This is the self-check in the stats.wilson_interval docstring: $(k, n) = (0, 20)$ must give $\text{lo} = 0$ and $\text{hi}$ well above $0.1$. If your implementation returns $[0, 0]$, you implemented Wald.

K.3★★
The "rule of three" says that after $n$ trials with zero successes, the 95% upper bound on the probability is about $3/n$. Derive it, and check it against K.2.
Show solution

Derivation. If the true probability is $p$, the chance of seeing zero successes in $n$ independent trials is $(1-p)^{n}$. The largest $p$ still "not rejected" at the 5% level is the one making that probability equal to $0.05$:

$$(1-p)^{n} = 0.05 \implies n\log(1-p) = \log 0.05 \implies -np \approx -3,$$

using $\log(1-p) \approx -p$ for small $p$ and $\log 0.05 = -2.996$. So $p \approx 3/n$. ∎

Check: $n = 20$ gives $3/20 = 0.15$, against Wilson's $0.161$. Close enough to be a useful mental arithmetic tool, and it errs slightly low.

Worth knowing because it is the one interval you can compute in your head, and because it makes the right point instantly: "we saw no failures in 20 runs" is compatible with a 15% failure rate. Useful whenever someone says a test suite passed.

K.4★★ check-in
Find the error. "My two accuracy points are $0.60$ and $0.80$, each from 40 trials. Their 95% error bars overlap, so the two are not significantly different."
Show solution

The error: overlapping confidence intervals do not imply a non-significant difference. The test is on the difference, and the standard error of a difference of independent quantities adds in quadrature, not linearly — so it is smaller than the sum of the two individual half-widths.

The numbers.

$$\mathrm{SE}_1 = \sqrt{\frac{0.6 \times 0.4}{40}} = \sqrt{0.006} = 0.0775,\qquad \mathrm{SE}_2 = \sqrt{\frac{0.8 \times 0.2}{40}} = \sqrt{0.004} = 0.0632.$$

Individual 95% intervals: $[0.448,\ 0.752]$ and $[0.676,\ 0.924]$. They overlap between $0.676$ and $0.752$.

But the difference:

$$\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_2 - \hat p_1) = \sqrt{0.006 + 0.004} = \sqrt{0.010} = 0.100, \qquad z = \frac{0.80 - 0.60}{0.100} = 2.00 > 1.96.$$

Significant at 5% — the opposite of the student's conclusion.

The general rule: non-overlapping intervals do imply significance, but overlapping intervals imply nothing. The overlap test is conservative, roughly corresponding to a much stricter significance level. If you want to compare two quantities, compute the interval on their difference. If you want to display a comparison, plot the difference with its own interval.

This matters directly in Week 12, where you compare the bead points at $n = 10$ and $n = 200$. Week 6 computed the standard error of exactly that difference — $0.1025$ for the 30/35 design — and got an expected $z$ of $2.03$, which is how the design's power was known in advance.

K.5★★★
To put a confidence interval on your fitted exponent, do you bootstrap the five fitted points or the individual trials? Argue for one, and identify a subtlety that arises in the bootstrap replicates.
Show solution

Bootstrap the trials. The correct procedure: for each draw size $n$ with $k$ correct out of $t$ trials, resample $t$ trial outcomes with replacement (equivalently draw $k^{*} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(t, k/t)$), recompute the accuracy, convert to $d'$, refit the slope, and repeat a few thousand times. The spread of the fitted slopes is the sampling distribution you want.

Why not the five points. Resampling the five $(n, d')$ pairs treats them as an exchangeable sample from a population of points. They are not: the draw sizes were chosen by you and are fixed by design. Resampling them would mean sometimes fitting through three copies of $n = 10$ and no $n = 200$, which corresponds to no experiment anyone could have run, and it conflates the (zero) uncertainty in the design with the (large) uncertainty in the measurements.

The subtlety. In a bootstrap replicate a point can land at or below chance, and $d' = 2\Phi^{-1}(p) \le 0$ has no logarithm, so that point drops out of the fit. This is not rare: at $n = 10$ the true accuracy is about $0.567$ and the standard error on 40 trials is $\sqrt{0.567\times0.433/40} = 0.078$, so falling to $0.5$ or below is a $0.86$-sigma event — roughly one replicate in five.

The consequence is that the bootstrap distribution is a mixture: replicates fitted through five points and replicates fitted through four (usually missing the leftmost, which is the one with the most leverage on a slope). That mixture is wider and biased relative to a clean five-point fit, and it is the honest answer — but you must report it: how many replicates lost a point, and which. beads.fit_power_law raises a ValueError rather than returning a silent NaN when fewer than two points survive, which is what turns this from an invisible problem into a loud one.

A defensible alternative worth mentioning: fit in $d'$ space but with a model that does not require taking logs of the response — for instance least squares on $\log n$ against $d'^{2}$, which is linear through the origin under the theory and handles $d' = 0$ gracefully. If you go that route, say why, and check that it gives the same answer on the points that both methods can handle.

L.1★★ check-in

(a) $\lambda_1 = 5000 \times 0.0020 = 10$, $\lambda_2 = 5000 \times 0.0026 = 13$.

The depth is being spent on a quantity that has saturated.

Caveat to state explicitly: this conclusion depends entirely on $\varphi \approx 0.5$ being right. If the true dispersion were an order of magnitude smaller, $\varphi = 0.05$, the knee moves to $4000$ UMIs, option A sits near it and option B well past it, and depth becomes worth buying: $d'$ would be $0.4/\sqrt{0.05+0.04} = 1.333$ for A and $0.4/\sqrt{0.05+0.01} = 1.633$ for B, a $22\%$ gain. So the first recommendation is to check $\varphi$ on any existing pilot data before spending anything.

(ii) Detecting a $2\%$ rare type: A, and it is not close. This is a question about how many cells of that type you see at all, and depth per cell is nearly irrelevant.

$$\text{A: } 0.02 \times 2000 = 40 \text{ expected}, \qquad \text{B: } 0.02 \times 500 = 10 \text{ expected}.$$

With 10 cells you cannot characterize a population, cannot separate it from a doublet artefact, and cannot put a usable interval on its frequency: the Wilson interval for $10/500$ runs from about $1.1\%$ to $3.6\%$, while for $40/2000$ it runs from about $1.5\%$ to $2.7\%$. Worse, the variability matters as much as the mean — the number of rare cells in option B is roughly $\mathrm{Poisson}(10)$, so a run yielding 4 is not unusual, and 4 cells is nothing.

The recommendation in one sentence: "Take option A for both questions, but measure $\varphi$ on pilot data first, because if the dispersion is much smaller than $0.5$ the classification answer flips to B."

This is what the whole project is for. The answer to a budget question is not a rule of thumb; it is a calculation whose inputs are things you can measure, with a stated condition under which it reverses. Someone who can produce that in a meeting is doing research.
