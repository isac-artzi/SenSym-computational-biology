Week 9 Plain-language companion

Dropout and Where the Law Bends, Explained Slowly

Losing molecules is the same as sequencing less · the "collapse" test · the difference between the scaling law and the accuracy formula · more than two cell types
This is the simplified companion to Week 9: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 9 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence Dropout that loses each molecule with probability $q$ is mathematically identical to sequencing at depth $D(1-q)$, so it needs no new theory; what does fail at low depth is not the $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ law but the bell-curve shortcut inside $\Phi(d'/2)$ — and it fails in a known direction.

1  What dropout is

Between the cell and the data, molecules get lost: not captured, not copied, not read. In real 10x experiments only something like 10–30% of a cell's molecules make it into the data. Each molecule is lost more or less independently, like a coin flip.

2  Dropout is just a smaller scoop

Week 2's thinning rule says: if each molecule survives with probability $1 - q$, a Poisson count with rate $\lambda$ becomes a Poisson count with rate $(1-q)\lambda$. Exactly. So:

Dropout = less depth Sequencing at depth $D$ with dropout $q$ produces data with exactly the same probabilities as sequencing at depth $D_{\text{eff}} = D(1-q)$ with no dropout. No test, statistic or classifier can tell them apart.
Analogy · a leaky scoop A 5,000-bean scoop with holes that lose 60% of beans gives you about 2,000 beans, chosen at random. That is indistinguishable from using a 2,000-bean scoop in the first place.
Worked example 1 · what dropout costs

Depth 5,000 with 60% dropout ($q = 0.6$).

  1. Effective depth: $5000 \times (1 - 0.6) = 2000$.
  2. $d'$ goes as $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$, so it is multiplied by $\sqrt{0.4} \approx 0.63$.
  3. So 60% dropout costs you 37% of your separation — not 60%. The square root softens the blow.

For the standard marker gene ($p = 0.0040$ vs $0.0055$): depth 5000 with 60% dropout gives $d' = 0.97$, and depth 2000 with no dropout also gives $d' = 0.97$.

Bonus: you do not need to know the true dropout rate to test the theory. Whatever it is, it just moves you along the curve you already predicted.

3  The collapse test

How the figure works
  1. Simulate cells at many depths, with dropout $q = 0$, $0.3$ and $0.6$. Measure accuracy.
  2. Plot accuracy against the nominal depth $D$: three separate curves; the high-dropout curves are shifted right.
  3. Re-plot the same points against effective depth $D(1-q)$: if the theorem is right, all three curves land on top of each other — and on the theory curve.

Nothing is fitted. You chose $q$, so $1 - q$ is known before you run anything. That is what makes it a test.

5  Why the bell curve fails at low depth, and which way

When a gene's expected count is tiny, say 0.3, most cells show zero — about 74% of them. The distribution is a spike at 0, not a bell. A smooth symmetric bell cannot represent a spike, so the shortcut misjudges the accuracy.

Analogy · "did I see any at all?" At very low depth the best rule is basically "zero molecules → type 2; one or more → type 1". Both types have a big lump of probability at exactly zero, but different-sized lumps. That difference is real information. The bell curve smooths the lumps away and so underestimates how well you can do.

From the full chapter (8 genes, per-gene mass 0.004):

The shortcut is pessimistic: the exact answer is higher. The error is biggest when counts are small and the fold change is large. So if your simulated accuracy at low depth sits slightly above the theory line, that is expected; if it sits below, look for a bug.

Three numbers, three meanings
  • simulated vs exact — should agree within sampling error. If not: a bug in your code.
  • exact vs shortcut — the size of the bell-curve error. A finding, not a bug.
  • simulated vs shortcut — the sum of both. Only interpret after the first check passes.

6  More than two cell types

  • Chance changes. With $T$ types, guessing gets $1/T$. With five types, 80% accuracy means much more than with two. Always state the chance level.
  • Every pair has its own $d'$. T cells vs monocytes: far apart. CD4 vs CD8 T cells: close.
  • The hardest pair dominates. Overall error is at least the worst pair's error and at most the sum of all pairs' errors. Usually the closest pair accounts for most of the mistakes.

7  Check yourself

Quick check 1
Depth 8,000 with 75% dropout. What is the effective depth, and by what factor does $d'$ shrink?
Show answer
$8000 \times 0.25 = 2000$. $d'$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$.
Quick check 2
A protocol has 30% dropout. How much deeper must you sequence to get back the $d'$ of a no-dropout run?
Show answer
Effective depth is $0.7D$; to match $D_0$ you need $D = D_0/0.7 \approx 1.43\,D_0$ — about 43% more sequencing.
Quick check 3
At very low depth your measured accuracy is 2 points above $\Phi(d'/2)$. Bug or expected?
Show answer
Expected, if the exact Bayes accuracy is also about 2 points above. The shortcut is pessimistic at low counts. Compare measured with exact first; that is the bug check.
