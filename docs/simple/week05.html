Week 5 Plain-language companion

Many Genes and $\sqrt{k}$, Explained Slowly

Scoring a whole cell · why evidence adds like the sides of a right triangle · the full scaling law in one line
This is the simplified companion to Week 5: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 5 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence With many independent marker genes, the best rule gives each gene a fixed number of "points per molecule" and adds them up, and the separations combine like Pythagoras: $d'_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{d_1'^2 + d_2'^2 + \cdots}$ — so $k$ equally good genes give $\sqrt{k}$ times one gene, and $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth} \times k}$.

1  From one gene to a whole cell

A real cell gives you counts for many genes at once: $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k$. How do you combine them? The key assumption this week is:

The "naive" assumption Once you know a cell's type, its genes' counts are independent: knowing gene 1's count tells you nothing extra about gene 2's. (This is false in real cells — genes switch on in groups — and the full chapter names it as the main limitation. But it gives a clean, testable answer.)

With independence, the probability of a whole cell's counts is just the product of the single-gene Poisson probabilities, the same way the probability of two independent coin flips is the product.

2  Taking logs turns the rule into a points system

Products are awkward, so take logarithms: logs turn multiplication into addition. After the algebra in the full chapter, the best rule becomes:

$$\text{score} = \sum_{g=1}^{k}\Big[\,x_g \cdot \underbrace{\log\frac{\lambda_{1g}}{\lambda_{2g}}}_{\text{points per molecule}} \;-\; \underbrace{(\lambda_{1g} - \lambda_{2g})}_{\text{fixed handicap}}\Big], \qquad \text{call type 1 if score} > 0.$$
Analogy · a points-based judge Each gene is a judge. Every molecule of a gene that type 1 makes more of earns positive points for type 1; every molecule of a gene that type 2 makes more of earns negative points. Each judge also starts with a fixed handicap so that an "average" cell scores around zero. Add up all the judges; positive total → type 1.
Worked example 1 · scoring three cells with two genes

Gene A: $\lambda_1 = 8$, $\lambda_2 = 4$ (type 1 makes more). Gene B: $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 6$ (type 2 makes more).

  1. Points per molecule: gene A $\log(8/4) = \log 2 \approx +0.693$; gene B $\log(3/6) = \log 0.5 \approx -0.693$.
  2. Handicap: $-\big[(8-4) + (3-6)\big] = -1$.
  3. Cell with counts (A=9, B=2): $9(0.693) + 2(-0.693) - 1 = 3.85$ → type 1.
  4. Cell with (A=5, B=5): $5(0.693) - 5(0.693) - 1 = -1.0$ → type 2.
  5. Cell with (A=3, B=7): $3(0.693) - 7(0.693) - 1 = -3.77$ → type 2, confidently.

(Here "log" means the natural log, np.log.)

Two things worth noticing: the factorials $x_g!$ in the Poisson formula cancel out completely (they are the same under both hypotheses), and the score is a straight-line function of the counts: weights × counts + a constant. That is why, in Week 8, logistic regression — which also uses "weights × counts + constant" — can learn the same rule from data.

3  How separations combine: Pythagoras

Each gene has its own $d'_g$. How far apart are the cell types when you use all of them?

Evidence adds "in quadrature" $$d'_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{d_1'^2 + d_2'^2 + \cdots + d_k'^2}.$$ Square each gene's $d'$, add the squares, take the square root.
Why Pythagoras? Walk 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north: you are $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ blocks from the start, not 7. Independent genes are like perpendicular directions — each one moves you apart in its own direction, and perpendicular distances combine by Pythagoras. (The reason underneath: noise variances add for independent genes, as you saw in Week 2.)
Worked example 2 · three genes

Genes with $d'_g = 2, 3, 6$:

  1. Squares: $4, 9, 36$.
  2. Sum: $49$.
  3. Square root: $d'_{\text{total}} = 7$.

Now add a weak fourth gene with $d' = 1$: $\sqrt{49 + 1} = 7.07$. It barely helps. Weak genes hardly matter next to strong ones.

The most common mistake this week: adding $d'$ directly

Ten genes, each with $d' = 0.4$.

  • Wrong: $10 \times 0.4 = 4.0$ → accuracy $\Phi(2) = 97.7\%$.
  • Right: $\sqrt{10 \times 0.4^2} = \sqrt{10} \times 0.4 = 1.26$ → accuracy $\Phi(0.63) = 73.6\%$.

Those are wildly different predictions. Always add the squares.

Try it · combine genes
Type the $d'$ of each gene, separated by commas (e.g. 2, 3, 6). Or use the second box: $k$ copies of the same gene.

4  The $\sqrt{k}$ law and the full scaling law

If all $k$ genes are equally good, each with $d'_g = c$, then

$$d'_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{k \cdot c^2} = \sqrt{k}\;c.$$

And from Week 3, each gene's $c$ grows as $\sqrt{D}$. Put them together:

The Aim 1 result $$d' \propto \sqrt{D \times k}, \qquad \text{accuracy} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{2}\right).$$ Doubling the depth and doubling the number of markers buy you exactly the same thing. Nothing in this is fitted to data — given the rates and the depth, the accuracy is predicted.
Worked example 3 · planning a panel

You have 20 genes, each with $d' = 0.3$. You want 95% accuracy.

  1. Now: $d' = \sqrt{20} \times 0.3 = 1.34$, accuracy $= \Phi(0.67) \approx 74.9\%$.
  2. Target: $\Phi(z) = 0.95$ at $z = 1.645$, so you need $d' = 2 \times 1.645 = 3.29$.
  3. Option A, more genes: $\sqrt{k} \times 0.3 = 3.29$ → $\sqrt{k} = 10.97$ → $k \approx 120$ genes.
  4. Option B, more depth: $d'$ must rise by $3.29 / 1.34 = 2.45$×, so depth must rise by $2.45^2 \approx 6.0$×.

Both are "6× the data": 6× the genes (20 → 120) or 6× the depth. That is the symmetry of $\sqrt{D \times k}$.

5  The subtle part: new evidence, or the same evidence cut thinner?

"More genes help" is only true if the new genes carry new differences between the types.

Analogy · cutting a pizza If the two cell types differ by one "pizza" worth of expression, cutting that pizza into 16 slices instead of 4 does not give you more pizza. Each slice is smaller, and the total evidence stays the same. Only if the types differ in more places (more pizzas) does $d'$ grow as $\sqrt{k}$.

In the code this is the difference between holding the marker mass per gene fixed (new genes = new evidence, $d' \propto \sqrt{k}$) and holding the total marker mass fixed (same evidence sliced thinner, $d'$ does not change with $k$). If your simulation doubles $k$ and nothing improves, check which one you used before deciding the law is wrong. Whenever you write "$d' \propto \sqrt{k}$", add "at fixed marker mass per gene".

6  Check yourself

Quick check 1
Combine genes with $d' = 3$ and $d' = 4$.
Show answer
$\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.
Quick check 2
100 genes, each $d' = 0.37$. Total $d'$ and accuracy?
Show answer
$\sqrt{100} \times 0.37 = 3.7$. Accuracy $\Phi(1.85) \approx 96.8\%$.
Quick check 3
One excellent gene ($d' = 3$) plus thirty poor ones ($d' = 0.2$ each). What is the total, and how much did the thirty contribute?
Show answer
Squares: $9 + 30 \times 0.04 = 9 + 1.2 = 10.2$. Total $= \sqrt{10.2} \approx 3.19$. The thirty genes raised $d'$ from 3.00 to 3.19 — they supply about 12% of the squared total (1.2 of 10.2).
Quick check 4
You double the depth and double the number of (equally good) genes. By what factor does $d'$ change?
Show answer
$\sqrt{2 \times 2} = 2$. $d'$ doubles.
