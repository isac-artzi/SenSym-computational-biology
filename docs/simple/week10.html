Real Data and Markers, Explained Slowly

Why we keep the raw counts · where the labels come from · how markers are ranked · the "peeking" trap · fold change versus $d'$ again · why downsampling is fair
This is the simplified companion to Week 10: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 10 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence Real data only tests the theory if you keep the counts as raw whole numbers, choose your marker genes using only training cells, and remember that the labels themselves came from a computation, not from a microscope.

1  The dataset in plain words

PBMC 3k is about 2,700 white blood cells from one healthy donor, sequenced by 10x Genomics. It is the standard teaching dataset of the field. The numbers in it are UMI counts: each molecule gets a random barcode before copying, so duplicates can be collapsed back to one original molecule. For this project, "UMI count" and "molecules captured" mean the same thing.

2  Why we must not normalize the counts

Almost every tutorial starts by dividing each cell's counts by its total and taking a log. That is fine for drawing pretty cluster plots. It is fatal here.

Example · what normalizing destroys

A gene count of 4 out of a total of 2,000 molecules becomes $\log(1 + 4/2000 \times 10{,}000) = \log 21 \approx 3.04$.

That number is no longer a whole-number count, and its variance no longer equals its mean. The Poisson structure — the thing the whole project measures — is gone. So the data loader keeps the raw integer counts and uses the normalized copy only to produce the cluster labels.

3  The labels are not ground truth

Nobody looked at these cells and identified them. The labels come from a computation on the same counts: cluster the cells, then name each cluster by which known marker genes it expresses. That is a standard, well-accepted annotation — but it is a cited label, not an independent measurement. Say so in the report.

4  Ranking marker genes

For each gene, compute its average count in each type, $m^{(1)}$ and $m^{(2)}$, then score it by

$$\text{score} = \left|\log_2 \frac{m^{(1)} + 1}{m^{(2)} + 1}\right|.$$
PieceWhy it is there
$\log_2$ of a ratiofold change on a doubling scale: 1 means 2×, 2 means 4×
absolute valueyou do not care which type is higher
the $+1$ ("pseudocount")stops tiny averages from producing huge, meaningless ratios
Worked example 1 · what the $+1$ does

Gene A averages 0.05 and 0.25 counts. Gene B averages 30 and 45.

  1. Raw fold changes: A is $0.25/0.05 = 5$×. B is $45/30 = 1.5$×. A looks far better.
  2. With the $+1$: A scores $|\log_2(1.25/1.05)| = 0.25$. B scores $|\log_2(46/31)| = 0.57$. Now B ranks higher.
  3. Check with $d'$ (Week 3): A has $d' = 0.52$, B has $d' = 2.45$. B really is about five times as useful.

The pseudocount pushes the ranking toward what actually matters — but it is a choice, and a different pseudocount gives a different gene list. State it in the report.

5  Peeking: this week's real lesson

Selection leakage If you use cells' labels to choose which genes to use, and then measure accuracy on those same cells, the accuracy is too high. The test cells helped pick the features, so they are no longer a fair test.
Analogy · picking "lucky" students Give 13,000 students a coin-flipping test. Pick the 20 who scored highest and announce they are "gifted coin-flippers". Retest them with new flips and they score average. The first scores were the luckiest of thousands, not skill.

Same with genes. Even if no gene truly differed between two groups, the 13,000 measured differences would scatter around zero, and the biggest of them would land about 3.8 standard deviations out — purely by chance. Rank by difference and the top of the list contains real markers plus lucky ones.

The honest protocol
  1. Split cells into training and test first, before looking at anything.
  2. Do everything that reads labels — picking markers, estimating rates, fitting, choosing thresholds — on training cells only.
  3. Touch the test set once, at the end, for one number.

Rule that catches every case: any step that reads the labels is part of the model.

6  Downsampling: making real data shallower, fairly

To trace accuracy against depth with real data, you need the same cells at many depths. You cannot re-sequence them, but you can downsample: keep each captured molecule with probability $q$. By Week 2's thinning rule, that produces exactly what sequencing at depth $qD$ would have produced (as long as the original counts were Poisson). It is the same knob as dropout. Week 11 uses it.

7  Check yourself

Quick check 1
Score a gene with averages 3 and 7 using the pseudocount formula.
Show answer
$|\log_2(4/8)| = |\log_2 0.5| = 1$. (A 2× difference after the +1.)
Quick check 2
You pick the top 20 markers using all 2,700 cells, then split 70/30 and report test accuracy. What is wrong?
Show answer
The test cells' labels were used to pick the markers, so the test is contaminated and the accuracy is inflated. Split first, then pick markers from the 70% only.
Quick check 3
Why is it fine to downsample real counts but not fine to normalize them?
Show answer
Downsampling keeps whole-number counts and, by thinning, keeps them Poisson with a lower rate — exactly a shallower experiment. Normalizing turns counts into non-integers whose variance no longer equals their mean, which destroys the structure being tested.
