The whole week in one sentence Every number you measured needs an honest range of values it is compatible with, and every claim you make needs a label saying how you know it — proved, cited, observed or conjectured.

1  The textbook interval and its two failures

AP Statistics teaches the interval $\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$ (called the Wald interval). It works well for large $n$ and proportions near 50%. Your bead points have $n = 40$ and some accuracies near 90–100%, which is exactly where it breaks:

Example · the Wald interval behaving badly
ResultWald intervalProblem
40 out of 40$[1.00,\ 1.00]$zero width — "certainly 100%", from 40 trials?
36 out of 40$[0.807,\ 0.993]$squeezed against 1 and too narrow
3 out of 10$[0.016,\ 0.584]$nearly touches 0; can go negative for smaller counts

The cause: the formula estimates the wobble using $\hat p$ itself. When $\hat p = 1$, the estimated wobble is $\sqrt{1 \times 0} = 0$.

2  The Wilson interval: a better idea

The key idea, in words A confidence interval is the set of true values your data does not rule out. For each candidate true value $p_0$, ask "if the truth were $p_0$, would a result like mine be surprising (more than 1.96 standard deviations away)?" — computing the standard deviation from $p_0$, not from your data. Keep every $p_0$ that passes. That set is the Wilson interval.

Solving that inequality gives a formula (in the full chapter). A handy way to remember what it does:

"Add two successes and two failures" The centre of the Wilson interval is almost exactly $\dfrac{k + 2}{n + 4}$: pretend you had two more right answers and two more wrong ones. It refuses to believe an extreme result from few trials, and the pull fades as $n$ grows.
Worked example 1 · Wilson versus Wald
ResultWaldWilson
40 / 40[1.000, 1.000][0.912, 1.000]
36 / 40[0.807, 0.993][0.769, 0.960]
28 / 40[0.558, 0.842][0.546, 0.819]
3 / 10[0.016, 0.584][0.108, 0.603]

For 36/40, the "add two and two" centre is $38/44 \approx 0.864$ — close to the true Wilson centre 0.865, and below the raw 0.900. Wilson never leaves $[0, 1]$ and never has zero width.

3  What "95% confident" actually promises

Analogy · a ring toss The true value is a peg you cannot see. Each experiment throws a ring (an interval). A "95% method" is one whose rings land around the peg 95% of the time, over many throws. Any single ring either caught the peg or did not — you never find out which. The 95% is a promise about the method, not about one interval.

This promise is called coverage, and the full chapter has you check it by simulation: generate many fake experiments with a known true value, and count how often the interval catches it. Wilson stays close to 95%; Wald drops well below near 0 and 1.

4  The bootstrap: error bars for anything

For a proportion there is a formula. For something complicated — a fitted slope, a $d'$, a median — there often is not. The bootstrap gets an interval by brute force:

The recipe
  1. From your $n$ data values, draw $n$ values with replacement (some repeat, some are left out).
  2. Compute your statistic on this "resample".
  3. Repeat 2,000 times.
  4. The middle 95% of those 2,000 values (2.5th to 97.5th percentile) is the interval.
Example · three resamples by hand

Data: 4, 7, 7, 9, 13 (mean 8.0). Three resamples, each of size 5:

  • 7, 4, 13, 7, 7 → mean 7.6
  • 9, 9, 13, 4, 7 → mean 8.4
  • 4, 4, 7, 9, 4 → mean 5.6

Do that 2,000 times, and the spread of the means tells you how much the mean could have wobbled.

What the bootstrap cannot do It only knows the data you gave it. If your data is biased (a jar that was not well mixed, a leaky marker list), the bootstrap reproduces the bias with a tight, confident-looking interval. It measures wobble, not mistakes.

5  The permutation test: "could this be luck?"

You have two groups (say, beads with and without dropout at the same effective $n$) and they differ. Could the difference be chance? Pretend the group labels mean nothing, shuffle them, and see how often shuffling alone produces a difference this big.

Worked example 2 · a permutation test you can do on paper

Group $a$: 5, 7, 9 (mean 7). Group $b$: 1, 2, 3 (mean 2). Observed difference: 5.

  1. Pool all six: 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9.
  2. There are $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ ways to choose which three are "group $a$".
  3. A difference of 5 or more happens only for $\{5, 7, 9\}$ vs $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and the reverse: 2 out of 20.
  4. $p = 2/20 = 0.10$.

Even though the groups look completely separated, with three values each, pure shuffling produces a split this extreme 10% of the time. Small samples cannot prove much. (With many values, you shuffle randomly a few thousand times instead of listing every split.)

6  Many tests at once

Compare theory and measurement at 8 depths, each at the 5% level, and even if the theory is perfect the chance that at least one comparison looks "significant" is

$$1 - 0.95^8 \approx 0.34.$$

A one-in-three chance of a false alarm. Bonferroni's fix: test each of the $m$ comparisons at level $0.05/m$ — here $0.05/8 = 0.00625$. Then the chance of any false alarm is at most 5%.

7  The claim ledger

Every sentence in the report that claims something gets a label:

LabelMeansExample from this project
provedyou can derive it at the whiteboard, without notes"$d' \propto \sqrt{D}$ for Poisson counts"
citedsomeone else showed it; you give the reference"the PBMC cell-type labels" (from the standard annotation)
observedyou measured it, with a seed, a sample size and an interval"bead slope 0.46, 95% interval [0.31, 0.62]" (your numbers will differ)
conjecturedyou believe it, and say it is a belief"correlated genes explain most of the gap to theory"
"I read a proof once" is not "proved" If you cannot reproduce it on demand, label it cited and give the reference. There is no shame in cited — the shame is in a label that does not match what you can actually do.

8  Check yourself

Quick check 1
You got 20 out of 20 right. Using "add two and two", estimate the Wilson centre.
Show answer
$22/24 \approx 0.917$. (The exact Wilson interval is about $[0.84, 1.00]$ — a long way from "certainly 100%".)
Quick check 2
You run 5 comparisons. What per-test level does Bonferroni require?
Show answer
$0.05/5 = 0.01$.
Quick check 3
Label each: (a) "accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$ for equal-variance Gaussians"; (b) "real counts have Fano factor above 1 in PBMC monocytes"; (c) "this would also hold for neurons".
Show answer
(a) proved, if you can derive it from Week 4. (b) observed — with the number of cells and an interval. (c) conjectured — you have not measured neurons.
