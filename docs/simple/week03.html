Week 3 Plain-language companion

The Separation Index $d'$, Explained Slowly

"How many wobbles apart are the two cell types?" · why it grows as the square root of depth · the 4× rule with real numbers
This is the simplified companion to Week 3: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 3 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence $d'$ ("d-prime") measures how far apart two cell types are in units of counting noise, and because the noise is $\sqrt{\text{count}}$, $d'$ grows as $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$: to double $d'$ you must sequence four times as deeply.

1  The problem, as a guessing game

A cell is either type 1 or type 2. For one gene, type 1 cells average $\lambda_1$ molecules and type 2 cells average $\lambda_2$. You see one count from one cell. Which type is it?

Analogy · two basketball players

Player A averages 16.5 points a game; player B averages 12. You are told one game's score, 14, and asked who played. Hard! Both players have good and bad nights, and their score ranges overlap.

Now suppose A averages 66 and B averages 48. Told "62", you would bet on A with some confidence. The gap is bigger compared with how much each player's score bounces around.

$d'$ is that comparison turned into a number.

2  The formula, piece by piece

$$d' = \frac{|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda}}, \qquad \bar\lambda = \frac{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}{2}.$$
PieceWhat it means
$|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|$the gap between the two types' average counts (the vertical bars mean "ignore the sign")
$\bar\lambda$the average of the two averages — a "typical" count for this gene
$\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$the typical wobble. Remember Week 1: a Poisson count with mean $\lambda$ has sd $\sqrt{\lambda}$.
$d'$gap ÷ wobble = "how many wobbles apart" the two types are
Worked example 1 · the course's standard marker gene

At depth $D = 3000$, a marker gene has $\lambda_1 = 12$ and $\lambda_2 = 16.5$.

  1. Gap: $|12 - 16.5| = 4.5$.
  2. Typical count: $\bar\lambda = (12 + 16.5)/2 = 14.25$.
  3. Typical wobble: $\sqrt{14.25} \approx 3.775$.
  4. $d' = 4.5 / 3.775 \approx 1.19$.

The types are about 1.2 wobbles apart: real, but heavily overlapping.

A rough feel for the scale:

$d'$What it feels like
0identical — pure guessing
about 1noticeable difference, lots of overlap, many mistakes
about 2clearly different, still some mistakes
4 or morealmost never confused

Week 4 turns $d'$ into an exact percentage. You do not need that yet.

3  Why the bottom has a square root (and why that is special)

For most kinds of data you would have to measure the wobble separately; it could be anything. For Poisson counts you do not: the wobble is forced to be $\sqrt{\text{mean}}$ (Week 1, variance = mean). So $d'$ needs nothing except the two averages. No extra knob, nothing to fit.

Keep a finger on this step. In Week 11 real data turns out not to have variance = mean, and this is precisely the line that breaks.

4  The depth law: $d' \propto \sqrt{D}$

Each count's average is depth × fraction: $\lambda_1 = Dp_1$ and $\lambda_2 = Dp_2$. Watch what happens when the depth goes up by a factor of 4:

Worked example 2 · quadruple the depth
  1. The gap gets 4× bigger (both averages are multiplied by 4, so their difference is too).
  2. The typical count gets 4× bigger, so the wobble $\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$ gets $\sqrt{4} = 2$× bigger.
  3. $d' = \dfrac{\text{gap}}{\text{wobble}}$ goes up by $\dfrac{4}{2} = 2$.

4× the data → 2× the separation. In general, multiplying depth by $m$ multiplies $d'$ by $\sqrt{m}$.

Written as algebra, plug $\lambda = Dp$ into the formula and pull $D$ out:

$$d' = \frac{D\,|p_1 - p_2|}{\sqrt{D\,\bar p}} = \sqrt{D}\cdot\frac{|p_1 - p_2|}{\sqrt{\bar p}}.$$

The second piece depends only on the biology (the two fractions). Only the $\sqrt{D}$ changes when you sequence more.

Worked example 3 · what each step up costs

Worked example 4 · same ratio, different depth

Compare $\lambda_1 = 100,\ \lambda_2 = 121$ with $\lambda_1 = 10,\ \lambda_2 = 12.1$ (the second is one-tenth the depth).

  1. First: gap 21, typical count 110.5, wobble 10.51, $d' \approx 2.00$.
  2. Second: gap 2.1, typical count 11.05, wobble 3.32, $d' \approx 0.63$.
  3. Ratio: $2.00 / 0.63 \approx 3.16 = \sqrt{10}$. Exactly what the depth law says.

5  Two things $d'$ is not

$d'$ is not a percentage $d' = 2$ does not mean "2% accurate" or "twice as good". It is a distance in wobble-units. Week 4 converts it to accuracy.
$d'$ is not the fold change

"Fold change" is the ratio $\lambda_2 / \lambda_1$. It ignores how big the counts are, and that is a trap:

Gene$\lambda_1$$\lambda_2$Fold change$d'$
A (rare)0.20.42.0×0.37
B (common)40551.375×2.18

Gene A has the "better" fold change but is nearly useless: most cells of both types show a count of 0. Gene B is six times more informative. Always compute the actual counts at your depth before trusting a marker.

6  Check yourself

Quick check 1
$\lambda_1 = 20$, $\lambda_2 = 30$. Compute $d'$.
Show answer
Gap 10. $\bar\lambda = 25$, wobble 5. $d' = 10/5 = 2$.
Quick check 2
That gene came from depth 2,000. What depth gives $d' = 4$? What depth gives $d' = 1$?
Show answer
$d'$ doubles when depth is ×4: $D = 8000$ for $d' = 4$. Halving $d'$ needs depth ÷4: $D = 500$ for $d' = 1$.
Quick check 3
Your budget triples the depth. By what factor does $d'$ improve?
Show answer
$\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73$. Tripling the money buys 73% more separation, not 200% more.
Quick check 4
Dropout keeps only a fraction $q = 0.25$ of molecules in both cell types. Using Week 2's thinning rule, what happens to $d'$?
Show answer
Both rates are multiplied by 0.25, which is the same as cutting the depth to a quarter. So $d'$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$ — it halves. (Week 9 is built on this.)
