The whole week in one sentence Drawing beads from a jar is the same counting problem as sequencing, so the same square-root law applies; and before you draw a single bead you must check that your error bars will be small enough to see the effect — if they will not be, change the jars, not the hope.

1  The Aim 1 argument as a chain of six links

The full chapter asks you to derive the accuracy formula with the book closed. Here is the chain in plain words. If you can say each link and why it follows from the one before, you are ready.

The chain
  1. A sequencer takes a random sample. Each of $D$ captured molecules belongs to gene $g$ with probability $p_g$. (Week 1)
  2. So a gene's count is binomial, and for rare genes, Poisson with mean $\lambda = Dp$ — and variance = mean, because $(1-p) \approx 1$. (Week 2)
  3. So the noise is $\sqrt{\lambda}$, and the separation between two types is gap ÷ noise: $d' = |\lambda_1 - \lambda_2| / \sqrt{\bar\lambda}$, which grows as $\sqrt{D}$. (Week 3)
  4. With the line drawn halfway, each type's centre is $d'/2$ wobbles from it, so accuracy $= \Phi(d'/2)$. (Week 4)
  5. Independent genes add in quadrature, so $k$ equal genes give $\sqrt{k}$ times one. (Week 5)
  6. Result: accuracy $= \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{Dk}\,c}{2}\right)$, where $c = |p_1 - p_2|/\sqrt{\bar p}$ is the per-gene biology.

And the assumptions you used: counts are Poisson (cells of a type identical, random capture), genes are independent, the two types are equally common, the rates are known, and counts are large enough for the bell-curve shortcut.

One difference: red is not rare in a jar (30%), so the bead count is binomial, not Poisson, and its variance is $n\bar p(1-\bar p)$ rather than $n\bar p$. The $d'$ formula picks up that extra factor:

$$d' = \frac{\sqrt{n}\;|p_A - p_B|}{\sqrt{\bar p\,(1-\bar p)}}.$$

But look at the front: $\sqrt{n}$. The same square-root law as depth.

Worked example 1 · the planned jars

$p_A = 0.30$, $p_B = 0.35$, so $\bar p = 0.325$.

  1. Gap: $|0.30 - 0.35| = 0.05$.
  2. Wobble per bead: $\sqrt{0.325 \times 0.675} = \sqrt{0.219} \approx 0.468$.
  3. Per-bead separation: $0.05 / 0.468 \approx 0.107$. So $d' \approx 0.107\sqrt{n}$.
  4. At $n = 100$ beads: $d' = 1.07$, accuracy $= \Phi(0.53) \approx 70\%$.

3  Error bars: how many trials do you need?

At each $n$ you will play the "which jar?" game some number of times — say 40 trials — and record the fraction you got right. That fraction is itself a noisy measurement. How noisy?

The size of an error bar on a proportion If the true chance of being right is $p$ and you do $N$ trials, a 95% error bar is roughly $$\pm\; 1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}}.$$ (This is the AP Statistics one-proportion interval.)
Worked example 2 · 40 trials

Suppose the true accuracy is 60% and you do $N = 40$ trials.

  1. $p(1-p) = 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.24$.
  2. $0.24 / 40 = 0.006$, and $\sqrt{0.006} \approx 0.0775$.
  3. Error bar: $\pm 1.96 \times 0.0775 \approx \pm 0.15$.

So "60%" really means "somewhere from 45% to 75%". Look at the 30/35 table: the whole curve from $n = 10$ to $n = 100$ only climbs from 57% to 70%. With ±15-point error bars, you could not tell the points apart.

Worked example 3 · turning it around

How many trials for an error bar of ±5 points at $p = 0.6$? Solve $1.96\sqrt{0.24/N} = 0.05$:

$$N = \frac{1.96^2 \times 0.24}{0.05^2} = \frac{3.8416 \times 0.24}{0.0025} \approx 369.$$

Halving the error bar costs four times the trials — the same 4× rule as depth, for the same reason.

4  The real lesson: make the effect bigger

Analogy · spotting a friend in a crowd If your friend is wearing grey in a grey crowd, you need a very long look. Ask them to wear bright orange and you spot them instantly. Both "look longer" and "wear orange" work — but one of them is free.

With jars 30/35 the effect is small and the experiment needs a lot of trials to detect it. With jars 30/40 the accuracy curve climbs from 63% to 93% across the same range of $n$ — big enough to see through ±15-point error bars. The full chapter computes the power (the chance your experiment detects the effect at all) for both designs and recommends widening the jars.

An experiment that cannot detect its effect is not an experiment Doing the arithmetic before collecting data takes twenty minutes. Doing it after a month of bead-counting, and finding the error bars swallow everything, costs the month. In sequencing you cannot choose your cell types; with beads you can choose your jars.

5  Check yourself

Quick check 1
With jars 30/35, how many beads per trial would you need for $d' = 2$?
Show answer
$0.107\sqrt{n} = 2$ → $\sqrt{n} = 18.7$ → $n \approx 350$ beads per trial. That is a lot of counting — another argument for wider jars.
Quick check 2
You do 160 trials instead of 40 at one point. What happens to the error bar?
Show answer
4× the trials → error bar shrinks by $\sqrt{4} = 2$. About ±7.5 points instead of ±15.
Quick check 3
Why do we put each bead back after drawing it?
Show answer
So every draw has the same chance of being red and the draws are independent. That makes the red count exactly binomial, which is the model the $d'$ formula assumes. (A jar with thousands of beads is close to this even without replacement, just as a cell with hundreds of thousands of molecules is.)
