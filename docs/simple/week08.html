Week 8 Plain-language companion

Classifiers From Scratch, Explained Slowly

The S-curve · what "loss" measures · gradient descent as walking downhill · why test accuracy is the only honest number · why nearest-neighbour struggles with many genes
This is the simplified companion to Week 8: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 8 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence Logistic regression is the Week 5 points system with the points learned from examples instead of computed from known rates; learning means repeatedly nudging each weight in the direction that reduces the mistakes, and the only accuracy you may report is the one measured on cells the model never saw.

1  The question this week answers

In Weeks 3–5 you knew the true rates, so you could compute the best possible rule. In real life you only have example cells with labels. Does a classifier that has to learn the rule do much worse? The answer this week: with a couple of thousand training cells, barely. The limit is the counting noise in the data, not the cleverness of the algorithm.

2  The S-curve (sigmoid)

Logistic regression computes the same kind of score as Week 5 — weights × counts + a constant, $z = w \cdot x + b$ — and then squashes it into a probability between 0 and 1 using the sigmoid function:

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.$$

Big positive score → almost certainly type 1. Big negative → almost certainly type 2. Score 0 → 50/50, which is the decision boundary.

3  Loss: a number that measures how wrong you are

To learn, the model needs a score for its own mistakes. Logistic regression uses log-loss: for each training cell, if the true answer is type 1 and the model said probability $p$, the penalty is $-\log p$.

Example · confident and right, unsure, confident and wrong
True type 1, model saysPenalty $-\log p$
$p = 0.9$ (confident, right)0.11
$p = 0.5$ (no idea)0.69
$p = 0.1$ (confident, wrong)2.30

Being confidently wrong is punished hardest. The loss is the average penalty over all training cells.

4  Gradient descent: walking downhill

Analogy · finding the bottom of a valley in fog You cannot see the whole valley. But you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. Take a small step downhill. Feel again. Step again. Eventually you reach the bottom. The "slope under your feet" is the gradient (the derivative from AP Calculus, one for each weight), and the step size is the learning rate $\eta$.

The full chapter derives the slope with the chain rule. The result is beautifully simple:

$$\frac{\partial\,\text{loss}}{\partial w_j} = \text{average over cells of } (p_i - y_i)\,x_{ij}.$$

In words: for each cell, take the error (predicted probability minus true label, 1 or 0), multiply by that cell's count for gene $j$, and average. Then update $w_j \leftarrow w_j - \eta \times \text{slope}$.

Worked example · one gene, four cells, two steps

Counts $x = 1, 2, 4, 5$ with labels $y = 0, 0, 1, 1$. Start at $w = 0$, $b = 0$; learning rate $\eta = 0.5$.

  1. Predict: every score is 0, so every $p = 0.5$.
  2. Errors $p - y$: $0.5, 0.5, -0.5, -0.5$.
  3. Slope for $w$: average of error × count $= (0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.5 \cdot 2 - 0.5 \cdot 4 - 0.5 \cdot 5)/4 = -3/4 = -0.75$.
  4. Slope for $b$: average of errors $= 0$.
  5. Step: $w = 0 - 0.5(-0.75) = 0.375$. Now high counts push toward type 1. ✓
  6. Step 2: new probabilities $0.59, 0.68, 0.82, 0.87$. The slopes are now $+0.14$ for $w$ and $+0.24$ for $b$, so $w$ eases back to $0.31$ and $b$ moves to $-0.12$, shifting the boundary toward the middle of the data.

Two useful facts hide in that example:

  • Cells that are already right stop mattering. If $p_i \approx y_i$, the error is about 0 and that cell barely moves the weights. Learning is driven by the confusing cells near the boundary.
  • The very first step points from the type-2 average toward the type-1 average. In the example, the type-1 mean count is 4.5 and the type-2 mean is 1.5; the first slope is exactly $-(4.5 - 1.5)/4$. So even one step already gets the direction right.
Why the provided sigmoid has two branches Computing $e^{-z}$ when $z = -5000$ overflows (the number is too big for the computer). The provided _sigmoid uses $1/(1+e^{-z})$ when $z \ge 0$ and the equivalent $e^{z}/(1+e^{z})$ when $z \lt 0$, so it never exponentiates a large positive number. Always call it instead of writing your own.

5  Train and test: the honesty rule

Analogy · the practice test and the real test If a teacher gives you the exact exam questions to practise on, your exam score says nothing about what you learned. A model is the same: its accuracy on cells it was trained on is inflated.
The three rules
  1. Split the cells once, at random, into training (70%) and test (30%). Report only test accuracy.
  2. Shuffle cells and labels with the same random order (the Week 7 bug again).
  3. Anything computed from data — averages for standardizing, chosen genes, thresholds — comes from the training cells only.

6  Nearest neighbours and too many genes

The $k$-nearest-neighbour classifier labels a cell by looking at the training cells closest to it. That works in 2 or 3 dimensions. With hundreds of genes, a strange thing happens: every cell is about the same distance from every other cell, so "nearest" stops meaning much.

Why distances bunch up A distance squared is a sum over genes of squared differences. By the Week 1 coin argument, a sum of $k$ independent pieces has mean $\propto k$ but wobble $\propto \sqrt{k}$. So the spread of distances, relative to their size, shrinks like $1/\sqrt{k}$. With 400 noisy genes, all distances are within a few percent of each other, and the handful of genes that actually differ get drowned out. Logistic regression does not have this problem: it learns to put near-zero weight on useless genes.

7  Check yourself

Quick check 1
A cell is type 2 ($y = 0$) and the model says $p = 0.8$. What is its error, and which way will it push the weight of a gene this cell has many counts of?
Show answer
Error $= 0.8 - 0 = +0.8$. The slope contribution is positive, and we step against the slope, so that gene's weight goes down: next time, high counts of that gene will push less toward type 1.
Quick check 2
Your classifier gets 99% on the training cells and 81% on the test cells. Which do you report? What might the gap mean?
Show answer
Report 81%. A big gap means the model memorised quirks of the training cells (overfitting). Compare the test number with the $\Phi(d'/2)$ prediction to see how much was lost.
Quick check 3
What is the log-loss for a type-1 cell predicted at $p = 0.99$? At $p = 0.01$?
Show answer
$-\log 0.99 \approx 0.010$. $-\log 0.01 \approx 4.6$. Confidently wrong costs about 460 times more.
