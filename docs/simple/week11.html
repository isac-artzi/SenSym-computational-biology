Week 11 Plain-language companion

Overdispersion and the Depth Ceiling, Explained Slowly

Real counts wobble more than Poisson · why: cells of one type are not identical · the formula $m + \varphi m^2$ · why sequencing deeper eventually stops helping
This is the simplified companion to Week 11: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 11 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence Real cells of the same type do not all have the same rate for a gene, so real counts carry an extra wobble that grows with depth just as fast as the signal does; that wobble can never be sequenced away, so $d'$ stops growing and hits a ceiling.

1  The measurement that breaks the model

Week 1 said: for Poisson counts, the Fano factor $F = \text{variance}/\text{mean}$ is 1. On real PBMC data, compute $F$ for each gene within one cell type, and you find $F$ well above 1, and growing for more highly expressed genes. That is called overdispersion: more variation than scoop-luck alone can explain. Something else is varying.

2  Why: every cell has its own rate

Analogy · jars that are not quite identical

In Week 1 every type-1 cell was the same jar of jelly beans. In real life, each type-1 cell is a slightly different jar: one is 0.15% red, the next 0.25%, the next 0.2%. Bigger cells, cells dividing, cells reacting to something — they all shift the recipe a little.

Now there are two sources of wobble when you scoop:

  1. scoop-luck (Poisson noise), as before, and
  2. jar-to-jar differences (biological variability), which is new.

The full chapter puts this into one formula. If the cell-to-cell rates have average $m$ and vary by a relative amount $\sqrt{\varphi}$ (the Greek letter "phi"), then the counts have

$$\text{mean} = m, \qquad \text{variance} = \underbrace{m}_{\text{scoop-luck}} + \underbrace{\varphi m^2}_{\text{cell-to-cell}}.$$

The two kinds of wobble simply add. When $\varphi = 0$ (all jars identical) it goes back to Poisson. When the rates are gamma-distributed this is called the negative binomial, but you only need the mean and variance.

How to read $\varphi$ $\sqrt{\varphi}$ is how much cells of the same type differ in their rate, as a fraction. $\varphi = 0.25$ means $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$: cells differ by about 50% from cell to cell. $\varphi = 0.04$ means about 20%.
Worked example 1 · how much extra wobble

A gene averages $m = 10$ with $\varphi = 0.25$.

  1. Scoop-luck variance: 10.
  2. Cell-to-cell variance: $0.25 \times 10^2 = 25$.
  3. Total variance: 35. Fano factor: $35/10 = 3.5$.

The new term is already bigger than the old one. And notice it grows with $m^2$ while scoop-luck grows with $m$ — so the deeper you sequence, the more the cell-to-cell term dominates.

3  Measuring $\varphi$ from data

Rearrange the variance formula: $\varphi = (\text{variance} - \text{mean}) / \text{mean}^2$. Equivalently, $F = 1 + \varphi m$. (If the answer comes out negative, set it to 0.)

Worked example 2 · estimating $\varphi$

Among monocytes, a gene has mean count 8 and variance 24.

  1. Extra variance beyond Poisson: $24 - 8 = 16$.
  2. Divide by mean squared: $16 / 64 = 0.25$.
  3. So $\varphi \approx 0.25$: monocytes differ by about 50% in this gene's rate.

4  The headline: a ceiling on $d'$

Week 3's $d'$ divided the gap by the Poisson wobble $\sqrt{\bar\lambda}$. With overdispersion, the wobble is bigger:

$$d' = \frac{|\lambda_1 - \lambda_2|}{\sqrt{\bar\lambda + \varphi\,\bar\lambda^2}}.$$

Now watch what happens as depth grows. Every $\lambda$ is depth × fraction, so:

  • the gap on top grows like depth;
  • the bottom eventually is dominated by $\varphi\bar\lambda^2$, whose square root also grows like depth.

Top and bottom grow at the same speed, so their ratio levels off. That level is the ceiling.

The Poisson column keeps climbing. The real column is flat almost from the start. Thirty times the sequencing buys almost nothing.

The ceiling in one formula If the two types differ by a fold change $f$, $$d'_\infty = \frac{2(f-1)}{(f+1)\sqrt{\varphi}}.$$ Depth is not in it. Neither is how highly the gene is expressed. Only the fold change and how variable cells of a type are.
Worked example 4 · plugging into the ceiling

$f = 2$, $\varphi = 0.25$:

  1. Top: $2(2 - 1) = 2$.
  2. Bottom: $(2 + 1)\sqrt{0.25} = 3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$.
  3. $d'_\infty = 2/1.5 = 1.33$, so the best this one gene can ever do is $\Phi(0.67) \approx 75\%$, at any depth.

5  What beats the ceiling, and what does not

IdeaWorks?Why
Sequence deeperNo (past the knee)The ceiling has no depth in it.
More marker genesYesPythagoras still applies: 32 genes each at 0.566 give $\sqrt{32}\times 0.566 = 3.2$, about 94.5%. (Caveat: if genes share their cell-to-cell variation, the gain is smaller.)
Better genesYesPick genes with big fold change and small $\varphi$ — rank by $f/\sqrt{\varphi}$, not $f$ alone.
More cellsFor averages, yes. For one cell, no.See below.
"Just measure more cells" — careful More cells let you pin down the average expression of a type very precisely. But classifying one particular cell uses only that cell's molecules. That cell's own rate really is different from the type's average, and no number of other cells changes that. (Week 1's Quick-check about "more cells vs more depth" returns here with real consequences.)

6  Check yourself

Quick check 1
A gene has mean 20 and $\varphi = 0.1$. What is its variance and Fano factor?
Show answer
Variance $= 20 + 0.1 \times 400 = 60$. Fano $= 60/20 = 3$. (Check: $1 + \varphi m = 1 + 2 = 3$ ✓.)
Quick check 2
Mean 5, variance 5.2. Estimate $\varphi$. Is this gene overdispersed in any meaningful way?
Show answer
$\varphi = 0.2/25 = 0.008$ — cells differ by only about 9%. Nearly Poisson; with a finite sample this could easily be zero.
Quick check 3
Compute the ceiling for $f = 3$, $\varphi = 0.25$, and the best possible single-gene accuracy.
Show answer
$d'_\infty = 2(2)/(4 \times 0.5) = 2$. Accuracy $\Phi(1) \approx 84\%$.
