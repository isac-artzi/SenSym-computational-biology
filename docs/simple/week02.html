Week 2 Plain-language companion

The Probability You Need, Explained Slowly

Expectation and variance in plain words · where the binomial and the Poisson come from · why "keeping only some molecules" changes nothing but the rate
This is the simplified companion to Week 2: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 2 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence Counting a rare gene among thousands of captured molecules is a binomial count; when the gene is rare the binomial becomes a Poisson, which is why variance = mean; and if you randomly throw away some molecules, the count stays Poisson with a smaller rate (thinning).

1  Expectation: a weighted average

The expectation $E[X]$ of a random number $X$ is its long-run average. You compute it by multiplying each possible value by how likely it is, then adding.

Worked example · a loaded die

A game pays you 0, 1 or 4 points with probabilities 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2.

  1. Multiply each value by its probability: $0 \times 0.5 = 0$, $1 \times 0.3 = 0.3$, $4 \times 0.2 = 0.8$.
  2. Add: $E[X] = 0 + 0.3 + 0.8 = 1.1$.

You will never actually win 1.1 points in one game. It is the average over many games.

Two rules you will use all the time:

  • Averages add. $E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$ — always, even if $X$ and $Y$ influence each other.
  • Constants come out. $E[3X + 2] = 3E[X] + 2$.

2  Variance: average squared distance from the average

Worked example · same game, its variance

The shortcut formula is $\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$.

  1. $E[X^2]$: square each value, then weight: $0^2(0.5) + 1^2(0.3) + 4^2(0.2) = 0 + 0.3 + 3.2 = 3.5$.
  2. $(E[X])^2 = 1.1^2 = 1.21$.
  3. $\mathrm{Var}(X) = 3.5 - 1.21 = 2.29$, so sd $= \sqrt{2.29} \approx 1.51$.

Variance follows different rules from expectation, and the difference matters a lot later:

  • Scaling squares. $\mathrm{Var}(3X) = 9\,\mathrm{Var}(X)$. (Tripling every value triples the distances, and variance uses squared distances.)
  • Shifting does nothing. $\mathrm{Var}(X + 2) = \mathrm{Var}(X)$. (Sliding everything over does not spread it out.)
  • Variances add only for independent things. $\mathrm{Var}(X + Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)$ if knowing $X$ tells you nothing about $Y$.
Example · why independence matters for variance but not for averages

Let $X$ be a fair coin (1 for heads, 0 for tails). Variance $= 0.25$.

Case A, independent: $Y$ is a second, separate coin. $X + Y$ can be 0, 1 or 2, with variance $0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$.

Case B, not independent: $Y$ is the same coin, $Y = X$. Then $X + Y = 2X$ is either 0 or 2, and its variance is $4 \times 0.25 = 1.0$ — twice as much as the "add the variances" rule predicts.

In both cases the average of $X + Y$ is 1. Averages did not care; variance did. Week 5 depends on exactly this: genes that move together do not add their noise the simple way.

3  The binomial: counting successes

Do something $D$ times. Each time it "succeeds" with probability $p$, independently. The number of successes is a binomial count, written $\mathrm{Binomial}(D, p)$.

For sequencing: each of the $D$ captured molecules is a "try", and it "succeeds" if it belongs to your gene (probability $p$). So the gene's count is $\mathrm{Binomial}(D, p)$.

Its mean and variance are

$$E[X] = Dp, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = Dp(1-p).$$
Why those formulas are believable

Think of $X$ as the sum of $D$ tiny 0-or-1 scores, one per molecule. Each tiny score has mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$ (the coin-flip calculation with $p$ instead of 0.5). Means add, so the total mean is $Dp$. The molecules are independent, so variances add too: $Dp(1-p)$. That is the whole proof, in words.

4  How a binomial turns into a Poisson

Look at the variance formula $Dp(1-p)$ and compare it with the mean $Dp$. They differ only by the factor $(1-p)$.

Worked example · a typical gene

$D = 3000$ molecules, $p = 0.002$.

  1. Mean: $Dp = 6$.
  2. Variance: $Dp(1-p) = 6 \times 0.998 = 5.988$.
  3. The Poisson answer would be variance $= 6$. The difference is 0.2% — invisible.

The whole table of probabilities agrees too: the binomial gives $P(X = 6) = 0.1608$ and the Poisson with $\lambda = 6$ gives $0.1606$.

Counter-example · the bead jar is not Poisson

Draw $D = 20$ beads from a jar that is 30% red ($p = 0.3$). Mean $= 6$, but variance $= 20 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 4.2$. That is 30% smaller than the mean, so the Poisson shortcut would be wrong here. This is why the bead weeks (6 and 12) use the binomial formula directly.

Rule of thumb: when $p$ is small (a gene is a tiny fraction of the cell), the factor $(1-p)$ is basically 1, the binomial is basically a Poisson, and variance = mean. That is where Week 1's "coincidence" comes from: it is not magic, it is just $(1-p) \approx 1$.

Try it · binomial versus Poisson
Change $D$ and $p$. When $p$ is small the two variances agree; when $p$ is large they do not.

5  Thinning: randomly losing molecules

Suppose each molecule in the cell is kept with probability $q$ and lost otherwise — independently, coin-flip style. This happens in real life in three ways:

  • sequencing less deeply (a smaller scoop),
  • dropout (molecules lost during chemistry),
  • discarding beads with a die roll in the Week 12 experiment.
The thinning rule If a count is Poisson with rate $\lambda$, and each item survives with probability $q$, then the survivors are exactly Poisson with rate $q\lambda$. Nothing else about the shape changes.
Analogy · a leaky bucket Raindrops land in a bucket at random, about 100 per minute. The bucket has holes, and each drop has a 50% chance of leaking out. What you collect still looks like random rain — just lighter rain, about 50 per minute. Thinning does not make the rain "clumpier" or "smoother"; it only turns down the rate.
The mistake almost everyone makes

"Half the molecules means half the noise." No.

Before ($\lambda = 100$)After keeping half ($\lambda = 50$)
sd $=\sqrt{\lambda}$107.07 (not 5!)
relative noise $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$10%14%

The absolute wobble went down, but only by $\sqrt{2}$. The wobble compared with the count went up. Losing data always hurts the relative noise.

6  Check yourself

Quick check 1
A value is 2 with probability 0.25 and 6 with probability 0.75. Find its mean and variance.
Show answer
Mean $= 2(0.25) + 6(0.75) = 0.5 + 4.5 = 5$. $E[X^2] = 4(0.25) + 36(0.75) = 1 + 27 = 28$. Variance $= 28 - 25 = 3$.
Quick check 2
$D = 4000$, $p = 0.001$. Compute the binomial mean and variance. Is Poisson a good approximation?
Show answer
Mean $= 4$, variance $= 4 \times 0.999 = 3.996$. Yes — they differ by 0.1%.
Quick check 3
A gene has $\lambda = 36$. Dropout keeps only 25% of molecules. What is the new rate, the new sd, and the new relative noise?
Show answer
New rate $= 0.25 \times 36 = 9$. New sd $= 3$ (was 6). Relative noise goes from $1/6 \approx 17\%$ to $1/3 \approx 33\%$. Keeping a quarter of the molecules doubled the relative noise ($\sqrt{4} = 2$).
Quick check 4
Using $P(X=k) = \lambda^k e^{-\lambda}/k!$ with $\lambda = 3$, compute $P(X = 0)$ and $P(X = 1)$.
Show answer
$e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$. $P(X=0) = 3^0 \times 0.0498 / 0! = 0.0498$ (about 5%). $P(X = 1) = 3 \times 0.0498 / 1 = 0.149$ (about 15%).
