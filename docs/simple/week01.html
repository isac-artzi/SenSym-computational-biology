Week 1 Plain-language companion

Counting Noise, Explained Slowly

Why a sequencer gives you a sample, what "Poisson" means, and why every square root in this project starts here
This is the simplified companion to Week 1: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 1 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence A sequencer does not measure how much of each gene a cell has; it grabs a small random handful of the cell's molecules and counts them, so every number it reports is wobbly, and the wobble follows a simple law: the bigger the count, the smaller the wobble compared with the count.

1  The picture to keep in your head

Analogy · the giant jar of jelly beans

Imagine a cell as a jar holding 300,000 jelly beans. Each colour is a gene. Maybe 0.2% of the beans are red (that is one gene), 1% are green (another gene), and so on for 20,000 colours.

The sequencer reaches in and pulls out a scoop of about 3,000 beans, then reports how many of each colour were in the scoop. It never sees the whole jar.

If 0.2% of the jar is red, you expect about $0.002 \times 3000 = 6$ red beans in the scoop. But scoop again and you might get 4, or 9, or 5. That scoop-to-scoop wobble is counting noise, and it is what this whole project is about.

Two words you will see constantly:

2  What "Poisson" means, without the formula first

When you take a big scoop and look for a rare colour, the count you get follows a very specific pattern called the Poisson distribution. It tells you how likely each count is. For example, if you expect $\lambda = 2$ red beans per scoop:

Red beans in the scoop0123456+
How often (out of 100 scoops)13.527.127.118.09.03.61.7

Notice: even though you "expect" 2, you get exactly 2 only about a quarter of the time, and in about 1 scoop out of 7 you get none at all. That zero is not a broken machine — it is just bad luck in the scoop. This is why single-cell data is full of zeros.

The formula that produced that table is

$$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}.$$

You do not need to love it yet. Here is how to read it with $\lambda = 2$ and $k = 3$:

Worked example · plugging into the Poisson formula
  1. $\lambda^k = 2^3 = 8$.
  2. $e^{-\lambda} = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$ (this same number appears for every $k$; it just makes the whole table add up to 1).
  3. $k! = 3! = 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$.
  4. Put it together: $\dfrac{8 \times 0.1353}{6} \approx 0.180$, i.e. 18.0 scoops out of 100. That matches the table.

3  The one fact everything rests on: variance equals mean

Every distribution has a mean (the average value) and a spread. Spread is measured two ways:

  • Variance: the average of the squared distances from the mean.
  • Standard deviation (sd): the square root of the variance. It is in the same units as the count, so it is the one to picture: "typically off by about this much".

The special thing about a Poisson count is that its variance is exactly equal to its mean. So if you expect $\lambda$, the standard deviation is $\sqrt{\lambda}$. You do not get to choose the spread separately; the mean decides it for you.

Read the table carefully. The absolute wobble grows (2 → 10 → 100), but the wobble compared with the count shrinks (50% → 10% → 1%). Bigger counts are more trustworthy, but only as a square root: to cut the relative noise by 10×, you need 100× the count.

Common confusion "Deeper sequencing removes the noise." Not quite. Deeper sequencing makes the noise bigger in absolute terms but smaller compared with the signal. It is the second one that matters for telling cell types apart.

4  The Fano factor: a one-number test for "is this Poisson?"

Since variance = mean for Poisson counts, the ratio

$$F = \frac{\text{variance}}{\text{mean}}$$

should be about 1. This ratio is called the Fano factor. If you measure a gene across many cells of the same type and get $F$ much bigger than 1, something besides scoop-luck is making the counts vary (in Week 11 you will find out what).

Worked example · computing a Fano factor by hand

Eight cells of the same type give these counts for one gene: 3, 5, 4, 6, 2, 4, 5, 3.

  1. Mean. Add them: $3+5+4+6+2+4+5+3 = 32$. Divide by 8: mean $= 4$.
  2. Distances from the mean: $-1, 1, 0, 2, -2, 0, 1, -1$.
  3. Square them: $1, 1, 0, 4, 4, 0, 1, 1$. Sum $= 12$.
  4. Variance. Divide by $n - 1 = 7$ (not by 8 — see the box below): $12/7 \approx 1.71$.
  5. Fano factor: $1.71 / 4 \approx 0.43$.

Is that "less than Poisson"? Probably not. With only 8 cells the Fano factor itself is very wobbly. Try a second set: 12, 30, 5, 22, 9, 18. Mean 16, variance 84.4, so $F \approx 5.3$ — far above 1, and that is suspicious.

Why divide by $n-1$ instead of $n$? You computed the distances from the sample's own mean, which is always a little closer to the data than the true mean is. Dividing by $n - 1$ corrects for that. In NumPy this is np.var(x, ddof=1). Your Week 1 test checks this.

5  Why square roots keep showing up

Here is the argument behind every square root you will meet this semester, told with coins.

Example · flipping coins

Flip a fair coin and score 1 for heads. One flip has mean 0.5 and variance 0.25 (sd 0.5).

Now flip $n$ coins and add the scores. Two facts:

  • Means add, always: the total has mean $0.5n$.
  • Variances add, if the flips are independent: the total has variance $0.25n$, so its sd is $0.5\sqrt{n}$.

The signal (mean) grows like $n$; the noise (sd) grows like $\sqrt{n}$; so signal-to-noise grows like $n/\sqrt{n} = \sqrt{n}$. That is the entire reason for every square-root law in this course — for depth (Week 3), number of genes (Week 5), number of beads (Week 12), and the width of error bars (Week 13).

A counted gene is like this: each captured molecule is a "flip" that either is or is not that gene. More molecules means signal grows faster than noise, but only as a square root.

6  Evidence versus derivation

You will hear this distinction every week, so here it is in everyday terms:

  • Evidence ("I ran it 10 times and it looked right") tells you something happened in the cases you tried.
  • A derivation ("starting from these assumptions, step by step, it must be true") tells you it holds in every case where the assumptions hold — and, just as importantly, it tells you which assumption to blame when it fails.

Example: the coin argument above used "the flips are independent". If someone hands you coins that are glued together in pairs, you know immediately which step breaks. A pile of simulations would not tell you that.

7  Check yourself

Quick check 1
A gene is 0.5% of a cell's molecules ($p = 0.005$) and the depth is $D = 2000$. What count do you expect, and what is the typical wobble?
Show answer
$\lambda = 2000 \times 0.005 = 10$. sd $= \sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$. So you would typically see somewhere around 7 to 13, and the relative noise is about 32%.
Quick check 2
You want that gene's relative noise to be 10% instead of 32%. By what factor must the depth go up?
Show answer
Relative noise is $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$. For 10% you need $\sqrt{\lambda} = 10$, so $\lambda = 100$. That is 10× the current $\lambda = 10$, so 10× the depth: $D = 20{,}000$. Cutting noise by about 3.2× costs 10× the data — the square root again.
Quick check 3
Two cell types have expected counts 9 and 11 for a gene. Would you trust one cell's count to tell you its type?
Show answer
No. Each count wobbles by about 3 ($\sqrt{9} = 3$, $\sqrt{11} \approx 3.3$), but the two types are only 2 apart. A type-1 cell will often show 11 and a type-2 cell will often show 9. Week 3 turns this "gap compared with wobble" idea into a number called $d'$.
Quick check 4
Counts for one gene in six cells: 2, 0, 1, 3, 1, 5. Compute the mean, the variance (divide by $n-1$) and the Fano factor.
Show answer
Mean $= 12/6 = 2$. Distances: $0, -2, -1, 1, -1, 3$; squares: $0, 4, 1, 1, 1, 9$, sum 16. Variance $= 16/5 = 3.2$. Fano $= 3.2/2 = 1.6$. With six cells, that is well within what pure Poisson luck can produce, so you cannot conclude much — which is exactly the lesson of Lab 2 in the full chapter.
