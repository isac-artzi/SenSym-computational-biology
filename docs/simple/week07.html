Week 7 Plain-language companion

The Simulator, Explained Slowly

Why simulate when you have a formula · the three-rule recipe for a fake cell · the algebra for choosing the two rates · reading the accuracy table along its diagonals
This is the simplified companion to Week 7: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 7 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence A simulator builds fake cells by literally following your assumptions — pick a rate, draw a Poisson count — and classifies them; because it never uses the formula's shortcuts, agreement with $\Phi(d'/2)$ is real evidence, and disagreement tells you exactly where to look.

1  Why simulate at all?

Analogy · checking a math answer a second way You solve an integral with a clever substitution. To check, you do not redo the same substitution — you estimate the area by counting squares on graph paper. The two methods share no steps, so if they agree, both are probably right. If they disagree, one has a mistake.

The formula uses three shortcuts: the bell-curve approximation, Pythagoras for combining genes, and a pooled noise level. The simulator uses none of them. It just draws random counts and counts how often the classifier is right. That is why their agreement means something.

2  The recipe for a fake cell

The generative model — three rules
  1. A cell type is a list of fractions. Type 1 has fraction $p^{(1)}_g$ for each gene $g$; type 2 has $p^{(2)}_g$. Every cell of a type uses the same list.
  2. Counts are Poisson. To make a cell of type $c$ at depth $D$: for each gene, draw $x_g$ from Poisson($D \times p^{(c)}_g$), independently.
  3. Dropout thins. Optionally, lose each molecule with probability $q$.

That is all. In Python, step 2 is one line: rng.poisson(D * p).

Every rule is known to be false for real cells (Week 11 breaks rule 1). That is fine: a model stated precisely enough to simulate is precise enough to test, and when real data disagrees you will know which rule to blame.

3  Choosing the two rates: a two-equation puzzle

For each marker gene the simulator needs a rate for type 1 ($a$) and type 2 ($b$). You want two things:

  • the fold change is $f$: $\;a / b = f$;
  • the average of the two is a chosen "base" level: $\;(a + b)/2 = \text{base}$.
Worked example 1 · solving it (Algebra I, nothing more)
  1. From the first equation, $a = f\,b$.
  2. Substitute into the second: $(f b + b)/2 = \text{base}$, so $b(f+1) = 2\,\text{base}$.
  3. So $b = \dfrac{2\,\text{base}}{1+f}$ and $a = f\,b = \dfrac{2\,\text{base}\,f}{1+f}$.

With numbers: base $= 0.004$, $f = 1.5$. Then $b = 0.008/2.5 = 0.0032$ and $a = 1.5 \times 0.0032 = 0.0048$. Check: $0.0048 / 0.0032 = 1.5$ ✓ and $(0.0048 + 0.0032)/2 = 0.004$ ✓.

Sanity check: at $f = 1$ both become "base" — the two types are identical, and $d'$ must be 0.

4  What the fake data looks like

Worked example 2 · depth 3000, the rates above
  1. Expected counts: $\lambda_1 = 3000 \times 0.0048 = 14.4$, $\lambda_2 = 3000 \times 0.0032 = 9.6$.
  2. Wobbles: $\sqrt{14.4} = 3.8$ and $\sqrt{9.6} = 3.1$.
  3. Per-gene $d'$: gap 4.8, typical count 12, $d' = 4.8/\sqrt{12} = 1.39$.

The data is a table of small whole numbers: 8, 12, 15, 11… Drop the depth to 250 and the expected counts become 1.2 and 0.8; then $e^{-1.2} = 30\%$ of type-1 entries and $e^{-0.8} = 45\%$ of type-2 entries are zero, just from Poisson luck.

Worked example 3 · two genes look hopeless, twenty are nearly perfect

Plot gene 1 against gene 2 for many cells and you see two overlapping clouds. It looks bad. But:

  • 2 genes: $d' = \sqrt{2} \times 1.39 = 1.96$ → accuracy $\Phi(0.98) \approx 84\%$.
  • 20 genes: $d' = \sqrt{20} \times 1.39 = 6.20$ → accuracy $\Phi(3.10) \approx 99.9\%$.

Your eyes can only see two dimensions at a time. Pythagoras sees all twenty.

The bold cells are all equal. Why? $4000 \times 1 = 1000 \times 4 = 250 \times 16 = 4000$. Since $d' \propto \sqrt{D \times k}$, any combination with the same $D \times k$ gives the same accuracy. Seeing this pattern in your simulated numbers is a strong check.

6  The silent bug: labels in the wrong order

No error message, completely wrong answer

Your data is a table of cells (rows) plus a separate list of labels (type 1 or type 2). If you shuffle the rows and the labels separately, every cell gets someone else's label. The shapes still match, nothing crashes, and accuracy drops to about 50%.

Fix: create one random ordering and apply it to both: idx = rng.permutation(n); X, y = X[idx], y[idx].

Do not tune until it agrees If the simulation and the formula disagree, do not adjust parameters until they match. Find out why. Either there is a bug (great — fix it) or the formula's shortcut genuinely fails there (even better — that is a finding).

7  Check yourself

Quick check 1
Base $= 0.006$, fold change $f = 2$. Find $a$ and $b$.
Show answer
$b = 2(0.006)/3 = 0.004$, $a = 2 \times 0.004 = 0.008$. Check: ratio 2 ✓, average 0.006 ✓.
Quick check 2
Using the table, what accuracy do you predict at $D = 1000$ with $k = 16$ genes? Which other cell has the same $D \times k$?
Show answer
94.5%. $1000 \times 16 = 16{,}000 = 4000 \times 4 = 16000 \times 1$, and those cells are also 94.5%.
Quick check 3
Your simulated accuracy at every depth is about 50%. What is the first thing to check?
Show answer
Whether the labels still belong to the right rows (the label-order bug). Print the first few rows with their labels and compare against how you built them.
