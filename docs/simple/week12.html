Week 12 Plain-language companion

The Beads and Three Lines of Evidence, Explained Slowly

Check the data first · how wide the error bars really are · turning accuracy back into $d'$ · what a slope on log-log axes means · why only slopes can be compared
This is the simplified companion to Week 12: the same ideas, slower, with more worked examples and fewer symbols. The labs, the full exercises, the Python assignment and the check-in list are in the original chapter. Open the full Week 12 chapter →
The whole week in one sentence Convert every measured accuracy back into a $d'$ (accuracy itself flattens out near 100%, but $d'$ does not), plot $\log d'$ against $\log(\text{amount of data})$, and the slope of that line is the number every line of evidence — theory, simulation, real data, beads — must agree on: $\tfrac12$.

1  Check the data before you plot anything

Hand-written tally sheets typed into a spreadsheet at night will have typos. The analysis script runs four checks first: the "correct" column must match "true jar = guess"; jar labels must be A or B; no trial may appear twice; and the jar order must have been randomized. If a check fires, go back to the photographed paper sheet. Never edit the data file just to make the message go away.

2  How wide are the error bars?

The full chapter uses the Wilson interval (explained in Week 13's companion). For now, one worked point:

Worked example 1 · 28 right out of 40
  1. Measured accuracy: $28/40 = 0.700$.
  2. Wilson 95% interval: $[0.546,\ 0.819]$.
  3. The theory predicts $0.703$ at that point — comfortably inside. But so is almost everything from 55% to 82%.

With 40 trials per point, each point is fuzzy. That is why the conclusion comes from the whole line of points, not from any single one.

3  Why not just fit a curve to the accuracies?

A power law $y = c\,n^{\alpha}$ keeps growing forever. Accuracy cannot pass 100%; it bends over and flattens. So if you fit a power law to accuracy, the slope you get depends on where along the bend your points happen to sit — it does not estimate anything real.

$d'$, on the other hand, really is a power law: $d' = c\sqrt{n}$, with no ceiling (for beads, which are genuinely binomial). So convert first:

$$d' = 2\,\Phi^{-1}(\text{accuracy}).$$

$\Phi^{-1}$ ("inverse phi") just runs the normal table backwards: "which $z$ gives this percentage?" — invNorm on a TI-84.

With 40 trials at $n = 10$ (true accuracy about 57%), landing at or below 50% happens roughly one time in five, just by chance. Drop those points, and say how many you dropped and which ones.

4  What the slope on log-log axes means

Take logs of $d' = c\,n^{\alpha}$:

$$\log d' = \log c + \alpha \log n.$$

That is a straight line, $y = b + mx$, with slope $\alpha$. So on log-log axes, a power law is a straight line, and its slope is the exponent.

Worked example 3 · slope from two points

From the 30/35 jars, theory says $d' = 0.534$ at $n = 25$ and $d' = 1.068$ at $n = 100$.

  1. Rise: $\log(1.068) - \log(0.534) = \log(2)$.
  2. Run: $\log(100) - \log(25) = \log(4)$.
  3. Slope: $\log 2 / \log 4 = 0.5$. ✓ (Any base of logarithm gives the same slope.)

In words: 4× the beads gave 2× the $d'$. A slope of 0.5 is the 4× rule.

5  The dropout arm collapses too

In the second arm, each drawn bead is discarded with probability $q$ (a die roll) before you look. The beads you keep are still a fair sample from the jar, just fewer: on average $n(1-q)$. Plot both arms against $n$ and you get two curves; plot them against $n(1-q)$ and they should land on top of each other — the same collapse as Week 9.

6  The figure of the project, and how to read it

All the evidence goes on one log-log plot: theory, simulation, real PBMC data, beads. Each line has a slope and a height.

Only the slopes can be compared A "unit of data" means one bead for the bead line and one molecule for the sequencing lines. Change the unit (count beads in dozens) and a line slides up or down without anything real changing. So the heights mean nothing across lines. The slope does not depend on units: "10× the data multiplies $d'$ by $10^{\alpha}$" means the same thing for beads and molecules.

7  Why the beads are worth the tedium

The theory and the simulation were written by the same person. If you misunderstood something, you probably misunderstood it in both places, and they will agree while both being wrong. The beads are physical. Nobody coded them. If the square-root law appears in a jar on a kitchen table, it is not an artifact of your software.

And if your fitted slope is 0.34 or 0.7 instead of 0.5? Report it with its interval. Do not round it to 0.5. If the interval includes 0.5, the beads are consistent with the theory; if it does not, that is a finding to explain.

8  Check yourself

Quick check 1
You measure 84.1% accuracy. What is $d'$?
Show answer
$\Phi^{-1}(0.841) = 1$, so $d' = 2$.
Quick check 2
On log-log axes, going from $n = 10$ to $n = 1000$ raises $d'$ by a factor of 10. What is the slope?
Show answer
$\log 10 / \log 100 = 1/2 = 0.5$.
Quick check 3
With 30% dropout and $n = 100$ beads drawn, what is the effective $n$?
Show answer
$100 \times 0.7 = 70$. That point should sit where a no-dropout run with 70 beads would.
