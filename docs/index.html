How Much Data Does It Take to Tell Two Cell Types Apart?

Counting statistics, detection theory, and the price of a sequencing read — an interactive textbook

A single-cell RNA sequencer does not measure a cell's transcriptome. It draws a sample from it — a few thousand molecules out of hundreds of thousands — and hands you the tally. Deciding what type a cell is, from that tally, is therefore not primarily a machine-learning problem. It is a statistical detection problem, of exactly the kind radar engineers solved in the 1950s, and it has an answer with no free parameters in it.

This project derives that answer, tests it in simulation, tests it again on real 10x PBMC data, and then tests it a third time with two jars of coloured beads on a kitchen table. The prediction is a single number: discriminability grows as $\sqrt{\text{depth}\times k}$, so doubling the separation between two cell types costs four times the data.

The central question How does the accuracy of cell-type classification scale with (a) sequencing depth per cell and (b) the number of informative marker genes? And where — precisely — does that scaling law stop being true?

Each week below is a chapter: objectives, exposition, interactive labs, exercises with full solutions, your Python assignment, and exactly what will be verified at check-in. gold = milestone week (a verification event) · green = buffer week (no new mandatory material) · WIN = a week that ends with something you can show someone.

The three aims

AimWhat it isWeeks
Aim 1 · Theory Poisson counting statistics → the separation index $d'$ → a closed-form accuracy prediction $\Phi(d'/2)$ with $d' \propto \sqrt{\text{depth}\cdot k}$ 1–5
Aim 2 · Simulation & data A synthetic-cell simulator, classifiers written from scratch, then the same measurement on downsampled 10x PBMC 3k data 7–11
Aim 3 · Physical analog Two jars of beads at 30/70 and 35/65; draw $n$, guess the jar, measure accuracy against $n$; add a dropout rule 6–12

Aim 3 is an honest analog of the sampling process, not a substitute for sequencing, and is presented as such throughout. Its value is that it is the only line of evidence in which nothing is simulated and no software can be wrong.

What success looks like

Read this before Week 1, and again in Week 8

Success is not high classification accuracy. Getting 99% on PBMC 3k takes an afternoon and teaches nothing — the cell types were chosen because they are easy to tell apart.

Success is a quantitative comparison between a derived prediction, a simulation, and a physical experiment, with an honest account of where they diverge. A clean negative result — dropout breaks the $\sqrt{n}$ law below some depth; overdispersion caps what depth can buy — is a strong outcome, and the report has a section reserved for exactly that.

How claims are labelled

Every claim in this project carries a status, in the chapters, in the progress logs, and in the report:

proved you can derive it at the whiteboard, on demand, without notes · cited someone else established it; the reference is given · observed measured here, with a seed and an error bar, but not derived · conjectured believed, stated plainly as a belief

An unlabelled claim is not admissible. This sounds like bureaucracy for one week and then becomes the most useful habit in the project, because it makes the difference between "the data agrees with the theory" and "I want the data to agree with the theory" impossible to blur.
